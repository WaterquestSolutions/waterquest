Converting Oil Hydraulics to Water: A Planned Upgrade, Not a Refill

Audit every wetted part, keep the structure that still works, upgrade what water needs, and commission against the original duty for an oil-free result.

Plan the conversion around useful reuse

A four-step retrofit checklist

StepRecord before proceeding
InspectIdentify every wetted body, seal, spring, plug, hose and instrument.
ClassifyDocument what is water-rated, what must be replaced and what needs supplier evidence.
RedesignCheck inlet conditions, clearances, pressure limits, controls and materials.
CommissionClean and flush under the approved procedure, then test the original duty and safe states.

The objective should be specific. The project may aim to remove oil from a circuit near a sensitive process, reduce a fluid-handling burden or replace an aging independent power unit.

Establish the actual duty before dismantling anything

Record loads, movement times, stroke, cycle frequency, holding periods and operating states. Measure relevant pressures under representative conditions where practical and authorized.

Keep observations separate from assumptions. A slow stroke may result from a changed process load, a restrictive return path or a mechanical problem rather than a pump limitation.

Inventory every component the new fluid would reach

Trace the complete wetted path, including reservoirs, pumps, valves, cylinders, hoses, fittings, filters, instruments, accumulators and drain connections. Small internal or supporting components can govern compatibility. A stainless exterior does not identify seals, springs or moving surfaces inside the assembly.

Classify proposed reuse as supported for the intended water duty, unsuitable or unresolved. Support should be for the exact configuration and cover fluid, pressure, temperature and operating conditions. The Water Hydraulics Company's purpose-designed pump information demonstrates why water service is an engineered application rather than a generic fluid substitution. A component's ability to contain pressure with oil does not establish acceptable lubrication, corrosion or leakage behaviour with water.

Separate hydraulic reuse from mechanical reuse

Brackets, foundations, guards or electrical supplies may remain useful even when the entire fluid path changes. Assess these interfaces independently. The retained structure and mechanism must support the new arrangement through the complete stroke and relevant fault states, not merely at the nominal operating point.

Mechanical reuse can provide real value when it is verified. It can reduce installation work and preserve a well-designed load path. The audit should therefore identify what is worth keeping and why, rather than treating conversion as either total replacement or maximum reuse.

Recalculate the complete circuit for the selected water equipment

The new pump, valves and actuator must meet the required force and movement together. Check both cylinder directions, opposing pressures, flow losses and the effect of unequal chamber volumes.

Sealing and construction options also need exact identification. Parker's cylinder information illustrates that fluid service can influence the supported assembly. That is a reason to obtain manufacturer confirmation for proposed reuse, not authority to substitute one seal material and assume the rest of an oil cylinder is converted.

Plan contamination removal and waste handling

Residual oil and cleaning fluids can remain in lines, cavities and components after a reservoir is drained. A newly filled circuit does not become clean water simply because the visible oil has been removed. The method must address accessible and trapped volumes without introducing an incompatible chemical or damaging a surface.

Define who performs the work, how used fluids and consumables are collected and what evidence confirms readiness for the new fill. Avoid prescribing an improvised universal flushing recipe; the correct procedure depends on the components, contamination and application. Cleaning is a controlled transition activity, not a substitute for compatibility assessment. An unsuitable pump does not become suitable for water because it has been thoroughly flushed.

Keep phased work within clear fluid boundaries

A phased conversion can be practical when independent circuits or machines can be changed separately. It requires a clear separation between oil and water systems. Do not connect a new water HPU to unreviewed oil-service branches or assume that a shared manifold can be used temporarily without analysis.

Plan the outage and recovery before disconnecting the existing service. Establish temporary operating arrangements, restoration criteria and responsibility for returning the machine to production. A commissioning problem may reveal an interface that was not fully understood; the owner needs a practical response that does not depend on unsafe improvisation. This planning does not weaken confidence in the design.

Rework controls around verified operating states

The new circuit may need different pump sequencing, fluid-condition indications or valve actuation. Review commands, permissions, feedback and protective responses as a complete sequence. A controller output does not prove a valve changed state or a mechanism completed its movement.

Stored hydraulic and mechanical energy remain relevant after conversion. CCOHS guidance emphasizes that isolating a primary source does not necessarily remove residual energy. Update the site's authorized procedures for the actual installed arrangement. ISO 4413's public scope provides hydraulic-system context, while the full applicable requirements and project risk assessment establish the necessary design measures. The change should leave controls and maintenance information more accurate, rather than preserve an old procedure whose assumptions no longer match the equipment.

Commission against the original process requirement

Before functional operation, confirm identification, connections, supported fluid condition and final settings through the approved commissioning plan. Verify pump supply conditions and the relevant protective functions. Site testing must address the actual mechanism, utilities and control interfaces that the converted system will serve.

Record baseline values for the new installation, including relevant pressures, movement times, temperature and fluid condition. Check that feedback corresponds to the observed mechanism and that the agreed interruption response is achieved. Commissioning establishes readiness for service; it does not automatically establish every lifecycle claim made in the original proposal.

Hand over a system the owner can maintain

Provide configuration-specific schematics, settings, approved fluids, service instructions and spare-part identification. Train operators and technicians for the differences that affect their roles.

A successful conversion leaves a documented system that performs the required duty and delivers a defined benefit, such as removal of oil from a consequential release pathway. WaterQuest can help establish that scope before the owner commits to installation.

Audit the complete wetted path

List pumps, valves, cylinders, hoses, seals, fittings, sensors and retained pipework. Conversion requires an engineered equipment review; replacing oil with water alone is not a retrofit.
