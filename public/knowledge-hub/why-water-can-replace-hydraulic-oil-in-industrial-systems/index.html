Why Water Can Replace Hydraulic Oil in Industrial Systems

Retain the hydraulic function. Reconsider the working fluid.

Water can transmit the pressure needed for industrial movement while removing hydraulic oil from the selected circuit. Learn what makes the approach practical and how materials, controls and water quality support the required duty.

Article 004Start Here10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Replace oil’s functions with a purpose-built water design

A water hydraulic system still needs to transmit pressure, manage moving contacts and carry heat away from losses. Purpose-built components provide those functions with materials, surfaces and controls selected for water. That makes oil removal a practical equipment choice, allowing the working circuit to deliver the required movement with a different fluid and service routine.

Water can transmit hydraulic pressure, so the basic force-generation principle is not the obstacle. The demanding work is at the component interfaces and in the operating arrangements. Suitable water-service pumps, valves and actuators manage those requirements through their construction and specified limits. This is different from pouring water into equipment that was approved only for an oil duty.

For an industrial owner, the decision begins with the process problem. An actuator near a product stream, a treatment channel or a sensitive receiving environment may carry an unwanted oil inventory. If suitable water equipment can provide the required function, that inventory can be removed from the selected circuit. The benefit is specific and understandable, while the remaining engineering duties stay visible.

Retain the force relationship while redesigning the interfaces

A cylinder produces force because pressure acts over an effective area. Flow into the working chamber is related to the area and movement speed. These relationships remain applicable when the medium is water. They provide a stable starting point for the mechanical duty, even though losses and component behaviour can differ between the old and new equipment. [1]

Suppose an industrial mechanism needs a particular force at a particular attachment point. The designer must account for the linkage geometry, travel and varying process resistance before selecting an actuator. Water does not remove that mechanical analysis. It changes the available system options for delivering the result. A larger bore, a different pressure or another mounting arrangement may be considered, but each choice has consequences for flow, structure and installation space.

This distinction helps keep a conversion discussion productive. The owner need not abandon hydraulic motion merely because the existing medium creates a problem. Equally, the supplier should not promise an exact drop-in replacement before checking the duty and interfaces. The transferable idea is the required function; the components that deliver it may need to change.

Move lubrication requirements into a supported contact design

The absence of conventional hydraulic oil does not mean moving contacts are ignored. A water-service design must provide suitable behaviour at bearings, sliding elements and seals under the intended load and motion. Material pairing, geometry, surface condition and the fluid supply all contribute. A successful arrangement can use different design strategies at different locations within the same component.

Engineered polymers are one possible part of that strategy. Victrex's tribology information explains why friction and wear need to be considered at the interface rather than inferred from a material name. A polymer's role must be assessed with its counterface and operating conditions. The evidence for one grade or one contact cannot establish that every polymer-labelled water component will behave the same way. [3]

For a buyer, the practical response is to select supported assemblies and retain their approved service parts. Ask the supplier to identify the intended fluid and duty, the relevant operating limits and the maintenance provisions. These are more useful than a broad promise that the equipment needs no lubrication-related attention. Removing oil changes how the contact requirements are met; it does not abolish friction or wear.

Treat material compatibility as a complete wetted-path question

A compatible main housing does not establish compatibility of the complete circuit. Water can contact springs, plugs, instrument connections, valve internals and other parts that may not be prominent in a sales description. Follow the wetted path and determine how each assembly is approved for the proposed medium. The review should include connections between purchased packages, where assumptions can otherwise fall between suppliers.

Stainless materials can be valuable, but they are not immune to every water environment. The actual grade, local geometry, fabrication condition and exposure affect performance. Corrosion guidance from material manufacturers is useful for recognising the relevant mechanisms; it should not be simplified into a claim that one grade eliminates all corrosion concerns. [4]

Separate internal fluid exposure from the exterior environment. A freshwater actuator installed near the coast may have an external salt-exposure problem even when its internal fluid is controlled. A washdown process can introduce a different external chemical exposure. Resolving these boundaries early avoids designing a satisfactory internal circuit while overlooking the surfaces that operators inspect and service most often.

Design heat management around losses and operating time

Any real power-transmission system has losses. Some of the input energy becomes heat in the drive, pump, valves and fluid. The resulting temperature depends on where those losses occur, how long the duty continues and how heat leaves the installation. Water's thermal properties are relevant, but they do not make a cooling assessment unnecessary.

Consider two machines with the same peak movement requirement. One operates briefly and then rests; the other repeats the movement throughout a shift. Their average heat loads can differ substantially. A reservoir size or cooling arrangement suitable for one duty should not be copied to the other solely because the actuator and peak pressure look similar. Include standby and auxiliary operation in the heat balance where relevant.

A useful proposal states the operating assumptions behind its temperature limits and thermal arrangement. Measurements during acceptance should represent the demanding supported duty, rather than only a few unloaded strokes. System-level pumping guidance reinforces the value of examining the complete requirement and losses. The aim is to deliver the process function with an appropriate power unit, not to attribute every efficiency improvement to the medium. [5]

Decide how working water will be supplied and managed

A recirculating water-hydraulic circuit reuses its working fluid, but it still needs a defined make-up and maintenance arrangement. Incoming water, particles introduced during service and changes during operation can affect the circulating condition. A once-through design presents a different water balance and discharge question. The architecture determines which requirements the owner must plan for.

Start with the selected equipment's fluid specification. The Water Hydraulics Company's P3 documentation demonstrates the importance of specifying the intended water and operating configuration. It should not be used as a blanket approval for any process water or for an arbitrary mixture introduced during maintenance. Obtain the current instructions for the purchased assembly and resolve uncertain supply conditions before commissioning. [2]

Then make the routine tasks practical. Provide access for approved filters and inspections, identify sample locations where required and explain what happens during extended shutdown. In climates where retained water can freeze, consider the whole retained volume and the supported preservation method. A clear operating plan makes the new medium manageable for the site's existing team instead of transferring hidden duties to them after installation.

Choose a conversion boundary that the site can support

An industrial facility does not need to change every hydraulic circuit at once. A self-contained actuator or machine function can provide a useful boundary for evaluating water service. Select a duty where the oil exposure matters and where the mechanical and control interfaces can be defined clearly. A bounded project can reveal practical installation and maintenance issues before a wider programme is considered.

List what remains and what changes. The support structure, electrical supply or operator interface may be retained if suitable, while the hydraulic assemblies need replacement. Reuse should follow evidence about each function rather than a target percentage of old equipment. An apparently inexpensive conversion can become costly when an unsupported retained item prevents reliable commissioning.

Plan the outage and recovery arrangement with operations. Identify what the process will do while the equipment is isolated and how any unresolved commissioning issue will be handled. This is not a prediction that the new system will fail; it is ordinary integration planning for equipment on which the site depends. It makes the proposed improvement easier to approve because the transition has been considered alongside the finished design.

Measure the benefits on the same service boundary

Define the result before the pilot begins. The required motion, holding behaviour, availability and operating limits should be clear. Record a representative baseline where the existing system is being compared. A new machine that performs a different task, omits an auxiliary function or runs on a different schedule cannot provide a straightforward efficiency comparison without explaining those differences.

Separate routine savings from uncertain scenarios. Oil purchasing and waste handling may be visible in site records. The consequences of a rare release may be strategically important but should not automatically be counted as a certain annual saving. Use transparent assumptions and show which conclusions depend on them. This allows procurement and management to understand both the predictable operating effect and the value of removing a particular exposure.

Include new duties in the comparison: water management, supported service parts, training and any changed inspection needs. A strong case can survive a complete account of costs. Omitting inconvenient items only makes the decision harder to defend when the owner encounters them later. The objective is a better-supported installation, not a narrowly favourable spreadsheet line.

Make the argument specific enough to test

Water can replace hydraulic oil in suitable industrial systems because the required force and motion can be delivered through purpose-designed water equipment. The decision becomes compelling where that function is combined with a meaningful reduction in oil-related exposure. The case becomes weaker when it relies on universal efficiency, maintenance or environmental claims that the proposed duty has not demonstrated.

A useful enquiry to WaterQuest describes the process, the load and movement, the existing interfaces and the reason the working medium deserves reconsideration. From there, the discussion can identify compatible equipment, outstanding data and a practical acceptance plan. That gives engineers something concrete to evaluate and gives business leaders a decision tied to their own operation. The question is not whether every hydraulic machine should change; it is whether this particular machine can meet its duty while removing a problem the owner currently has to manage.
