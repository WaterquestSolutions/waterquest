What Are Water Hydraulic Systems?

Discover hydraulic force and motion with water as the working fluid.

Need hydraulic force without hydraulic oil? Water hydraulic systems use pressurised water to drive cylinders and motors. Learn how the circuit works, where it can help and what makes equipment suitable for water service.

Article 001Start Here9 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Picture a gate powered by water

A treatment-channel gate must move against its load, reach the commanded position and hold until the next instruction. A pump supplies pressurised water, control valves direct it and a cylinder creates the movement. Purpose-built water hydraulics performs that familiar job while removing hydraulic oil from the gate’s working circuit.

Water carries the power; a motor, engine or other drive supplies the energy that turns the pump. The benefit is especially relevant beside a waterway, product stream or process where an oil release would create extra work or contamination. Materials, protection and maintenance are selected for water service so the circuit can deliver the required movement.

Begin with three practical questions: what must the machine do, how will the circuit deliver the movement, and where does removing oil help? Together, the answers connect the environmental benefit to equipment the owner can specify, operate and maintain.

Follow the energy through the circuit

The drive turns a pump, which supplies flow to a circuit. Pressure develops in response to the resistance encountered by that flow. A valve directs fluid to the appropriate actuator connection, and the actuator converts hydraulic power into mechanical movement. In a cylinder, a pressure difference acts over effective piston areas. In a hydraulic motor, the supplied fluid produces rotation through the motor's internal mechanism. These are different motion devices, so their selection methods are related but not interchangeable.

For an ideal cylinder under a simple pressure difference, force follows pressure multiplied by effective area. Flow determines how quickly fluid fills the changing chamber volume. These relationships explain why pressure and flow must be specified together. A pump able to reach a high pressure may still deliver too little flow for the required travel time. Conversely, abundant flow does not establish that the actuator can overcome the maximum load. [1]

A larger piston can create more force at the same pressure, but it also needs more water for the same travel. Pumps, drives, valves and pipework introduce losses. Comparing electrical energy per completed movement, with the load and travel time recorded, shows how effectively the complete system performs the task.

Work through a simple cylinder example

Consider an illustrative cylinder whose effective pressurised area is 0.004 square metres. Applying a pressure difference of 80 bar (1,160 psi) produces an ideal force of 32,000 newtons, because 80 bar (1,160 psi) equals eight million pascals. If the piston travels at 0.04 metres (0.131 ft) per second, the ideal chamber flow is 0.00016 cubic metres per second, or 9.6 litres per minute (2.54 US gpm). The useful mechanical power in this simplified example is force multiplied by speed: 1.28 kilowatts (1.72 hp).

The calculation is a teaching model, not a selected assembly. A real double-acting cylinder has pressures acting on both sides, and the rod reduces the effective area on one side. Friction, valve losses and acceleration must also be considered. The supporting structure, pins and mountings need their own mechanical checks. A pressure rating cannot establish whether a slender rod is safe against buckling or whether an attachment can withstand a side load.

The example turns a movement requirement into pressure and flow that can be checked against equipment data. It also gives the project team a shared basis for comparison: the selected circuit must deliver both values together while completing the required movement.

Understand what changes when oil is removed

Conventional hydraulic oil performs several jobs: it transmits pressure, participates in lubrication, carries heat and interacts with surfaces and seals. Water can transmit pressure effectively, but it does not reproduce every other property of the oil it replaces. Purpose-designed water equipment addresses the difference through its material pairs, clearances, sealing arrangements and operating conditions. Filling an unsupported oil component with water is therefore a different proposition from buying equipment engineered for water service.

Low viscosity is one of the important differences. It can reduce some resistance to flow while allowing more leakage through a given clearance. Whether that helps or harms the overall machine depends on the component design and duty. The same reasoning applies to lubrication: a compatible material family is useful, but load, speed, surface finish and alignment still influence a sliding interface. A material name alone cannot establish its service life.

The Water Hydraulics Company's P3 pump is an example of commercially documented water-service equipment. Its relevance is that the pump has an intended medium and operating envelope. It does not prove that every pump, valve or actuator attached to it is compatible. The complete circuit needs a consistent water-service specification, including the small internal components that may be invisible in a general arrangement drawing. [2]

Choose an architecture that suits the application

In a recirculating arrangement, fluid returns for reuse after passing through the actuator or other working element. The design must manage that returning fluid, maintain an adequate supply and control contamination and temperature. A once-through arrangement has a different water balance and a defined destination for discharged water. Neither description establishes acceptability by itself; the process, utility connections and receiving environment determine what can be used.

Separate motion control from process pumping. An actuator circuit uses hydraulic power to move machinery. A reverse-osmosis feed pump supplies a pressure-driven separation process. Both may involve high-pressure water and purpose-designed components, but their performance objectives differ. Cylinder movement, load holding and stopping behaviour do not replace membrane-feed requirements, and a desalination pump curve does not establish actuator control performance.

This distinction becomes especially useful when a site contains several water-related services. There may be opportunities to share an approved utility or maintenance capability, but a common fluid name is not sufficient reason to connect systems. Differences in cleanliness, chemistry, pressure and interruption tolerance can justify keeping them separate. Define the interfaces before promising simplification.

Make fluid management part of normal operation

A suitable water source is an operating input that needs to remain suitable. Suspended particles can damage or obstruct equipment; dissolved substances may affect deposits and material compatibility; temperature changes influence both fluid properties and component limits. The required controls depend on the supported equipment and exposure. There is no universal treatment recipe that applies to every water hydraulic system.

Begin with the manufacturer's fluid requirements and identify how the installation will meet them. Provide appropriate sample points, access to filters and a practical method for replacing or replenishing fluid. Record where make-up water enters and what happens during long periods of inactivity. In a cold climate, consider every volume that may retain water, including branches and valves, rather than treating reservoir drainage as proof that the circuit is protected against freezing.

Make routine service straightforward for the operating team. Provide access to valves and filters, identify consumables and explain the checks that reveal changing condition. Removing oil-related tasks can simplify part of the maintenance programme, while water quality, seals and other component requirements remain part of normal care.

Evaluate failure behaviour alongside normal movement

A system that performs well during a demonstration may behave differently after a hose failure, a lost command signal or an interruption to power. Establish what the machine should do in those states. A gate may need to stay in position, close, open or move under a controlled emergency procedure. The correct response depends on the process and cannot be inferred from the use of water.

Stored pressure, a suspended load or mechanical spring energy can remain hazardous after the drive stops. Isolation and maintenance procedures must address those sources. Pressure limitation is another distinct function: it protects against specified overpressure conditions but does not automatically provide safe load holding, safe access or protection against every transient. Public information on ISO 4413 identifies hydraulic-system safety as a system-level subject; conformity requires the applicable requirements and evidence for the actual design. [3]

This is why a procurement specification should request an operating sequence and failure-state description, not merely a list of components. Review the interfaces with electrical controls and the process equipment. Agree on acceptance demonstrations for normal movement, stopping, holding and recovery. The aim is to make the intended behaviour visible before equipment arrives on site.

Build a business case around the problem being removed

Start the business case where oil removal solves a specific problem. Record the hydraulic oil being removed, the route a leak could take and the work involved in managing that exposure. Compare installation, energy, water management, inspection, spares and downtime for the same required service. Keep possible incident costs separate from recurring expenses.

Energy assessment should include the actual duty cycle. A machine that moves briefly and waits for hours can be dominated by standby consumption or auxiliary equipment rather than motion energy. System-level pump guidance is useful because it encourages attention to the complete operating requirement instead of a single best efficiency point. Measurements from one component or one laboratory condition should not be presented as a guaranteed whole-installation saving. [4]
