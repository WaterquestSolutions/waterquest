Water vs Oil, Property by Property: Where Water Has the Edge

Compare water with mineral hydraulic oil using stated temperatures and typical properties. Water offers high stiffness and heat capacity; purpose-built components control leakage and wear.

The comparison must also identify the fluids and conditions. Water properties change with temperature and pressure.

Use property data to support component selection and calculation, then evaluate the complete installation at its required duty. It does not establish an efficiency, corrosion or service-life result for an assembled water-hydraulic machine. Those outcomes require additional design and operating information.

Separate dynamic viscosity from kinematic viscosity

Dynamic viscosity describes resistance to shear in a fluid. Kinematic viscosity is dynamic viscosity divided by density.

An illustrative fluid has a kinematic viscosity of 20 square millimetres per second and a density of 900 kilograms per cubic metre (56.2 lb/ft³) at the same stated condition. Converting the viscosity to 0.000020 square metres per second and multiplying by density gives a dynamic viscosity of 0.018 pascal-seconds, or 18 millipascal-seconds. The example demonstrates the unit relationship; it is not a specification for a particular oil.

When reviewing a comparison chart, check the temperature, pressure, units and identity of the fluid behind each number. A viscosity-grade label is not a complete operating curve, and a value at one reference temperature does not describe a cold start or a warm continuous duty.

Understand why viscosity affects both losses and leakage

Fluid moving through a passage encounters resistance associated with the flow conditions and geometry. Viscosity influences that behaviour.

This is why low viscosity cannot establish overall pump efficiency by itself. Internal leakage can reduce delivered flow, while mechanical friction and other losses influence input power. The component designer must manage the balance through clearances, materials and operating conditions. A water-service pump is assessed as a complete design, rather than as an oil pump whose fluid-friction term has simply been reduced.

For diagnosis, distinguish external losses in lines and valves from internal losses in the pump or actuator.

Recognize that density affects pressure head and mass

Density connects volume to mass and appears in several hydraulic relationships. Using an illustrative density of 1,000 kilograms per cubic metre (62.4 lb/ft³), a two-metre column produces about 19,620 pascals (2.85 psi), or 0.196 bar (2.85 psi), of hydrostatic pressure difference.

This calculation helps explain why a modest reservoir elevation may provide less pressure head than a casual description suggests. It does not establish the actual pump inlet condition during flow, because line losses and other effects must also be included.

Density also matters when converting a volume of working fluid into a mass for a heat calculation or assessing structural loading from stored fluid.

Distinguish heat capacity from heat rejection

Specific heat capacity describes the energy associated with raising the temperature of a unit mass. Neither one, by itself, tells an owner whether a power unit will remain within its operating temperature limit during a long shift.

For a teaching calculation, suppose 100 kilograms (220 lb) of fluid has an assumed constant heat capacity of 4.18 kilojoules per kilogram per degree Celsius. Raising its temperature by ten degrees would require 4.18 megajoules if other effects were neglected.

The useful design question is therefore whether heat rejection balances the relevant losses over the specified duty. Do not translate a favourable fluid property directly into a claim that the complete system needs no cooling assessment.

Account for compressibility and the compliance of the circuit

Liquids compress to a limited extent under pressure. It can provide an introductory estimate, but a complete response calculation must also consider the construction and contents of the circuit.

For example, an assumed bulk modulus of two gigapascals and a pressure increase of 100 bar (1,450 psi) give a fractional volume change of about 0.005. Applied to ten litres, that corresponds to approximately 50 millilitres. This is an illustrative model using an assumed constant, not a quoted property value for a purchased fluid or a prediction for an installed machine.

Hoses and other boundaries can expand, structures can deflect and trapped gas can change the response.

Consider vapour pressure at the pump inlet

Vapour pressure relates to the condition at which a liquid can form vapour at a given temperature. In a pump inlet assessment, the relevant local absolute pressure and losses must be considered against the selected equipment's requirements. A favourable outlet pressure reading does not establish satisfactory inlet conditions.

Temperature changes are relevant because the fluid properties and vapour condition change. A design that appears satisfactory with a full cold reservoir may need review at minimum level and the demanding supported operating condition.

Water-service product information, such as the P3 documentation, illustrates the importance of selecting and connecting the supported pump configuration. Do not infer that a pump can lift water, accept any boosted supply or tolerate a restricted inlet from its maximum outlet rating. Resolve those conditions for the exact assembly and use the current operating instructions.

Keep chemistry separate from physical-property comparisons

A viscosity or density table does not describe dissolved salts, particles or biological condition. Distilled, municipal, recycled and seawater supplies should not be treated as interchangeable merely because their main constituent is the same.

Likewise, an oil's viscosity value does not describe its complete formulation or suitability for a particular component.

For the owner, identify how the required fluid condition will be maintained after commissioning. Supply changes, service interventions and extended shutdowns can alter the exposure.

Compare the system output after applying the properties

Property data become valuable when they inform a real design and a measurable comparison. System-level pumping guidance supports this approach because it directs attention toward the complete duty and its losses.

Water hydraulics deserve consideration where the required function can be delivered while removing an unwanted hydraulic-oil inventory. They do not establish automatic superiority on every measure.

Where it fits

Property values describe stated fluids and temperatures. Final selection also needs water chemistry, component ratings and measured circuit losses.

Account for temperature and trapped volumes

Viscosity, expansion and vapour pressure change with temperature. Check cold starts, hot operation, inlet margin and thermal expansion of isolated fluid. Water’s low viscosity does not prevent high pressure, but it changes leakage, lubrication and clearance requirements.
