Do I Need Filters or Additives for Tap Water?

Match your water supply to the equipment’s requirements.

Clear tap water can still carry particles and dissolved minerals. Learn which water-quality checks and filters your equipment needs, and when a proposed additive requires specific approval from the component supplier.

Article 110Components & Materials10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Give the equipment clean, suitable working water

Using water can remove the need for hydraulic oil and its associated handling. The supply still needs to suit the pump, valves and seals. Check particles, dissolved constituents and the manufacturer’s water limits, then select filtration for the circuit. Any proposed additive needs its own compatibility review so the working water continues to support the equipment.

Start with the equipment documentation and the actual water supply. The Water Hydraulics Company's product information includes water and filtration requirements, demonstrating that tap-water operation does not mean uncontrolled fluid condition. [1] Use current instructions for the ordered model rather than transferring a value from another configuration.

This distinction makes water hydraulics easier to own. Operators can understand what enters the system and what checks preserve suitability. It also prevents the two most common assumptions: that drinking-quality water is automatically ideal for every precision component, or that an oil-style additive package must be added before water can perform hydraulic work.

Separate particles from dissolved constituents

A particle filter is intended to remove particles within its stated performance. It does not automatically remove dissolved salts or correct the chemistry of the supply. A water sample can therefore be clear and well filtered while still requiring a separate compatibility assessment for the materials in the circuit.

This matters when choosing treatment. If the concern is construction debris, particle control is relevant. If the concern is a dissolved constituent, a different assessment is needed. Installing a finer filter without identifying the problem may increase resistance while leaving the actual compatibility question unresolved.

Describe the water-management objective before selecting equipment. The owner should know which condition each treatment stage addresses and how its performance is checked. This turns a collection of filters and treatment devices into an understandable system, with responsibilities that can be maintained after the original installer leaves.

Read a filter specification beyond the micron label

Two elements carrying the same micron description may not have identical capture performance. Filter ratings need a defined test basis and efficiency description. Parker's filtration handbook explains rating concepts and the importance of matching filtration to the equipment and operating conditions. [2]

For procurement, request the complete accepted element specification and its compatibility with water service. Include the housing, seals and relevant pressure and temperature conditions. An element that fits physically is not necessarily an equivalent replacement. The material and performance requirements both matter.

Avoid selecting a universal micron number for the whole water-hydraulics industry. The necessary cleanliness and filtration arrangement depend on the selected components. The useful question is which specification the manufacturer requires and how the installation will achieve it. That answer provides a defensible replacement part and prevents maintenance from having to interpret an incomplete label at the next service interval.

Choose filter locations with the pump supply in mind

Filtration affects the pressure available along a flow path. A restrictive or loaded element placed in an unsuitable location can compromise the conditions required at a pump inlet. The filtration design must therefore protect components while preserving the supply conditions on which they depend.

The appropriate arrangement may include filling, return or pressure-side filtration, depending on the architecture and component requirements. Each location serves a different purpose and encounters different conditions. The system designer should establish the arrangement rather than adding a filter wherever there is convenient space.

Consider the condition as an element loads, not only when it is new. Monitoring and replacement criteria should allow the owner to act before the accepted operating basis is lost. A filter is a maintained component of the hydraulic design; it is not a one-time accessory whose presence alone proves that the fluid will remain suitable.

Prevent contamination during filling and service

The original water supply is not the only possible source of contamination. Assembly debris, unsuitable containers, exposed connections and maintenance work can introduce material into an otherwise acceptable circuit. Clean handling therefore complements filtration rather than being replaced by it.

Follow the equipment supplier's preparation and service requirements. Identify the accepted filling route and keep the required materials available to the maintenance team. A clear procedure is particularly useful when a plant has several fluid systems and staff might otherwise reuse a container or tool without recognising the consequence.

Record interventions that open the circuit. If a new problem appears immediately after service, that history can help the team investigate the cause. It also supports a proportionate response: the issue may be contamination introduced during a particular activity, rather than evidence that the ongoing water supply or entire hydraulic design is unsuitable.

Decide whether an additive has a defined purpose

An additive should address a specified requirement and have explicit support for the complete wetted assembly. Possible purposes might include a manufacturer-approved low-temperature arrangement or another identified operating need. The fact that a chemical is marketed for water treatment does not establish compatibility with a pump, valve, hose and seal combination.

Ask what problem the proposed additive solves, what evidence supports its use and how its condition will be maintained. Identify whether it changes the fluid's properties, equipment performance or disposal requirements. These questions are more useful than assuming that adding a chemical automatically improves reliability.

Do not create a mixture by borrowing a recipe from an unrelated system. The concentration, product formulation and operating range can matter, and a change may require manufacturer review. Where the selected equipment is supported on an accepted water supply without an additive, adding one unnecessarily can create uncertainty rather than remove it.

Treat freezing protection as a complete arrangement

Outdoor or unheated equipment may require low-temperature planning. That plan can involve equipment location, environmental control, a supported preservation method or an approved working-fluid arrangement. The appropriate choice depends on the actual installation and the conditions it must survive.

An additive-based approach must be assessed for the selected configuration rather than treated as a generic winter remedy. The fluid change may affect viscosity, clearances, seals and performance. Manufacturer documentation and project-specific confirmation should establish the acceptable arrangement and the associated operating limits.

Likewise, an insulation proposal does not create heat by itself. The design must consider the duration and severity of cold exposure and what happens when normal power is unavailable. Keeping the question at system level prevents an owner from relying on a single attractive measure that does not protect all relevant components during the governing condition.

Keep hygiene claims within their proper boundary

Water suitable for drinking at the supply point does not automatically remain suitable for food contact after circulating through industrial equipment. The circuit's materials, cleanliness, storage and operation can affect its condition. Removing hydraulic oil does not by itself establish a sanitary or food-contact approval.

CFIA's guidance on water used in food preparation illustrates the importance of water suitability within a food business's control arrangements. [3] The application team must determine how any hydraulic circuit relates to the process and what evidence is required for the actual exposure. A fluid name cannot answer that question alone.

This is particularly important where a possible leak could reach product or a contact surface. Water hydraulics may remove a meaningful oil-contamination pathway, but the resulting water exposure still needs assessment. The benefit can be stated accurately without implying that every discharge from the machinery is potable, sterile or acceptable for direct food contact.

Build a monitoring plan that operators can use

Water management should have observable checks and assigned actions. Depending on the equipment and supply, these may include filter condition, temperature, water-level behaviour or periodic analysis of relevant parameters. The purpose is to detect departure from the accepted basis before it becomes a larger operating problem.

Do not collect measurements merely because sensors are available. Each check should answer a question and identify the response to an abnormal result. A filter indication without a replacement criterion or an available spare can create awareness without improving reliability.

Establish a baseline after commissioning and retain the operating context. A change in supply source, maintenance activity or duty can explain a changed reading. Records that connect measurements to events are more useful than isolated values, particularly when the owner needs supplier assistance to determine whether the condition is acceptable or requires intervention.

Make the water specification part of purchasing and handover

The enquiry should identify the proposed water source, expected temperature, duty and any treatment or additive being considered. Ask the supplier to state what information is needed to confirm suitability. Resolve those questions before procurement where they can influence the configuration, rather than leaving them for the installer at first fill.

The handover record should identify the accepted fluid, filter elements, relevant checks and prohibited unreviewed substitutions. It should also explain who is responsible for maintaining the supply condition. This is especially important when facilities staff manage the water treatment while a separate maintenance team owns the hydraulic equipment.

WaterQuest can make tap-water operation practical by connecting those responsibilities to a supported equipment scope. The advantage is an understandable working medium in an appropriate design, with the hydraulic-oil inventory removed from the selected circuit. Dependable operation comes from applying the water specification consistently, not from pretending that filters, compatibility or maintenance cease to matter.

If the supply later changes, treat the change as an engineering input. A new treatment plant, different seasonal source or revised cleaning programme may alter the original assumptions. Rechecking the affected requirements is usually more effective than waiting for symptoms and then trying to reconstruct when the fluid conditions changed.

For procurement managers, a small acceptance matrix can keep these decisions visible. Record each required water parameter or filter characteristic, the proposed evidence and the party responsible for confirmation. This reduces ambiguity between a supplier's equipment limits and a site's ability to maintain them, while giving operators a concise reference for future enquiries.

From understanding to equipment selection

Specify water quality and filtration separately

Particle filtration does not remove dissolved salts or necessarily control microorganisms. Give the supplier a water analysis and the operating temperature, then select filter media, rating and location for the equipment's actual requirement.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the system pressure-drop calculator to combine component losses, elevation and a design allowance. Use the lifecycle cost calculator to compare capital, recurring service, replacement and residual costs over the same study period.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Request filtration and water-quality requirements for the supplied pump and power unit, including the rating method, filter location and service criteria.

Explore water hydraulic pumps and water hydraulic power units.

What should filter and water-condition records tell the operator?

Trend filter differential pressure and inspect retained debris. Rising restriction or unusual deposits should trigger investigation of the source and circuit, not an automatic change to a coarser filter.

A pressure-loss budget can include filter loading, but it does not select filter media or predict water chemistry and fouling.
