Preparing Your Water Hydraulic System for Winter

Prepare the circuit for operation, standby and winter shutdown.

Winter readiness depends on what happens during shutdown as well as normal operation. Learn how to inspect exposed water circuits and plan heating, insulation, drainage and restart procedures for your site.

Article 119Water Hydraulic Systems10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Prepare every exposed part for winter

A winter-ready water circuit needs protection during operation, waiting, shutdown and restart. Map the lines, instruments and trapped volumes that can cool, then review heating, insulation, drainage and monitoring against the site conditions. A clear seasonal procedure helps operators preserve the equipment and know what to check before returning it to service.

An HPU in a heated room may still serve an exposed actuator or line. A normally warm enclosure may become cold during a prolonged outage. Identify those different conditions before selecting a winterisation method. Protecting the most visible equipment while overlooking a remote water-filled section can leave the installation vulnerable.

This article explains planning principles rather than a drain-down or chemical-mixing procedure. The actual method must be supported for the selected equipment and site. WaterQuest and the responsible designer should establish a configuration-specific approach that preserves the required function and provides a practical way to return the system to service.

Distinguish operating limits from freeze protection

The freezing behaviour of the fluid is only one part of low-temperature suitability. Seals, hoses, instruments and drive equipment also have accepted operating conditions. A fluid arrangement that avoids freezing does not automatically make every component suitable for operation at the same temperature.

Manufacturer information should therefore be read as a complete operating envelope. The Water Hydraulics Company's pump documentation identifies normal temperature conditions and directs users to discuss alternative low-temperature arrangements. [1] That is a reason to obtain current confirmation for the intended configuration, not to invent a universal winter limit from one product page.

Also distinguish storage from operation. A component may tolerate a particular preserved storage condition while requiring a different condition before starting. The winter plan should name both states and the transition between them. This prevents a successful period of cold storage from being mistaken for permission to energise equipment before its operating requirements are restored.

Map every water-filled region

The circuit drawing should identify reservoirs, pumps, actuators, valves, instruments and connecting lines that can contain water. Include remote branches and spaces that may remain filled when the principal tank is emptied. Geometry and valve states can create retained volumes that are easy to overlook.

A statement that the system has been drained should have an equipment-specific meaning. It should not be inferred from the disappearance of flow at one accessible point. The responsible method must address the actual internal arrangement and the manufacturer's preservation requirements.

This mapping is particularly useful for seasonal machinery. A rarely operated branch may receive little attention during normal service yet experience the longest cold idle period. The winter review should follow the fluid throughout the installation rather than relying on the location of the power unit alone.

Understand why insulation is only part of a heat strategy

Insulation reduces heat transfer; it does not create a continuing heat source. It can delay cooling, but a water-filled component exposed long enough to a colder environment may still approach that environment's temperature. The required protection depends on duration, heat input and the complete installation.

Where environmental heating or another heat source is part of the design, consider its reliability and coverage. A heated cabinet may not protect external connections. A local temperature sensor may not represent the coldest vulnerable point. The responsible designer should identify the governing location and conditions.

Plan for loss of normal power or heating if that is relevant to the site. An alarm is useful only when someone can respond within the available time and a supported response exists. The design should connect the detected condition to a realistic action rather than assuming that notification alone prevents freezing or equipment damage.

Evaluate a manufacturer-supported fluid arrangement

An approved low-temperature working-fluid arrangement may be appropriate for some equipment, but compatibility must be established for the complete assembly. Product formulation and concentration can affect viscosity, seals, materials and performance. A mixture accepted by one component supplier is not automatically accepted by every other item in the circuit.

Obtain the relevant manufacturer information and define the actual fluid specification. The plan should identify how the condition is established and maintained, including the effect of make-up additions. Repeatedly adding plain water to a mixture can change its properties, while adding more chemical without an approved basis can create another departure.

Do not use an automotive or unrelated process-fluid recipe as a substitute for equipment approval. The goal is a supported operating medium, not simply a liquid with an attractive freezing-point claim. Any implications for handling, discharge and the installation's original oil-removal claims should also be reflected in the ownership documents.

Consider preservation when the machine will not operate

For seasonal shutdown, preservation may be more appropriate than maintaining an operating arrangement throughout the cold period. The selected method must protect the actual equipment and allow a controlled return to service. Different components can have different requirements, so the complete assembly needs coordination.

An approved drain or storage method must consider retained volumes, internal surfaces and contamination during the idle period. It should also address the condition of disconnected or exposed interfaces. Leaving components open without the required protection can replace a freezing concern with a contamination or corrosion problem.

Record the preserved state clearly. Future staff should know that the equipment is unavailable for operation and what restoration work is required. A machine that looks assembled can still be in a non-operating preservation configuration. Labels and asset records help prevent an unintended start before the required fluid and component conditions are re-established.

Account for fluid properties during cold operation

Even above a freezing concern, temperature can change fluid properties and component response. IAPWS provides a reference basis for liquid-water properties within its stated range. [2] The appropriate engineering assessment uses conditions relevant to the actual fluid and equipment, including any approved mixture rather than assuming it behaves exactly like pure water.

Changes in flow resistance, internal leakage and seal behaviour can affect the machine's response. The significance depends on the design and duty. A system that performs satisfactorily in a warm workshop may need additional assessment for a cold start at the installation.

Acceptance should therefore cover the required operating range or rely on appropriate supporting evidence. Do not infer full low-temperature performance from a brief test at a convenient ambient condition. The owner needs to know when the equipment can perform the required function and what preparation is needed before it is asked to do so.

Make monitoring relevant to the coldest risk

Temperature monitoring should answer a defined question about the installation. A reservoir reading can be useful, but it may not represent an exposed line or actuator. Choose locations and thresholds through the equipment and site assessment rather than using one generic number for every water system.

Consider the time between detection and required action. A remote alarm has limited value if the site is unattended and no response can arrive before the condition becomes unacceptable. The winter strategy should be credible under the actual staffing and access arrangements, including severe weather that may make travel difficult.

Keep the response understandable. Operators should know whether a warning calls for investigation, an authorised transition to a preserved state or another approved action. Those decisions must come from the installation-specific plan. This prevents an alarm from becoming a prompt for improvised draining, heating or additive use under time pressure.

Plan restart as a separate verification event

Returning from winter storage requires confirmation that the accepted operating configuration has been restored. The relevant checks depend on the preservation method, equipment and period of inactivity. The process should address fluid identity, supporting conditions and the required machine function.

Inspect and assess any evidence of exposure outside the accepted condition before operation. An absence of visible leakage does not prove that every internal component remains sound. The responsible team should determine what information or checks are needed when freezing or another damaging condition is suspected.

Hazardous-energy control remains relevant during restoration and investigation. CCOHS explains the general need to account for stored and supplied energy. [4] The authorised site procedures must govern the work. Familiar water and a seasonal shutdown do not remove the possibility of trapped pressure or movement from an attached load.

Make winterisation an owned part of the asset

Assign responsibility for preparing, monitoring and restoring the system. The task may involve facilities heating, hydraulic maintenance and operations, so the interfaces should be explicit. A plan that belongs to everyone in principle can end up belonging to no one when the first cold period arrives.

Retain the accepted method, fluid information and evidence of completed work. If the installation changes, review whether the winter plan still covers it. A new external branch or a revised operating schedule can introduce an exposure that the original arrangement did not address.

Water hydraulics can be practical in Canadian service when the temperature conditions are treated as design inputs. The credible proposition is a supported low-temperature or seasonal arrangement that the owner can maintain. It is not a promise that water-filled machinery can ignore winter, nor an assumption that one bottle of additive resolves every component and operational question.

Consider a hypothetical indoor power unit serving an outdoor gate through a sheltered but unheated route. Heating the power-unit room protects only part of that system. The route, gate actuator and any isolated instrument connections still need assessment. This example illustrates why the circuit map and exposure map should be reviewed together before choosing a measure.

The economic comparison should include the chosen winter strategy. Heating, inspection, preservation labour or approved fluid management can affect ownership cost. Including them openly helps the owner compare complete alternatives and avoids discovering a recurring seasonal task only after the equipment has been installed.

General hydraulic guidance, including ISO 4413, supports considering the conditions under which the system is intended to operate. [3] The specific winterisation answer must still come from the selected configuration and site. A short, usable plan with clear responsibilities is more valuable than a generic assurance that the equipment is suitable for cold climates.

From understanding to equipment selection

Turn winterisation into a repeatable procedure

Identify the water that remains in cylinders, valves and low points after draining. Define which components stay heated, which can be emptied and which require supplier-approved preservation, including during loss of normal power.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the energy savings calculator to compare annual electricity and demand costs using measured or explicitly assumed input power. Use the lifecycle cost calculator to compare capital, recurring service, replacement and residual costs over the same study period.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Discuss enclosure, water management and preservation with the power-unit and cylinder suppliers for the stated cold-weather duty. Include all exposed and trapped volumes in the review.
