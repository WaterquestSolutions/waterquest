Desalination for Drought: Four Numbers That Don't Mean the Same Thing

Recovery, salt rejection, energy recovery and kWh per cubic metre each answer a different question. Water-hydraulic actuation fits naturally in a water plant.

Industry Applications6 min read

Download the worksheet for this guide (editable text) · Open the ro requirement register (CSV)

Written by WaterQuest Solutions · Updated 2 November 2026 · About the distributor

Build around the water supply your site needs

Start by distinguishing drinking-water demand from membrane production. Treatment losses, cleaning, storage management and plant availability influence how much capacity is needed.

Reverse osmosis uses pressure to make a separation

In reverse osmosis, a membrane separates a feed stream into permeate and a more concentrated stream. The US Department of Energy describes membrane desalination as one of the principal desalination approaches.

The high-pressure pump therefore serves a process requirement; it does not determine water quality on its own. A pump capable of delivering a headline pressure may still be unsuitable if its supported flow range, inlet conditions or wetted construction do not match those operating states.

Follow three streams rather than one output number

Feed, permeate and concentrate are linked by a mass balance. Ignoring small ancillary streams for a simple teaching example, 10 cubic metres per hour (44 US gpm) of feed at 40 percent recovery yields 4 cubic metres per hour (17.6 US gpm) of permeate and 6 cubic metres per hour (26.4 US gpm) of concentrate. It does not mean membrane salt rejection, pump efficiency or the percentage of energy recovered.

These distinctions matter because the three percentages answer different questions. Increasing recovery changes the concentrate stream and can change scaling or operating constraints; it is not a free improvement available by turning a control knob. The acceptable recovery must come from the process assessment.

Saline service requires a specific construction review

Seawater presents a different exposure from ordinary fresh water. Dissolved salts, suspended solids, biological material and the site's temperature range influence equipment selection.

The Water Hydraulics Company publishes a dedicated seawater pump range, illustrating why configuration-specific information matters. A purchase enquiry should identify the actual feed composition and treatment stage reaching each component. Raw seawater at the intake, pretreated membrane feed, concentrate and permeate are different services.

Pretreatment protects the process and its machinery

Pretreatment addresses the contaminants and conditions that would interfere with the downstream equipment. A filter selected for particle control does not automatically resolve dissolved scaling constituents, and a clear-looking feed is not necessarily suitable for high-pressure machinery or membranes.

The hydraulic supplier and process designer should agree the water condition expected at their interface.

An inlet problem can undermine an otherwise suitable pump

A high-pressure pump must receive the inlet conditions required by its manufacturer. Tank level, feed-pump behaviour, pipe losses, restrictions and changing water temperature all affect the available inlet condition.

This is a practical reason to assess the whole supply path. Instruments should be located where they can help confirm the condition experienced by the pump, rather than only at a convenient upstream point.

Pressure energy in concentrate can have useful value

Concentrate leaving a reverse-osmosis process may retain substantial pressure. An energy-recovery arrangement can return part of that energy to the process, reducing the external input required for the same production duty.

The Water Hydraulics Company's Power Centre describes a pump and brine-energy-recovery arrangement. Its relevance to a project depends on the actual flow balance, pressure conditions and operating schedule.

Measure energy per useful quantity of water

Specific energy consumption expresses energy use relative to a defined volume. The boundary must state whether intake pumping, pretreatment, high-pressure pumping, post-treatment, distribution and standby consumption are included.

Suppose a hypothetical plant uses 240 kilowatt-hours while delivering 80 cubic metres of acceptable product during a defined period. Its measured specific consumption at that boundary is 3 kilowatt-hours per cubic metre. This is arithmetic, not a predicted WaterQuest performance figure.

Drought readiness includes interruptions and recovery

A drought-response asset must function when water is needed, which makes resilience a central design question.

The operating team also needs a restart strategy. After extended shutdown, the condition of the feed path, membranes and stored water may require checks or treatment before normal supply resumes.

Concentrate management belongs in the project from the start

Fresh-water production creates a concentrate stream that requires an acceptable destination. Its characteristics depend on the feed, recovery and treatment chemicals used.

A responsible concept also accounts for cleaning solutions, replaced filters and other waste streams separately. The fact that a pump uses water rather than hydraulic oil does not provide permission to discharge process water or establish that the receiving environment is unaffected.

Procure a performance envelope and a service plan

An effective request for proposal describes feed-water ranges, required product quality, production schedule and site constraints. Clarify who is responsible for integration between the pump package and the treatment process so that essential functions do not fall between separate contracts.

Acceptance should demonstrate the agreed service under representative conditions, with an explicit method for interpreting conditions that differ from the design basis. A single short run at favourable feed temperature is not a complete demonstration of seasonal reliability.

Build the case around dependable water, not a component slogan

Water-hydraulic technology can contribute to desalination through machinery designed for the actual fluid and through suitable pressure-energy arrangements. System-level energy assessment, as discussed in the Department of Energy pumping guidance, supports this wider view.

For engineers, the opportunity is a coherent design across process and machinery boundaries. For procurement, it is a comparison with fewer hidden exclusions.
