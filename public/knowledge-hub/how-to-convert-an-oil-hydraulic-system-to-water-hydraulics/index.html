How to Convert an Oil Hydraulic System to Water Hydraulics

Plan the equipment and interfaces for a water hydraulic conversion.

A successful conversion keeps the machine’s required function at the centre of the work. Learn how to assess existing hardware, replace unsuitable components and plan controls, commissioning and downtime for water service.

Article 037Testing & Adoption9 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Plan the conversion around useful reuse

A water hydraulic conversion can retain suitable mechanical and control interfaces while replacing components that depend on oil. Document the existing machine, review every wetted part and define the required movement before selecting replacements. This turns the change into a planned engineering project, with a clear equipment scope, commissioning sequence and outage requirement.

The objective should be specific. The project may aim to remove oil from a circuit near a sensitive process, reduce a fluid-handling burden or replace an ageing independent power unit. State what will improve and how the result will be verified. A fluid change should not be expected to correct unrelated mechanical binding, inadequate mounting or poorly defined controls. Understanding those existing issues prevents the conversion from reproducing an old weakness inside a new hydraulic package.

Establish the actual duty before dismantling anything

Record loads, movement times, stroke, cycle frequency, holding periods and operating states. Measure relevant pressures under representative conditions where practical and authorised. Review maintenance history, oil additions and recurring interruptions. Confirm the mechanism and installed connections against available drawings. An old schematic or nominal pressure setting may not describe what the machine currently does. The baseline should establish both the process requirement and the performance of the existing arrangement.

Keep observations separate from assumptions. A slow stroke may result from a changed process load, a restrictive return path or a mechanical problem rather than a pump limitation. Record the conditions under which symptoms occur so the replacement design can address the correct cause. The baseline also supports later evaluation of benefits. Without it, the project may claim improvement based on memory or compare the new equipment under an easier duty than the old system experienced. Useful evidence begins before the conversion work starts.

Inventory every component the new fluid would reach

Trace the complete wetted path, including reservoirs, pumps, valves, cylinders, hoses, fittings, filters, instruments, accumulators and drain connections. Small internal or supporting components can govern compatibility. A stainless exterior does not identify seals, springs or moving surfaces inside the assembly. Record model, options and available documentation. Where identification is uncertain, make that uncertainty visible rather than assuming the component belongs to a familiar standard configuration.

Classify proposed reuse as supported for the intended water duty, unsuitable or unresolved. Support should be configuration-specific and cover fluid, pressure, temperature and operating conditions. The Water Hydraulics Company's purpose-designed pump information demonstrates why water service is an engineered application rather than a generic fluid substitution. [1] A component's ability to contain pressure with oil does not establish acceptable lubrication, corrosion or leakage behaviour with water. Reuse should follow evidence, not the desire to minimise the number of new parts in the quotation. Photograph component markings and connection locations during the audit. Retain the images with the inventory so that model identification can be checked after access is restricted. This can prevent a later spare order from relying on an incomplete transcription or an obsolete drawing.

Separate hydraulic reuse from mechanical reuse

Brackets, foundations, guards or electrical supplies may remain useful even when the entire fluid path changes. Assess these interfaces independently. A replacement cylinder may fit the existing mounting but create different forces, speeds or return flows. A new power unit may use a different control sequence or utility demand. The retained structure and mechanism must support the new arrangement through the complete stroke and relevant fault states, not merely at the nominal operating point.

Mechanical reuse can provide real value when it is verified. It can reduce installation work and preserve a well-designed load path. The audit should therefore identify what is worth keeping and why, rather than treating conversion as either total replacement or maximum reuse. Record any repairs needed to existing guides, pivots or supports. This gives procurement a more accurate scope and helps the owner distinguish the cost of correcting an inherited defect from the cost of adopting water hydraulics.

Recalculate the complete circuit for the selected water equipment

The new pump, valves and actuator must meet the required force and movement together. Check both cylinder directions, opposing pressures, flow losses and the effect of unequal chamber volumes. Evaluate inlet conditions at the pump and the permitted return or case-drain arrangement. A familiar pipe size or old relief setting should not be carried forward without review. The selected water configuration may have different requirements even when the useful mechanical task remains unchanged.

Sealing and construction options also need exact identification. Parker's cylinder information illustrates that fluid service can influence the supported assembly. [2] That is a reason to obtain manufacturer confirmation for proposed reuse, not authority to substitute one seal material and assume the rest of an oil cylinder is converted. Materials, counterfaces, guidance and clearances work together. The design should show a coherent supported fluid path rather than a collection of parts individually described as water-resistant without evidence of their combined duty.

Plan contamination removal and waste handling

Residual oil and cleaning fluids can remain in lines, cavities and components after a reservoir is drained. A newly filled circuit does not become clean water simply because the visible oil has been removed. If any hydraulic items are retained, establish a supported cleaning and verification method appropriate to their construction and the new fluid requirements. The method must address accessible and trapped volumes without introducing an incompatible chemical or damaging a surface.

Define who performs the work, how used fluids and consumables are collected and what evidence confirms readiness for the new fill. Avoid prescribing an improvised universal flushing recipe; the correct procedure depends on the components, contamination and application. Cleaning is a controlled transition activity, not a substitute for compatibility assessment. An unsuitable pump does not become suitable for water because it has been thoroughly flushed. Keeping those questions separate prevents the project from investing effort in cleaning components that should not remain in the converted circuit.

Keep phased work within clear fluid boundaries

A phased conversion can be practical when independent circuits or machines can be changed separately. It requires a clear separation between oil and water systems. Do not connect a new water HPU to unreviewed oil-service branches or assume that a shared manifold can be used temporarily without analysis. Identify every connection and any temporary equipment. Labels, drawings and work instructions should make the boundary understandable to installation and maintenance personnel.

Plan the outage and recovery before disconnecting the existing service. Establish temporary operating arrangements, restoration criteria and responsibility for returning the machine to production. A commissioning problem may reveal an interface that was not fully understood; the owner needs a practical response that does not depend on unsafe improvisation. This planning does not weaken confidence in the design. It recognises that conversion occurs in an operating facility and that maintaining the required service during the transition is part of the project's success.

Rework controls around verified operating states

The new circuit may need different pump sequencing, fluid-condition indications or valve actuation. Review commands, permissions, feedback and protective responses as a complete sequence. Determine what happens during normal stop, interrupted travel, loss of supply and missing feedback. A controller output does not prove a valve changed state or a mechanism completed its movement. Feedback should represent the process function the owner needs to confirm, with useful information available for fault diagnosis.

Stored hydraulic and mechanical energy remain relevant after conversion. CCOHS guidance emphasises that isolating a primary source does not necessarily remove residual energy. [4] Update the site's authorised procedures for the actual installed arrangement. ISO 4413's public scope provides hydraulic-system context, while the full applicable requirements and project risk assessment establish the necessary design measures. [3] The change should leave controls and maintenance information more accurate, rather than preserve an old procedure whose assumptions no longer match the equipment.

Commission against the original process requirement

Before functional operation, confirm identification, connections, supported fluid condition and final settings through the approved commissioning plan. Verify pump supply conditions and the relevant protective functions. Then demonstrate movement under representative load, including the required directions, stopping and holding behaviour. A successful unloaded stroke is useful evidence of only a limited part of the duty. Site testing must address the actual mechanism, utilities and control interfaces that the converted system will serve.

Record baseline values for the new installation, including relevant pressures, movement times, temperature and fluid condition. Check that feedback corresponds to the observed mechanism and that the agreed interruption response is achieved. Resolve deviations through controlled changes and update the installed documentation. If a predicted benefit depends on energy or maintenance performance, distinguish initial calculations from results requiring longer observation. Commissioning establishes readiness for service; it does not automatically establish every lifecycle claim made in the original proposal.

Hand over a system the owner can maintain

Provide configuration-specific schematics, settings, approved fluids, service instructions and spare-part identification. Train operators and technicians for the differences that affect their roles. Familiar hydraulic principles remain useful, but the water circuit may have different compatibility, preservation or inlet requirements. Make the support route clear and identify what information is needed when reporting a fault. These provisions help the converted asset remain within its supported envelope after the original project team leaves.

A successful conversion leaves a documented system that performs the required duty and delivers a defined benefit, such as removal of oil from a consequential release pathway. WaterQuest can help establish that scope before the owner commits to installation. Engineers gain a traceable reuse and replacement decision, procurement gains a complete offer and operations gains practical ownership information. The persuasive result is not simply that the reservoir now contains water. It is that the redesigned circuit is compatible, verified and supportable while solving the problem that justified the change.

From understanding to equipment selection

Convert the circuit as an engineered assembly

Inventory every wetted component and its materials, clearances, seals and service history. Retaining the frame or prime mover may be practical, but the suitability of an oil-service pump or valve cannot be inferred from the machine's previous performance.
