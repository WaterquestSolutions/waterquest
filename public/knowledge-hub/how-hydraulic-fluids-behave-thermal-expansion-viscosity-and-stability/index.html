How Hydraulic Fluids Behave: Thermal Expansion, Viscosity, and Stability

Turn fluid properties into practical design inputs.

Temperature changes can alter how a hydraulic circuit behaves. Learn what expansion, viscosity and fluid stability mean for pressure, leakage and movement, and how to include them in equipment selection.

Article 083Performance Comparisons10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Plan for the way the fluid changes during operation

Temperature and pressure influence the viscosity, density and volume of a hydraulic fluid. Those changes affect leakage, flow losses, clearances and heat management. Understanding them helps you select a water circuit for cold starts, sustained operation and standby, with the equipment kept within its supported conditions throughout the cycle.

For water, IAPWS provides scientific formulations with stated ranges and assumptions. [1] Hydraulic-oil data depend on the actual product and its formulation. Comparisons should use compatible conditions and units rather than combine a water value at one temperature with an oil value at another. A property table becomes useful when it supports a calculation or design decision; without that connection, it can create a misleading impression of precision.

Viscosity describes resistance to deformation

Dynamic viscosity relates shear stress to the rate of shear in a fluid under the applicable conditions. Kinematic viscosity is dynamic viscosity divided by density. The two are related but use different units, so their numerical values should not be compared directly. An oil viscosity grade describes a classification convention; it is not a complete statement of how the oil behaves at every temperature or pressure.

In machinery, viscosity influences flow through passages and clearances. Lower viscosity can reduce some resistance while also increasing leakage through a given gap. These effects can act in opposite directions within the same system. The outcome depends on geometry, flow regime and operating condition, which is why a lower viscosity does not establish a universal efficiency advantage. Purpose-designed equipment balances the relevant effects for its intended medium.

Lubrication requires more than a viscosity number

A moving interface may rely on a fluid film, surface properties, material pairing and geometry to manage friction and wear. Load, speed, temperature and contamination influence that interface. A viscosity comparison alone cannot predict whether a bearing surface or seal will perform acceptably. The construction that works with one medium may need different materials or clearances for another.

This is an important distinction for water hydraulics. Dedicated water equipment should be evaluated as a designed assembly, while an existing oil-service component requires explicit compatibility evidence before reuse. The Water Hydraulics Company's pump information illustrates configuration-dependent fluid and installation requirements. [2] The correct enquiry identifies the working medium and duty so that the supplier can assess the actual interfaces, rather than treating water as a simple substitute in an unchanged oil circuit.

Temperature changes both the fluid and the assembly

As a circuit warms, fluid properties change and the solid components also expand. The resulting operating clearance depends on the materials, geometry and temperature distribution of the assembly. A component can therefore experience a different leakage or friction condition even when the measured bulk-fluid temperature remains within a general system range. Local conditions may matter more than one convenient reservoir reading.

The design should consider starting from cold, reaching normal temperature and operating after a shutdown. These are different states with different property combinations. A supplier's temperature range is not merely an ambient-weather statement; it belongs to a specified fluid and equipment configuration. If the application approaches a limit, ask which temperature is being limited, where it should be measured and how a departure should be handled.

Thermal expansion becomes a pressure question in a trapped volume

A fluid that warms generally tends to change volume. If it is trapped in a sufficiently rigid enclosure, that tendency can produce a pressure change instead of free expansion. The actual response depends on the fluid's properties and the compliance of the pipework, chamber and any gas present. A section isolated by closed valves may not be protected by a relief device elsewhere in the circuit.

The engineering review should therefore include blocked-in volumes during shutdown, sunlight exposure or nearby process heating. A stopped pump does not guarantee low pressure throughout the installation. The required protective arrangement must be determined for the actual circuit and applicable requirements. This article explains the mechanism; it does not prescribe a relief setting or provide a construction detail for an unknown installation.

Compressibility affects response and stored energy

Bulk modulus describes resistance to volume change under pressure. A higher bulk modulus indicates less volume change for a given pressure change under the specified conditions. In a real circuit, hoses, structures and entrained gas also contribute to compliance. The response of the complete hydraulic system can therefore differ substantially from a calculation using only the liquid's bulk modulus.

For a deliberately simplified example, assume 10 litres (2.64 US gal) of liquid, a constant bulk modulus of 2 gigapascals and a pressure increase of 100 bar (1,450 psi). Using volume change approximately equal to initial volume multiplied by pressure change divided by bulk modulus gives 0.05 litres (0.0132 US gal), or 50 millilitres. This is a teaching calculation with assumed properties. It excludes enclosure deformation and gas and is not a prediction for a particular product or operating circuit.

Air can change behaviour disproportionately

Gas in a hydraulic circuit can affect stiffness, response and noise. It may enter through maintenance, supply conditions or other pathways, and it can behave differently as pressure changes. A system that appears to have an actuator-control problem may therefore require investigation of fluid condition and the supply path. The appropriate diagnostic approach follows the actual circuit rather than assuming that every delayed response originates in the controller.

Commissioning and service procedures should address filling and air management according to the equipment instructions. Randomly opening connections or altering settings is not a substitute for an authorised method. The objective is to establish a known operating condition so that measurements can be interpreted. Without that baseline, a comparison between water and oil may accidentally compare one well-prepared circuit with another containing an unresolved installation problem.

Vapour pressure matters at the inlet

A liquid's vapour pressure varies with temperature. Local pressure reductions can therefore affect whether liquid remains in the intended state, particularly at pump inlets or restrictive passages. The available inlet condition depends on supply pressure, elevation, pipe losses, restrictions and the operating flow. Temperature changes can alter the margin even when the physical pipework is unchanged.

A high-pressure discharge rating does not establish that a pump can draw from any convenient source. The selected equipment's inlet requirements must be met under the relevant operating conditions, including a low tank level or a partially loaded filter where applicable. Manufacturer guidance is the appropriate basis for the offered configuration. [2] This connects a thermodynamic property to a practical installation decision rather than treating cavitation as an unexplained defect of one fluid.

Heat capacity does not remove the need to reject heat

Heat capacity describes how much energy is associated with a temperature change in a defined amount of material. It helps estimate temperature response, but it does not make generated heat disappear. A circuit's eventual temperature depends on the balance between heat generation, storage and transfer to the surroundings or a cooling system. Reservoir size, ambient conditions and operating schedule all influence that balance.

This distinction is important when water's thermal properties are presented as a benefit. They can create design opportunities, but the complete package still needs an acceptable thermal operating condition. An inefficient restriction can continue generating heat regardless of the medium. System-level pumping guidance encourages attention to the causes of losses, not only the equipment used to remove their heat. [4] A good design addresses both where energy is lost and how remaining heat is managed.

Chemical stability and service condition are different ideas

A fluid can remain chemically recognisable while its suitability for machinery changes. Water can collect particles, dissolved substances, maintenance residues or biological contamination. Oil can also change through contamination and service processes, with behaviour depending on its formulation and conditions. Saying a medium is stable should therefore specify which characteristic remains stable and over what exposure.

For a water circuit, define the permitted supply and the monitoring or inspection needed to preserve the component requirements. Filtration, water treatment and cleaning address different issues and should not be substituted casually for one another. An additive introduced to solve one problem can change compatibility or the meaning of an environmental claim. The operating record should identify the approved medium so that later maintenance does not unknowingly alter the design basis.

Use measurements to connect properties with symptoms

When performance changes, record the operating condition before adjusting the system. Useful observations may include temperature, pressure at relevant points, flow, movement time and the condition of the working medium. The combination helps distinguish an increasing restriction from internal leakage or a changing load. One symptom can have several causes, and one property can influence several symptoms.

Measurements should be compared at similar loads and operating states. A slower movement during a colder start is not directly comparable with a warm steady-duty test unless the expected temperature effect is understood. Instrument location and uncertainty also matter. The goal is a repeatable picture of the system, allowing the responsible team to investigate mechanisms rather than attributing every difference to the broad label water or oil.

Turn property knowledge into a better specification

A useful specification states the fluid, operating envelope, load cycle and environmental conditions, then asks for supported component performance within that envelope. It identifies important startup, shutdown and isolated-volume conditions instead of describing only normal steady operation. The public scope of ISO 4413 offers hydraulic-system context, while actual design and compliance decisions require the applicable full requirements and responsible engineering review. [3]

WaterQuest can help explain how water's properties influence a proposed arrangement and what evidence is needed for the selected machinery. The educational value lies in separating mechanisms: viscosity affects resistance and leakage, thermal expansion can affect trapped pressure, compressibility influences response, and heat capacity influences temperature change. Once those relationships are clear, engineers and buyers can assess real equipment without relying on a misleading claim that one fluid property determines the entire system.

From understanding to equipment selection

Include trapped-fluid heating in the design review

A blocked-in section can experience pressure change as temperature rises even without pump operation. Identify isolated volumes, solar exposure and process heat when reviewing the circuit and its pressure-protection arrangement.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the Reynolds number calculator to examine flow regime using water temperature, salinity, velocity and diameter. Use the pipe pressure-loss calculator to evaluate pipe friction and local losses using flow, internal diameter, length and water condition.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Review pump and valve suitability using the actual water condition, temperature and operating range in the enquiry.

Explore water hydraulic pumps and water hydraulic control valves.

Why should temperature accompany performance measurements?

Inspect thermal protection and isolation procedures during planned service. Record the operating temperature with leak or drift observations so the cause can be assessed in context.

Fluid-property and pipe-loss calculations do not establish component compatibility or a permitted temperature range.
