Stainless Steel and Brass Fittings: Selecting for Marine Duty

Select marine fittings by the medium, exposure and connection duty.

Marine fittings must suit the water they carry and the metals they connect. Learn how stainless steel and brass differ in relevant services, and why grade, compatibility and connection details matter more than a material label alone.

Article 066Components & Materials10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Make every connection part of the marine design

A fitting must hold pressure, maintain its seal and withstand both its working fluid and surrounding exposure. In marine equipment, the exact alloy, thread or tube standard and sealing arrangement matter throughout the circuit. Selecting those details together helps protect the reliability of the larger water hydraulic assembly and makes installation and service clearer.

The choice is not a universal contest in which one material always wins. Different alloys within each family behave differently, and the installation can create conditions that a material label does not describe. Internal seawater, controlled freshwater, external salt spray and intermittent immersion are distinct exposures. Start by separating those conditions. This gives the designer a practical basis for selecting the connection and helps procurement avoid substituting visually similar parts that do not share the required construction or supported service.

Identify the actual alloy and product form

A family name can hide important differences. Stainless steels include several metallurgical families and grades. Brass refers to a range of copper-zinc alloys, some with additional constituents selected for particular properties. A supplier should identify the offered alloy and the relevant product specification where needed. Do not assume that colour or a generic catalogue category establishes the composition. For critical service, the required traceability should be defined before purchase rather than reconstructed from unmarked parts after installation.

The completed fitting also has a manufacturing route and geometry. Threads, wall thickness and sealing features determine the mechanical function. A corrosion-resistant alloy does not by itself establish the fitting's pressure rating. Conversely, a pressure-rated fitting may be unsuitable for the environment. Obtain the manufacturer's limits for the exact product and consider the connected parts. The assembly is governed by its actual components and conditions, not by the highest rating printed on one item in the line.

Distinguish salt spray from continuous seawater contact

External salt deposits can concentrate as surfaces wet and dry. Water may remain beneath clamps, insulation or deposits, creating local conditions different from a clean exposed surface. Continuous internal seawater presents another environment, including flow, temperature and stagnant periods. A fitting that performs well in a sheltered freshwater cabinet may not be an appropriate substitution on an exposed deck or in a saline process circuit. Describe where the fitting sits and what reaches each surface.

A coastal water-hydraulic actuator can use a controlled freshwater circuit while its exterior faces marine exposure. That arrangement may simplify some internal selection questions, but the external connections still need assessment. Conversely, protecting the outside of a seawater fitting does not change the fluid inside. Keeping the two boundaries visible prevents a common misunderstanding: that the location alone determines the working fluid or that one material decision automatically addresses both environments. The installation should specify internal and external exposure separately.

Understand local corrosion in stainless connections

Stainless steel relies on suitable surface and environmental conditions for its corrosion resistance. Chlorides, temperature, crevices and deposits can influence local attack. A threaded or gasketed connection contains geometries that differ from an open flat test specimen. Outokumpu's corrosion guidance describes these mechanisms and the importance of service conditions. [1] For fittings, the practical lesson is to examine the assembled joint rather than select a grade solely from a general reputation for marine suitability.

Avoid treating 316 as an immunity claim. It may be appropriate in some duties and inadequate in others. The selection should address the actual water chemistry, temperature, stagnation and geometry, with specialist review where needed. A higher-alloy option may improve resistance to a relevant mechanism, but it should not conceal poor drainage or an unsuitable joint design. Construction details and maintenance access can influence the local environment. The preferred material should be part of a coherent connection arrangement, not a stand-alone answer to every corrosion concern.

Recognise that brasses differ in seawater behaviour

Some brasses are susceptible to dezincification, a corrosion process in which the alloy can lose zinc preferentially and develop a weakened structure. Alloy composition and service conditions affect susceptibility. The Copper Development Association's seawater guidance distinguishes behaviour among copper alloys rather than treating all yellow metals as equivalent. [2] This is a reason to obtain exact material identification and application support, not to assume that every brass fitting is either unsuitable or immune in every marine environment.

Ask the manufacturer whether the specific fitting is supported for the intended internal fluid and external exposure. A designation associated with improved resistance to one corrosion mechanism still does not establish the complete pressure assembly's suitability. Consider temperature, flow, stagnation and connected metals. If the material cannot be identified or its service support cannot be established, classify it as unresolved instead of relying on past appearance. The absence of visible deterioration during a short period is limited evidence for long-term pressure-containing service.

Evaluate dissimilar-metal connections as a system

Joining different metals in an electrically connected assembly exposed to a conducting fluid can create galvanic considerations. The effect depends on the materials, their surface conditions, the environment and relative exposed areas. A simple statement that two metals should never touch is too broad, while ignoring the combination is equally unhelpful. Review the actual electrical and fluid path and seek appropriate materials guidance for the configuration. The surrounding system can matter as much as the fitting itself.

Potential isolation measures must be engineered for their mechanical, pressure and service functions. An improvised insulating piece should not be inserted into a pressure connection merely to address corrosion in theory. It can create a new sealing, strength or maintenance problem. The preferred arrangement should explain how corrosion, pressure containment and practical assembly are addressed together. Document any restrictions on replacement materials so that maintenance does not unintentionally remove the feature or introduce a different couple during an otherwise routine component change.

Match the connection standard and sealing method

Thread appearance is not enough to establish compatibility. Different thread forms and sealing methods can look similar while relying on different contact surfaces and assembly requirements. Confirm the exact connection standard, size and permitted mating components. A connection that can be forced together is not necessarily a correct pressure joint. The manufacturer's assembly instructions, including any approved sealant or lubrication requirements, belong to the installation method for that product.

Keep the sealing material compatible with the water and operating conditions. A suitable metal fitting paired with an unsuitable elastomer or sealant is still an unsuitable assembly. Do not assume that a material used successfully in an oil circuit can be carried into water service without review. Where a retrofit retains existing connections, inspect and identify them rather than relying on a drawing alone. The objective is a complete documented joint whose pressure, fluid and environmental requirements are supported by the actual parts being installed.

Control mechanical loads beyond internal pressure

Piping weight, vibration, thermal movement and installation misalignment can load a fitting. A connection selected only for internal pressure may be exposed to forces it was not intended to carry. Provide suitable supports and flexible arrangements where required by the design. Avoid using a fitting to pull misaligned equipment into position. Mechanical loading can affect sealing and fatigue independently of the corrosion selection, so both need attention in the layout review.

Marine installations may experience motion and vibration patterns that differ from a stationary indoor plant. The exact duty and applicable vessel requirements should inform design and inspection. Transport Canada's published framework helps identify the regulatory context, but applicability depends on vessel category and installation. [3] It is not a certificate for a particular fitting. The project team must establish the relevant requirements and obtain configuration-specific evidence. This keeps the materials discussion connected to the pressure system and the asset in which it operates.

Design inspection and replacement into the installation

A fitting hidden behind equipment or beneath persistent deposits is difficult to inspect and service. Arrange connections so that relevant leakage or surface deterioration can be recognised and the approved maintenance method can be performed. Consider how the system is isolated, drained and accessed. A material choice that appears economical can become costly if replacing one small component requires extensive dismantling. Accessibility is part of the lifecycle comparison, especially in remote or operationally constrained marine locations.

Record the installed alloy, connection type and approved replacement identification. Maintainers should not need to choose from a bin of visually similar fittings during an urgent repair. When deterioration occurs, preserve evidence of location, fluid and exposure so the cause can be assessed. Replacing the part with the same unspecified material may repeat the problem. Equally, changing to a more expensive metal without understanding the mechanism may leave the actual cause, such as trapped deposits or mechanical strain, unresolved.

Choose from the complete service conditions

A sound fitting enquiry identifies working fluid, external exposure, temperature, pressure, connection standard and mechanical context. It also states inspection access and required documentation. Compare proposals on these conditions rather than on material family alone. The preferred answer may vary across one vessel or coastal installation because different parts of the circuit experience different environments. Consistency is valuable when the duties match; it should not override a material difference in exposure.

WaterQuest can help define the supported connection requirements within a water-hydraulic assembly. The benefit of removing mineral oil is strongest when the replacement circuit is reliable and maintainable, including its smallest pressure joints. Engineers need exact materials and connection data, procurement needs traceable parts and operators need practical service access. Stainless steel and brass can only be judged meaningfully at that level. The professional choice is the fitting whose complete construction and installation are supported for the actual duty, rather than the one with the most reassuring generic label.

Assess fitting material, thread or sealing system, pressure rating and the connected metals together. Consider galvanic contact, crevice geometry and the actual water chemistry rather than assuming one alloy always outperforms another.
