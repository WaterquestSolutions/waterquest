Preventing Corrosion in Water Hydraulic Systems

The five corrosion mechanisms in water hydraulic systems, engineering controls for each, and the water quality monitoring programme that maintains system integrity over 20+ year operational life.

Understanding the specific corrosion mechanisms allows targeted, efficient prevention. Engineers who treat all corrosion as 'the same problem' design inadequate protection. This article systematically addresses each mechanism relevant to water hydraulic systems, the engineering controls that prevent each mechanism, and the water quality management programme that maintains system integrity over its operational life.

1. General (Uniform) Corrosion

General corrosion is the gradual, uniform dissolution of a metal surface through electrochemical reaction with water. For carbon steel in oxygenated water, this is the dominant mechanism , the iron-to-rust conversion that proceeds across the entire exposed surface. The rate depends on dissolved oxygen content, pH, temperature, and water chemistry.

Prevention: Material selection. Specifying 316 stainless steel, which resists general corrosion across a wide pH range and dissolved oxygen concentrations typical of treated water supplies. If 316 stainless is correctly specified throughout, general corrosion is not a design concern.

Design Failure Risk

The most common cause of general corrosion in water hydraulic systems is not material degradation , it is incorrect material selection. A single carbon steel component in an otherwise stainless system will corrode, generating iron oxide particles that contaminate the water, damage pump internals, and cause premature seal and valve failure throughout the entire system.

2. Pitting Corrosion

Pitting corrosion is the localized breakdown of the passive layer on stainless steel in the presence of chloride ions, resulting in highly localized, deep corrosion pits. Pitting is more dangerous than general corrosion because it proceeds rapidly once initiated, is difficult to detect visually in early stages, and can penetrate a component wall while the surrounding surface remains apparently sound.

Prevention: Match alloy PREN to the chloride content and temperature of the water. 316 stainless (PREN 24 to 26) is adequate for most fresh water and municipal water service (chloride typically below 200 mg/L). For systems in coastal environments or process waters with elevated chloride levels, specify duplex stainless steel (PREN 34 to 36) for the most exposed components. Monitor chloride levels in systems supplied from variable-quality sources.

3. Crevice Corrosion

Crevice corrosion occurs in confined spaces where stagnant water can accumulate and oxygen concentration becomes depleted , underneath gaskets, within threaded connections, between overlapping surfaces. The depleted oxygen chemistry creates an electrochemical cell with the aerated water outside the crevice, driving accelerated corrosion within the confined zone.

Prevention: Eliminate crevices wherever possible through design. Use full-penetration butt welds rather than fillet welds on overlapping surfaces. Avoid rubber or fibre gaskets in permanent connections , use PTFE or PEEK face seals instead. Ensure all threaded connections are completely sealed so that water cannot penetrate and stagnate in thread roots.

4. Galvanic Corrosion

Galvanic corrosion occurs when two dissimilar metals are electrically connected in the presence of an electrolyte. The more active (anodic) metal corrodes preferentially. The severity depends on the potential difference between the metals and the ratio of cathode area to anode area , a small anode adjacent to a large cathode suffers particularly severe attack.

Prevention: Specify 316 stainless steel throughout all wetted components to eliminate galvanic potential differences. Where dissimilar metals are unavoidable at interfaces, use electrical isolation (PTFE bushings, non-conductive coatings, or dielectric fittings) to break the galvanic circuit.

5. Microbiologically Influenced Corrosion (MIC)

MIC is driven by microorganisms , particularly sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) , that colonize metal surfaces in water systems. SRBs produce hydrogen sulfide, which is highly corrosive to stainless steel and can locally break down the passive layer even on 316 grades. MIC risk is elevated in systems that are periodically idle, operating at 20 to 40°C (optimal for bacterial growth), or supplied with water containing organic nutrients. Wastewater-adjacent applications require particular attention.

Prevention: Maintain continuous fluid circulation or flush systems that are idle for extended periods. Use UV sterilization in the return line for warm-climate or warm-water applications. Include a biocide compatible with the seal materials for systems at MIC risk. Monitor for biological contamination during routine water quality testing.

Water Quality Management: The Ongoing Defence

Material selection provides the first line of defence against corrosion. Water quality management is the second , equally important , line of defence. Properly conditioned water for hydraulic service should maintain:

  • pH 7.5 to 8.5: The passive layer on 316 stainless steel is fully stable, scale formation is minimal, and MIC organisms are suppressed. Correction via sodium hydroxide solution (to raise pH) or citric acid solution (to lower pH) through a chemical dosing system integral to the HPU.
  • Dissolved oxygen below 0.1 mg/L: Dissolved oxygen is the primary driver of corrosion in water systems. Minimized through deaeration (mechanical vacuum deaeration or nitrogen blanketing of the reservoir) or oxygen scavenger addition.
  • Corrosion inhibitor package: Molybdate-based inhibitors are effective for stainless steel systems and have low toxicity , important in wastewater and food-adjacent applications. The inhibitor concentration must be maintained within the supplier's recommended range; too low is ineffective, too high may cause deposit formation.
  • Conductivity below 200 µS/cm: Use demineralized or reverse osmosis water as the base fluid. Maintain calcium hardness below 50 mg/L as CaCO₃ to prevent scale formation on heat exchanger surfaces and in narrow flow passages.

Key Takeaways

  • Corrosion in water hydraulic systems is preventable, not inevitable , it is a consequence of poor design, incorrect material selection, or inadequate water quality management
  • The five relevant mechanisms (general, pitting, crevice, galvanic, and MIC corrosion) each have specific, well-understood engineering controls
  • 316 stainless steel prevents general and pitting corrosion in standard water service , all five mechanisms require either material selection or water quality responses, not both simultaneously for each
  • Water quality management (pH 7.5 to 8.5, dissolved oxygen below 0.1 mg/L, inhibitor dosing, biocide where needed, and regular monitoring) provides ongoing protection
  • A properly designed and maintained water hydraulic system can operate for 20+ years without significant corrosion-related degradation
