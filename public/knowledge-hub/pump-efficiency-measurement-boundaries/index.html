Pump Efficiency: Quote the Right Number, Measured in the Right Place

Volumetric, overall and wire-to-water efficiency answer different questions. A worked example shows how 92 % becomes 69 %, and where water's low line losses help.

Performance Comparisons6 min read

Download the worksheet for this guide (editable text) · Open the pump requirement register (CSV)

Written by WaterQuest Solutions · Updated 2 November 2026 · About the distributor

Compare efficiency where your pump will run

The operating condition matters just as much. Comparing an ideal value from one product with an installed electrical measurement from another is not a fair test.

For water versus oil, begin with the useful duty that must be delivered. Water's properties create design opportunities and challenges, but they do not provide a universal efficiency ranking.

Establish theoretical and delivered flow

For a positive-displacement pump, theoretical flow follows displacement per revolution multiplied by shaft speed. An illustrative pump displacing five cubic centimetres per revolution at 1,500 revolutions per minute has a theoretical flow of 7.5 litres per minute (1.98 US gpm). If its measured outlet delivery is 6.9 litres per minute (1.82 US gpm) at a stated condition, the corresponding volumetric efficiency is 92 percent.

This example does not describe a particular WaterQuest product. The theoretical number is a reference for the calculation, not a promise that every revolution transfers that full volume to the outlet under every pressure and temperature.

Record where flow is measured. A measurement downstream of a bypass or another branch may not represent pump outlet delivery.

Calculate hydraulic output from the pressure difference

Hydraulic output power at a defined pump boundary is pressure difference multiplied by delivered volume flow. With bar and litres per minute, dividing their product by 600 gives kilowatts. A delivered flow of 6.9 litres per minute (1.82 US gpm) across a 120 bar (1,740 psi) pressure difference represents 1.38 kilowatts (1.85 hp) of hydraulic output.

Suppose the measured shaft input for that illustrative condition is 1.8 kilowatts (2.41 hp). Overall pump efficiency would be about 76.7 percent. That result is different from the 92 percent volumetric efficiency in the preceding example because mechanical and other losses also contribute to shaft power. High volumetric delivery alone does not establish high overall pump efficiency.

A simple arithmetic check is to preserve the boundary in every term. Do not use pressure measured at a distant actuator with flow measured before a bypass and call the product pump output without accounting for the intervening circuit.

Add the electrical boundary when comparing power units

The motor and drive require electrical input to produce shaft power. In the illustrative example, if measured electrical input is two kilowatts while hydraulic output is 1.38 kilowatts (1.85 hp), the electrical-to-hydraulic ratio is 69 percent. The pump-only figure remains about 76.7 percent because it uses the smaller shaft-input boundary.

A purchasing comparison should state which boundary matters to the owner. Utility consumption is an electrical measure, while pump development may reasonably focus on shaft performance.

Use suitable instruments and a measurement method that accounts for the actual electrical supply and drive arrangement.

Compare a useful operating map, not one attractive point

A pump may operate across several pressure and flow demands within a single machine cycle.

Plotting or tabulating several conditions can reveal a trade-off that a single maximum number hides.

The Water Hydraulics Company's P3 page provides performance information for an identified water-service product alongside its operating context. Do not extend one model's information across an entire product range or treat a graph as a guarantee for an installation with different fluid, inlet or control conditions.

Include the energy consumed while the actuator is waiting

Peak efficiency can be a poor predictor of annual energy where the machine operates intermittently.

Consider a hypothetical daily schedule with 20 minutes of operation at two kilowatts and 460 minutes at 0.2 kilowatts (0.268 hp). The first state consumes about 0.667 kilowatt-hours; the second consumes about 1.533 kilowatt-hours. Total daily consumption is approximately 2.2 kilowatt-hours for the stated eight-hour period.

These are teaching assumptions, not typical values for water or oil equipment. Their purpose is to show why measured duty states matter. DOE's system-level pumping guidance supports this broader operating assessment.

Understand the role of viscosity without oversimplifying it

Viscosity influences resistance to flow and leakage through internal paths. Water's lower viscosity relative to many hydraulic oils changes the balance that the pump designer must manage. The resulting efficiency belongs to the engineered component and operating condition, not to the liquid considered in isolation.

Use consistent temperature and units when discussing the fluids. Water-property formulations such as the IAPWS reference provide a scientific basis within a defined range, while the chosen oil needs its own applicable data.

Temperature can also change the operating result during a test. A short measurement taken before conditions stabilize may not represent sustained duty.

Design the test to separate improvements from uncertainty

A useful comparison holds the required output and test conditions consistent while measuring the inputs and delivered function.

Repeat observations where needed to distinguish a consistent effect from variation in load, temperature or control sequence.

The acceptance plan should also cover functionality. A more favourable energy result is not sufficient if the machine fails to meet movement, holding or availability requirements.

Put efficiency beside the other reasons for selecting water

Energy is one part of the decision. The owner may also value removal of hydraulic oil from a location where a release affects a process or receiving environment.

A water-hydraulic proposal can be attractive even where the measured energy advantage is modest or not yet established, provided its other benefit for the applications justify the complete investment. Equally, an unsupported claim of major efficiency improvement can weaken an otherwise credible case.

WaterQuest can help translate the operating schedule into a comparison that engineers, procurement and management can all inspect. It gives the owner a basis for acceptance and later review, rather than a percentage that cannot be connected to the machine's actual work.
