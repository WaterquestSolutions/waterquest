How to Test Water and Oil HPUs for Desalination

Set up an HPU comparison that produces useful evidence.

A useful HPU comparison measures the same work under the same conditions. Learn how to plan a water-versus-oil test for a desalination application, including useful output, energy, temperatures and service requirements.

Article 102Testing & Adoption10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Test like-for-like output to make the comparison useful

A desalination feed pump delivers process flow at pressure; an auxiliary HPU moves valves or other mechanisms. Define which duty you are evaluating before comparing water and oil equipment. Set the same useful output and operating sequence for both arrangements, then measure their inputs, losses and service behaviour within a clear test boundary.

An oil hydraulic power unit usually supplies an oil circuit that produces mechanical work. A water power unit supplies a compatible water circuit. An RO feed pump instead handles process water directly. Some desalination packages combine feed pumping with energy recovery. The Water Hydraulics Company's Power Centre is an example of that integrated architecture. Its comparison boundary must include the functions it replaces, rather than treating it as an interchangeable actuator HPU. [2]

Begin the test plan with a diagram showing the equipment, energy inputs and useful output. Mark which components change and which remain. That single page often prevents more confusion than a detailed spreadsheet assembled around an ambiguous label.

Choose the correct useful output

For a desalination process comparison, the useful product is an agreed quantity and quality of permeate under defined feed conditions. Membrane desalination separates water from dissolved constituents using a membrane process; feed characteristics influence the work required. DOE's overview provides the process context, while the actual membrane and plant documents establish the project-specific operating basis. [1]

For an auxiliary hydraulic comparison, useful output may instead be completed valve movements at the required torque, speed and duty. Energy per cubic metre of permeate would obscure that actuator's behaviour if production varied for unrelated reasons. Energy per agreed operating cycle, together with completion time and holding performance, may be a more useful measure.

Neither boundary is inherently superior. They answer different ownership questions. A plant owner assessing total treatment cost may need the whole process boundary. An engineer evaluating a replacement valve actuator needs a narrower functional test first. Connecting those two views later is legitimate; mixing their measurements at the start creates a result that cannot be interpreted confidently.

Establish equivalent process conditions

A fair RO comparison holds the important process conditions sufficiently consistent or explicitly accounts for their differences. Record feed temperature, salinity, feed pressure, permeate quality, production rate, recovery and membrane condition. Describe the pretreatment and any recirculation. A lower energy reading achieved while producing less acceptable water is not an improvement in equivalent service.

A freshly cleaned membrane train and a fouled train do not provide identical resistance. Seasonal feed changes can also alter the required operating point. Where a simultaneous side-by-side test is impractical, document the baseline period and explain the method used to address those differences. Do not present an adjusted estimate as though both configurations were physically tested in the same condition.

Choose acceptance ranges before collecting the data. Otherwise the test team may unintentionally select the most flattering run after seeing the results. A pre-agreed protocol gives procurement a defensible comparison and gives both suppliers a clear opportunity to demonstrate their equipment within the same rules.

Draw the electrical measurement boundary

A pump shaft measurement describes pump performance. A package electrical measurement includes the motor and drive. A plant meter may additionally include pretreatment, controls, ventilation and other services. Each can be useful, but the report must name the boundary beside the result. A number without its boundary is difficult to compare and easy to misuse.

If an integrated recovery arrangement removes a separate booster or drive, account for that change openly. If another arrangement requires a circulation pump or cooling load, include it where the agreed boundary requires it. Do not compare the complete input of one option with only the principal motor of the other.

DOE's pumping guidance encourages consideration of the operating system rather than an isolated component. [3] In a test plan, that principle becomes a practical instrument list and a marked drawing: which feeders are measured, which auxiliary loads are included, and which site loads are excluded. This also makes future retesting possible when the package or plant changes.

Use energy and volume over the same interval

Specific energy consumption for an RO process is electrical energy divided by accepted permeate volume. The usual engineering unit is kilowatt-hours per cubic metre. Kilowatts divided by cubic metres is not the same quantity. A report should distinguish an instantaneous power reading from energy accumulated during a defined production period.

For a hypothetical teaching example, a test consuming 24 kilowatt-hours while producing eight cubic metres of acceptable permeate has a specific energy consumption of three kilowatt-hours per cubic metre. These are illustrative values, not a prediction for a WaterQuest installation. If start-up water is diverted and not accepted as product, identify its treatment in both the energy and volume accounting.

Use synchronized measurement intervals. A power average from one hour and a production total from another can create an apparently precise but invalid ratio. Record interruptions and rejected data with reasons. Keeping the original observations alongside the calculated result allows a reviewer to reproduce the arithmetic rather than relying on a polished summary alone.

Capture the operating range that the owner will use

An attractive best-point result is valuable information, but it does not necessarily represent annual operation. A desalination plant may run at reduced production, respond to storage demand or operate with changing feed conditions. An actuator may spend most of its time waiting. The test should include the operating states that materially influence ownership.

Describe steady operation separately from starts, stops and transitions. A short start-up loss can matter when equipment cycles frequently, while the same loss may be minor during long production runs. Likewise, part-load behaviour can outweigh a small difference at full output where demand rarely reaches the nominal maximum.

Build a duty-weighted estimate only after measuring the relevant states. State the assumed annual schedule and retain the individual measurements so another reader can substitute a different schedule. The result then becomes a useful planning model. It remains an estimate of future operation, rather than an assertion that one laboratory sequence establishes every site's annual consumption.

Plan uncertainty before interpreting small differences

Measurements have uncertainty. Pressure instruments, flowmeters and electrical meters have limitations, and operating conditions also fluctuate. Instrument specifications alone do not describe the complete uncertainty of a test; installation, calibration, sampling and calculation methods matter. A comparison should explain whether the observed difference is large enough to be distinguished by the chosen method.

Suppose two configurations return closely spaced results while feed conditions vary noticeably between runs. The appropriate response may be another controlled test, not a confident percentage saving. Conversely, a consistent difference across repeated representative runs deserves attention even if neither measurement is perfect. Precision in the displayed decimal places should not exceed the confidence supported by the method.

Have the responsible test team select suitable instruments and document their locations. Retain calibration information and note any data gaps. This discipline is particularly important where the commercial decision depends on a small energy advantage that could disappear within the combined measurement and operating uncertainty.

Observe service behaviour without claiming lifetime proof

Efficiency is only one test outcome. Record noise, vibration observations, temperature stability, leakage, operator interventions and access difficulties. Note water-quality management and filter changes during the trial. These observations can reveal installation issues that a favourable energy total would otherwise hide.

A short trial cannot establish multi-year service life. It can identify immediate defects, demonstrate a supported operating point and provide evidence for a longer evaluation. Keep those conclusions distinct. A successful acceptance run should not become an unsupported claim that the equipment will require no maintenance or retain the same performance indefinitely.

Where reliability uncertainty controls the decision, define a follow-on monitoring period with appropriate inspection and reporting. Include the condition of relevant components when service occurs. A structured record of what happened, under which conditions, is more useful than a general statement that the machine ran well. It also helps distinguish a component limitation from an installation or operating problem.

Translate the result into an ownership decision

The final comparison should show complete installed cost, measured performance, expected duty and the support arrangement. Energy savings have a financial value only after a defensible tariff and operating schedule are applied. Installation access, replacement parts and maintenance capability may materially affect the preferred option even when measured electrical performance is similar.

Separate verified findings from estimates and unresolved questions. A useful decision table identifies what was measured, what was supplied by the manufacturer and what still requires confirmation. It should also show whether the tested configuration matches the one being offered. A change in pump, drive, recovery arrangement or controls may require the comparison to be revisited.

WaterQuest can make a strong desalination proposal by explaining exactly which function its equipment performs and what evidence supports that scope. The convincing question is not whether water has won an abstract contest against oil. It is whether the proposed architecture produces the required service with a better supported combination of energy use, operational practicality and exposure control for this plant.

Preserve the test package as an asset document. Include the marked boundary drawing, equipment identifiers, control settings, instrument record and raw data location. Record who witnessed the test and how exceptions were resolved. Those details help the owner investigate a later change in performance without reconstructing the original arrangement from memory.

An executive summary can remain concise while linking to that evidence. It should state the decision, the measured range and the assumptions most likely to change the recommendation. For example, the preferred package may depend on a particular production schedule or on retaining a specified recovery arrangement. Making those dependencies visible turns the test into a procurement tool rather than a promotional demonstration, and gives future operators a clear basis for checking whether the expected benefit has actually been delivered.

From understanding to equipment selection

Define a fair desalination comparison test

For utility actuation, compare the same loaded movement; for process pumping, compare the same feed conditions and delivered process duty. Keep those tests distinct and measure input energy at a consistent boundary.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the hydraulic power calculator to translate pressure and flow into hydraulic output and estimated electrical input. Use the lifecycle cost calculator to compare capital, recurring service, replacement and residual costs over the same study period.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Compare Power Centre configurations against the feed and brine duties, and review seawater pumping separately where needed. Supply the process conditions and production target.

Which process and maintenance records make the RO comparison useful?

Log pressure, flow, temperature, operating hours and service events. Publish results with their test conditions so a short trial is not mistaken for a lifetime performance guarantee.
