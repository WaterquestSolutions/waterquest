Water Mist or Sprinklers in the Data Centre? Start With the Fire

Water mist and sprinklers protect different tested hazards and arrangements. Compare the adopted NFPA 750 and NFPA 13 design requirements for the data centre.

Industry Applications7 min read

Download the worksheet for this guide (editable text) · Open the fire requirement register (CSV)

Written by WaterQuest Solutions · Updated 2 November 2026 · About the distributor

Use water effectively against the defined fire hazard

Use the fire-protection design basis

NFPA 750 covers water-mist fire-protection systems; NFPA 13 covers sprinkler installation. NFPA 20 addresses stationary fire pumps and NFPA 25 addresses inspection, testing and maintenance of water-based protection. ISO 4413 covers hydraulic fluid-power safety and is not the governing fire-suppression design standard.

Engineers should distinguish controlling fire growth, suppressing a fire and extinguishing it. A system can provide valuable cooling and limit spread without guaranteeing that every hidden ignition source has been extinguished.

Why droplet size creates an opportunity

Dividing water into smaller droplets increases surface area relative to volume. That can accelerate heat transfer where droplets encounter hot gases or burning surfaces. A fine spray that is carried away or blocked cannot be credited with the same effect as a tested arrangement.

This explains both the attraction and the limits of water mist. It may achieve a particular protection objective with substantially less discharge than a different water-based arrangement, but the result belongs to the complete system and hazard. Pressure alone does not establish effectiveness. A general-purpose water-hydraulic pump is not, by itself, an approved fire-suppression installation.

What a zone actually does

A zone is a defined part of the protection arrangement, not a promise that water stops at an invisible boundary.

A clear drawing should identify detection areas, water-supply sections, head or nozzle types and physical compartments separately. Those boundaries need not coincide. The design must also consider how heat and smoke move between spaces, including through cable penetrations or shared ventilation.

Pre-action is a sequence, not a universal safeguard

Pre-action systems add a defined release arrangement between fire detection and water delivery. The lesson for procurement is to obtain the actual cause-and-effect sequence rather than accept the word pre-action as a complete specification.

For example, the responsible designer must establish what occurs after a detector alarm, a damaged head, a valve fault, loss of supervisory pressure or loss of power. A sequence intended to reduce unintended discharge can introduce dependencies that must be monitored and tested. Convenience during normal operation must not obscure the equipment's required response to a real fire.

Calculate water use from the design

Water consumption depends on the flow through operating devices and the duration of discharge. If several sections operate or flow changes with pressure, the calculation must include those conditions. Storage requirements may also include reserves or other design provisions; the expected amount used in one illustrative incident is not automatically the required tank capacity.

Consider a purely arithmetic example. An assumed discharge of 60 litres per minute (15.9 US gpm) for ten minutes uses 600 litres (159 US gal). Another assumed arrangement at 180 litres per minute (47.6 US gpm) for the same duration uses 1,800 litres (476 US gal). The difference is 1,200 litres (317 US gal), or two thirds of the second volume. They neither size a data-centre system nor establish equal fire performance, and they must not be presented as product ratings.

Marioff publishes application-dependent water-use benefits for its own data-centre solution. Those claims should lead to a project comparison using the offered design and its approval evidence, rather than being transferred to an unrelated package. Ask each bidder to show the protected hazard, required discharge conditions and water demand on a common basis.

Reduced discharge can reduce consequences without eliminating damage

Less water reaching a protected area may reduce the amount that must be collected and dried. It can also affect the scale of drainage, storage and recovery arrangements.

The useful engineering question is therefore where water can go. Review floor slopes, penetrations, cable routes, equipment elevations and drainage capacity. Promising absolutely minimal damage is not defensible; designing to limit the extent and consequences of the event is a concrete and valuable objective.

Airflow and equipment changes can alter the original basis

Data centres often change faster than their building systems. New rack layouts, containment panels, cable trays, battery equipment or cooling arrangements may affect spray distribution and fire development.

Ventilation deserves particular attention because it can transport heat and deflect droplets. The relevant approval conditions and design documentation should identify the supported arrangement. Simulation can help explore a question, but its assumptions and validation must be understood. For executives, change control is part of preserving the original investment, not an administrative burden added after the system is installed.

Read approval evidence at the correct level

A manufacturer may offer several configurations with different applications, limitations and documentation. Ask for the precise system identification, approved hazard coverage and installation conditions that apply to the proposed project. A component certificate, a supplier quality certification or a successful installation elsewhere does not automatically establish approval of the complete arrangement at your site.

The owner should agree responsibilities among the specialist designer, installer, equipment supplier, insurer and relevant authority. This article explains selection principles; it does not reproduce a fire standard or establish compliance. Likewise, the public scope of a hydraulic machinery standard cannot replace the specific fire-protection requirements. The correct evidence is the evidence governing the actual system, including the interactions between detection, release, water delivery and building operation.

Make inspection and impairment management part of the purchase

A suppression system spends much of its life waiting. A compact installation that cannot be inspected properly creates an avoidable operating problem.

Impairment procedures matter when sections are isolated for construction or service. The responsible team must know which area is affected, how the condition is communicated and how protection is restored and verified. Temporary measures should be set through the site's authorized process.

Compare proposals through a realistic event

A useful review workshop follows an imagined fire from detection through recovery. Establish where the fire begins, how the system recognizes it, which section operates, how water reaches the hazard and what happens to nearby services. Then follow the operator's information: which alarm is displayed, who responds, and how the affected area is made safe.

Repeat the discussion for an impaired supply, maintenance isolation and a changed room layout. Any verification work must be planned by qualified specialists using authorized methods.

Handover should preserve the design assumptions

The final records should connect the installed equipment to the approved drawings, settings and inspection requirements.

The strongest case is specific and measurable

Water mist deserves serious consideration where an applicable system can meet the fire objective while reducing discharge, limiting the affected area and supporting recovery.
