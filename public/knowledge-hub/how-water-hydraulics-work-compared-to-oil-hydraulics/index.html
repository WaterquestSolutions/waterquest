Performance Comparisons

How Water Hydraulics Work Compared to Oil Hydraulics

Compare how each system delivers the movement you need.

Water hydraulics use the same pressure-and-flow principles as oil hydraulics, with equipment designed for water’s different properties. See what stays familiar, what changes and how to compare both systems at the same useful output.

Article 002Performance Comparisons10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Keep hydraulic force and change the working medium

Water and oil hydraulics use the same pressure-and-area relationship to create ideal actuator force. Water-service equipment changes the internal materials, surfaces and clearances to suit the different fluid. That gives you a route to controlled hydraulic movement while removing working oil and its handling tasks from the selected circuit.

Consider a gate that must open in twenty seconds and remain stationary for several hours. Its movement requires flow, its load requires pressure, and its holding function requires an appropriate valve or mechanical arrangement. Neither oil nor water guarantees all three simply by filling the reservoir.

Why low viscosity is both useful and demanding

Viscosity describes resistance to shear. Lower viscosity can reduce some fluid-friction losses, while making leakage through a given clearance easier. A pump with excessive internal leakage delivers less useful outlet flow even if its shaft speed has not changed. Purpose-designed water equipment manages those clearances and sliding interfaces; conventional oil equipment cannot be judged suitable from pressure rating alone. [1]

The comparison must also use matching temperatures and units. An oil's ISO viscosity grade is based on kinematic viscosity at a specified temperature. Comparing that number directly with water's dynamic viscosity produces a misleading ratio.

Maintenance changes its emphasis

An oil circuit may require attention to oil condition, additive performance and contamination. A water circuit still needs particle control, compatible seals, sound inlet conditions and inspection for scale or corrosion. Fresh make-up water can introduce dissolved substances even when it looks clean. Removing an oil-change task does not justify removing the maintenance programme.

For procurement, the useful question is which tasks disappear, which remain and which are introduced. Cost those tasks using the actual circuit volume, access time and operating schedule.

Define an equivalent output before comparing inputs

A fair comparison begins with the work completed. Suppose two systems move the same gate through the same stroke, but one takes twice as long. Their peak electrical readings cannot establish which design is more efficient or better suited to the process. The test needs a common load, movement requirement and operating sequence. Where speed is allowed to vary, explain why that variation is acceptable and compare energy over the completed task rather than at an arbitrary instant.

The useful output may include more than movement. A machine may need to hold position, achieve a specified stopping behaviour or remain ready for an emergency command. Count the energy and supporting equipment required to provide those functions. If one offer includes an independent emergency arrangement and the other omits it, the two purchase prices do not describe equivalent systems. Establish the comparison boundary before drawing a commercial conclusion.

This approach gives different readers a common basis for discussion. Engineering defines the required output and acceptable variation. Operations supplies the real sequence and interruptions. Procurement makes sure each quotation covers the same functions. Management can then compare costs without having to assume that every lower-priced offer provides the same service. The working medium remains important, but it is evaluated inside a clearly described machine duty.

Investigate where losses occur instead of assigning them to the fluid

A hydraulic system can lose useful power through internal leakage, mechanical friction, pressure drops and control arrangements that supply more flow or pressure than the active duty needs. Some losses are strongly influenced by fluid properties; others are mainly consequences of the architecture or operating point. Calling all observed consumption an oil penalty or a water advantage obscures which design change would actually improve the result.

For example, a valve that drops 20 bar (290 psi) while passing 12 litres per minute (3.17 US gpm) dissipates approximately 0.4 kilowatts (0.536 hp). The calculation follows pressure difference multiplied by flow. Changing the medium does not make that required pressure drop disappear. The designer must consider whether the control function can be delivered with less loss, whether another architecture is appropriate or whether the loss is an accepted part of the selected arrangement.

A useful investigation measures pressures at meaningful boundaries and records flow during the relevant sequence. Pump outlet pressure alone cannot identify the pressure available at a distant actuator. Electrical input alone cannot separate motor losses from hydraulic losses. Better measurements do not merely support marketing claims; they show where an improvement is possible and prevent a supplier from attributing an architectural benefit to the fluid without evidence.

Include the waiting time between movements

Consider a hypothetical actuator that moves for ten minutes during each eight-hour shift. Even a substantial improvement in its motion efficiency may contribute less to the daily result than a small difference in the remaining standby period. Conversely, a continuously loaded machine may justify close attention to efficiency near its dominant operating point. The same pair of power units can therefore produce different economic comparisons when assigned different schedules.

Represent the schedule with operating states rather than a single percentage labelled utilisation. Include moving under load, holding, waiting, starting and any required conditioning or circulation. Multiply the power associated with each state by its duration, then add the contributions. Where power changes during a stroke, use an appropriate measured energy total or a defensible time-based estimate. State which values are measured and which remain assumptions.

The duty schedule should also include seasonal variation and exceptional operating periods when those matter to the asset. A flood-control installation may remain idle for long periods and then face sustained demand. An average day may conceal the event that determines component capacity or emergency readiness. Separate annual energy estimates from the conditions used to size equipment; neither number should be forced to serve both purposes.

Put water consumption on a defined boundary

Using water as a hydraulic medium does not by itself specify the amount of water consumed. A recirculating circuit may reuse its working fluid, while make-up, leakage, maintenance drains and treatment losses contribute to actual demand. A once-through arrangement has a different balance. Comparing either system with oil requires a clear description of what enters, what leaves and what is retained during operation.

This distinction is particularly important where the site's water has a treatment or pumping cost. A litre supplied to a remote facility may carry more operational effort than its commodity price suggests. The receiving side also matters: water that has contacted the machine is not automatically suitable for discharge to any drain or process. Its condition and the site's requirements determine the permitted destination.

For procurement, request the expected normal and exceptional water demands separately. Normal demand supports operating-cost estimates. Exceptional demand helps size storage, replenishment or servicing arrangements. Ask what assumptions determine each figure and how the installed result will be checked. This creates a practical comparison without suggesting either that water circuits consume no water or that the working-fluid volume must be replaced on every movement.

Translate a release scenario into a design consequence

The value of removing hydraulic oil depends strongly on location. An actuator directly above an open channel presents a different release pathway from equipment inside a maintained containment area. Trace the likely route from a failed connection to the exposed asset or process. Identify existing controls, the material inventory involved and the response that would be required. This exercise makes the benefit concrete without assigning an unsupported probability to a rare event.

A suitable water circuit removes the hydraulic-oil inventory associated with that circuit. It does not remove pressurised-fluid hazards, loss of function, flooding or damage from other site materials. The comparison should therefore identify which failure consequence changes and which remains. A business case is more credible when it acknowledges the residual duties than when it describes the entire installation as risk-free.

Avoid treating every avoided incident as a certain annual saving. Routine oil purchasing, waste handling and inspections can often be estimated from records. Rare event costs need a different treatment, such as a clearly described sensitivity or scenario. That separation lets an executive understand both the predictable operating effect and the strategic reason for reducing a particular exposure, without confusing a hypothetical event with money already being spent.

Compare the transition, not just the finished machines

A new installation can select compatible components together. A retrofit must deal with inherited mountings, controls, pipework, access and outage constraints. The cost of changing medium may therefore be driven by integration rather than the purchase price of the pump. Reusing a bracket can be reasonable even when reusing the original hydraulic component is not; mechanical fit and fluid compatibility are separate approvals.

Prepare a boundary drawing that identifies what will change, what will remain and where the new equipment interfaces with the existing machine. Verify the information that could disrupt commissioning: actual port arrangements, electrical supply, movement sequence, support structure and access for replacement. A proposal based only on old drawings can underestimate work if the installation has been modified during its service life.

Training and spare-parts support belong in the transition plan. Operators need to understand the new fluid-management requirements and the permitted maintenance procedures. Stores personnel need a way to distinguish supported water-service parts from superficially similar oil-service stock. These provisions are not arguments against adoption; they are how a technically attractive option becomes dependable in the owner's existing maintenance organisation.

Establish what would change the decision

Before committing to a trial, agree which observations would justify adoption and which would require redesign. The criteria might include completing the specified motion, acceptable leakage, stable fluid condition, service access and energy use over a representative duty. A pilot that produces only a general impression of smooth operation leaves the main investment questions unanswered.

Use the comparison to identify information gaps rather than award a predetermined winner. Water may be compelling where the operating duty can be met and removing oil resolves a costly exposure. An existing oil installation may remain reasonable where the duty is well served and the conversion burden is disproportionate. A supported conclusion in either direction strengthens the next decision because it explains the conditions that produced it.
