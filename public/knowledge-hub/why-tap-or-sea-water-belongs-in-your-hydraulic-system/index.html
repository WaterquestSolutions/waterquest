Choosing Tap Water or Seawater for a Hydraulic Circuit

Choose the working water before choosing the components.

Tap water and seawater create different demands inside a hydraulic circuit. Learn how chemistry, particles, temperature and corrosion exposure influence component selection and the water-management plan.

Article 051Specification & Design10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Choose the working water before the components

Freshwater and seawater create different material and operating requirements. Identify the actual supply, its chemistry and expected variation, then select the pump, valves, seals and treatment around it. A dedicated conditioned-water loop can separate the hydraulic duty from the surrounding water; a seawater circuit needs equipment specifically supported for that service.

A coastal location does not automatically require seawater inside an actuator circuit. The external environment may expose the equipment to salt spray while a recirculating circuit uses controlled freshwater. Conversely, an installation that pumps seawater as part of a process cannot avoid internal saline exposure merely by protecting its enclosure. Keep these two environments separate in the enquiry. This allows the supplier to evaluate internal wetted parts and external construction for their respective duties instead of compressing both questions into the word marine.

Define what the available water actually contains

A useful fluid description identifies source, temperature and relevant chemistry, together with particles and treatment chemicals. Provide available analyses and explain when and where samples were taken. A single result may not represent seasonal variation, a changed supply or intermittent contamination. Ask the component supplier which parameters are important for the proposed equipment rather than ordering an arbitrary set of tests that misses the controlling condition. The aim is a fluid envelope that can be assessed and maintained.

Physical appearance is not an adequate compatibility test. Clear water can contain dissolved salts or treatment constituents, while cloudy water may contain particles that a suitable treatment stage can remove. These are different issues. Filtration does not necessarily address dissolved chemistry, and a chemistry result does not establish particle cleanliness. Separating the questions helps the owner understand what treatment is needed and why. It also avoids assuming that a familiar label such as potable, filtered or demineralised automatically proves suitability for every internal material and moving interface.

Consider the complete seawater supply arrangement

Seawater may be abundant at a site, but available volume is only part of the supply question. An intake, screening, treatment, storage or boost arrangement may be needed to deliver the required conditions. The supply must remain suitable during operation and the credible low-supply state. Debris, marine growth and changing source conditions can affect the arrangement. Include these supporting functions in the technical and commercial comparison rather than treating raw-water availability as equivalent to a ready hydraulic utility.

The inlet conditions at the selected pump need configuration-specific confirmation. Pipe length, elevation, restrictions and demand influence the pressure available at the inlet while water is flowing. A static gauge reading elsewhere in the supply network does not establish that condition. The Water Hydraulics Company's pump information highlights the importance of fluid and inlet configuration when selecting purpose-designed water equipment. [1] For a project, request current instructions for the exact model and avoid transferring details from another pump or a general web page into the final design without verification.

Understand why chloride exposure changes material selection

Stainless steel is a family of alloys, not a guarantee of immunity. Chloride-containing environments can create local corrosion concerns whose severity depends on temperature, geometry, deposits and other conditions. Small crevices can matter even when most of a component appears sound. The relevant selection question includes the alloy, its condition and the way it is used. A stainless body label does not establish the suitability of internal springs, plugs, fasteners or mating parts. [2]

This does not mean seawater service is impossible. It means the material package must be chosen for it. Ask for a complete wetted-material schedule and the supported operating envelope. Consider seals, polymer elements and dissimilar-metal contacts as well as the principal metal parts. Where a different alloy or construction is proposed, identify which exposure it addresses. A more expensive material should solve a defined problem, while a less expensive material should not be accepted simply because another component elsewhere has operated successfully under different conditions.

Compare recirculating and once-through operation

A recirculating hydraulic circuit returns its working fluid for reuse. Its water condition can change through contamination, temperature, make-up and service activities. A once-through arrangement continually draws from a source and produces an outflow. These architectures create different consumption, treatment and discharge questions. Neither should be selected solely from an assumption that water is cheap or plentiful. Establish the operating schedule and calculate the relevant volumes for the actual arrangement before comparing ownership costs.

For an illustrative once-through duty, a flow of 5 litres per minute (1.32 US gpm) operating for 12 minutes uses 60 litres (15.9 US gal) during that period. That arithmetic says nothing about whether the discharge is acceptable or whether the circuit should be once-through. A recirculating version may have much lower routine consumption, but its reservoir, fluid management and heat rejection become part of the design. The useful comparison includes all supporting equipment and the conditions it must maintain. It does not compare raw-fluid prices while ignoring the system around them.

Evaluate freshwater as an engineered alternative

Controlled freshwater can simplify some internal corrosion questions in a coastal actuator installation, but it still requires a supply and management plan. Determine how the circuit is filled, how make-up is provided and how contamination is prevented during service. If freshwater is scarce or expensive at the site, quantify the expected requirement rather than assuming it is negligible. A small closed circuit and a large once-through process can have very different implications for local resources.

Freshwater is not automatically the same as the component manufacturer's reference fluid. Treatment residuals, hardness, pH or other constituents may require review. Very low mineral content is also a distinct condition rather than a universal improvement. Provide the actual proposed fluid and obtain support for it. This approach avoids replacing one assumption with another: seawater is not inherently unsuitable for all water equipment, and tap water is not inherently suitable for every configuration. The engineering task is to match a defined fluid to a supported complete circuit.

Include shutdown, flushing and preservation

Operating conditions change when equipment stops. Water can remain in small cavities, lines and valve passages for long periods. The preservation method should address the expected duration, temperature and contamination risks. If a seawater circuit will be flushed with another fluid, confirm that the transition and final stored condition are supported by the components. A general instruction to flush thoroughly is incomplete unless the method can reach the relevant volumes and the operator can verify the intended result.

Do not improvise additives or cleaning chemicals because they appear compatible with the main metal body. Seals, polymers and instruments may have different limits, and residues can alter subsequent operation. The supplier should identify the approved procedure and any return-to-service checks. Design access and drainage with that procedure in mind. A system that is easy to run but difficult to preserve may perform poorly in seasonal or standby service. The fluid choice should therefore be evaluated over the entire operating calendar, not only during a steady running condition.

Separate corrosion data from a service-life promise

Material reference data can help screen candidates, but it is not a direct prediction of assembled equipment life. Laboratory corrosion results depend on the tested solution and conditions. Real geometry, deposits, flow and fabrication can change exposure. Outokumpu's corrosion tables explain that such data must be interpreted in the context of actual service. [3] Use them to inform questions and selection, then seek configuration-specific support and appropriate verification for the proposed installation.

The same principle applies to an isolated successful example. A component that has worked in one seawater duty may have had different temperature, maintenance or material options. Ask what conditions the evidence covers and which differences matter. Where uncertainty is material, a representative test or monitored pilot may be more useful than a broad assurance. Define what will be observed and what would trigger review. This gives procurement a way to handle uncertainty without demanding an unsupported universal lifetime guarantee or accepting one at face value.

Make fluid management practical for the operator

A selected fluid envelope must be maintainable. Identify which conditions can be monitored on site and which require periodic sampling or supplier review. Locate sampling and service points so that the team can use them safely and obtain representative information. Establish how a changed source, unusual result or treatment failure is reported. The monitoring plan should focus on parameters that influence the installed equipment rather than collect data that nobody uses to make an operating decision.

Keep approved fluids, treatments and procedures with the asset documentation. Label fill and drain connections clearly, especially where freshwater and seawater systems coexist. Train staff to recognise that an available water connection is not automatically an approved make-up source. These ordinary controls protect the engineering decision over time. They are particularly valuable at remote or shared facilities where different contractors may service the equipment. Compatibility is sustained through the way the system is operated, not established permanently by the first fill alone.

Choose the complete arrangement with the best fit

The final comparison should bring together fluid availability, material package, inlet supply, treatment, recirculation, discharge and maintenance. Compare installed and recurring costs on the same duty. Identify which benefits depend on assumptions that still need confirmation. The preferred answer may be a freshwater actuator circuit in a saline external environment, or a specifically supported seawater configuration where the process requires it. The site and function determine the choice.

WaterQuest can help turn these inputs into a defined equipment enquiry and a supported circuit boundary. The benefit of water hydraulics is the ability to provide useful hydraulic service while avoiding mineral oil in the selected working circuit. That benefit is strongest when the fluid is chosen deliberately. A clear specification protects the buyer from treating all water as equivalent and gives the supplier the information needed to select equipment that the owner can operate and maintain with confidence.

From understanding to equipment selection

Treat freshwater and seawater as different specifications

Provide salinity, temperature, solids and the intended operating pattern when selecting a pump circuit. Confirm approval of the complete supplied configuration for the selected water; a generic water-service description does not authorise seawater substitution.

What should be planned for seawater inspection and shutdown?
