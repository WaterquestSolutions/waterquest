Sizing a Water Cylinder: Start at the Load Pin

A worked 80 mm example shows how load, linkage geometry and both piston areas set the pressure and flow, using the same arithmetic as any oil design.

Specification & Design7 min read

Download the worksheet for this guide (editable text) · Open the system requirement register (CSV)

Written by WaterQuest Solutions · Updated 2 November 2026 · About the distributor

Turn the customer’s movement into engineering inputs

This guide: Load and linkage sizing

Motion quality

Water hydraulics then provide one way of delivering that motion. Pressure acts on the piston; flow fills the expanding chamber; the control circuit manages the return side and any overrunning load.

A worked force and flow example

Take an illustrative cylinder with an 80 mm (3.15 in) bore and a 40 mm (1.57 in) rod. Its full piston area is 0.00503 m² (0.0541 ft²) and its annular area is 0.00377 m² (0.0406 ft²). With 100 bar (1,450 psi) at the cap end and 5 bar (72.5 psi) at the rod end, ideal extension force is approximately 48.4 kN (10,881 lbf): cap-end pressure times full area, minus rod-end pressure times annular area. Friction has not yet been deducted.

To extend 500 mm (19.7 in) in ten seconds, piston speed is 0.05 m/s (0.164 ft/s). The ideal cap-end supply flow is about 15.1 L/min (3.99 US gpm).

Turn ideal performance into controllable performance

Allow for seal friction, valve pressure loss, hose expansion, trapped air and internal leakage. The rod, mounting pins and supporting structure also need mechanical checks; hydraulic capacity does not establish buckling resistance or allowable side load.

For a descending load, metering inlet flow alone may not control speed. The load can drive the actuator. Appropriate load-control architecture must manage that condition, including stopping and loss of supply.

Convert a mechanism load into actuator force

The load at a cylinder pin is not necessarily equal to the weight or process force being moved. A linkage changes the relationship through its geometry.

An illustrative lever requires a turning moment of 4,000 newton metres. If the perpendicular moment arm is 0.20 metres (0.656 ft), the ideal cylinder force is 20,000 newtons. If the geometry reduces that arm to 0.05 metres (0.164 ft) near the closed position, the ideal demand becomes 80,000 newtons for the same moment. Neither number includes friction or a design allowance.

Evaluate the full motion envelope and the direction of the applied load. Give the hydraulic designer a load-versus-position relationship where available, and identify uncertain portions rather than hiding them inside an unexplained safety factor.

Check both directions and the fluid displaced on return

A single-rod cylinder has unequal chamber areas. At equal piston speed, the volume entering one chamber differs from the volume leaving the other.

Use the illustrative 80 millimetre bore and 40 millimetre rod from the earlier example. The annular area is three quarters of the full piston area.

The volume balance also matters for servicing and fault investigation. The appropriate fluid-management arrangement follows the actuator geometry and circuit architecture, not a rule that every installation needs the same reservoir size.

Distinguish breakaway, running and stopping requirements

A stationary mechanism can require a different force to start than it needs while moving. Seal friction, process contact and mechanical constraints can contribute to this difference. A specification containing only a maximum static load leaves these parts of the motion undefined.

Stopping deserves equal attention. Fluid has inertia, lines can be elastic and the load may continue moving after a command changes. Pressure-limiting equipment must be assessed within that architecture rather than treated as proof that every stopping event is benign.

An acceptance demonstration should therefore record time histories where the duty warrants them. A slow display that reports an average value cannot establish the size of a short transient.

Match feedback to the control question

A control system can only act on the information available to it. A limit switch may confirm that the actuator reached an endpoint, but it does not describe position throughout travel. A position sensor can provide a continuous measurement, while a pressure sensor describes a hydraulic condition that must be interpreted in context. Pump pressure alone is not a direct measurement of actuator force when line losses and opposite-chamber pressure are significant.

Start by specifying the outcome: acceptable position error, movement time, settling behaviour or confirmation of a completed sequence. A proportional valve can be useful in a suitable system, but the label does not guarantee a particular accuracy.

Consider what the operator should see when the commanded movement does not occur.

Treat the cylinder as part of a mechanical assembly

A cylinder's ability to generate axial force does not make it suitable for arbitrary side loading. Where the actuator pushes a compressive load, the rod and its effective unsupported length need a buckling assessment appropriate to the actual end conditions.

Installation tolerances belong in the design handover. Do not rely on a flexible hose to compensate for a mechanical mismatch at a rigid cylinder mounting.

Access should be considered before the structure is finalized. Operators need a practical way to inspect connections, replace supported service parts and secure the mechanism before maintenance.

Decide which uncertainties need a calculation and which need a test

Not every unknown is resolved in the same way. Piston area follows geometry and can be calculated directly. The load imposed by an existing mechanism may need measurement because available drawings do not describe its current condition.

For a retrofit, create a short evidence register linking each requirement to a calculation, a supplier limit, a site measurement or an acceptance demonstration. A successful warm test does not necessarily establish cold-start behaviour, and a no-load stroke does not prove loaded movement. Where a full demonstration cannot be performed before installation, agree how the remaining uncertainty will be managed and who accepts that boundary.

The purpose is not to demand a test for every familiar engineering relationship. It is to focus effort where uncertainty could change the design or operating outcome.

Test the operating sequence

A useful acceptance demonstration includes starting against the load, movement at minimum and maximum demand, stopping between endpoints, holding, and recovery after an interruption.

The figures above are teaching calculations, not a component selection or certified design. Their value is that they connect a process requirement to measurable hydraulic quantities.
