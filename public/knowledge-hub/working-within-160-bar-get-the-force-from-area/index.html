Working Within 160 Bar: Get the Force From Area

Going from an 80 to a 100 mm bore adds 56 % force at the same pressure. Size the cylinder and water delivers the force the job needs.

Specification & Design6 min read

Download the worksheet for this guide (editable text) · Open the cylinder requirement register (CSV)

Written by WaterQuest Solutions · Updated 2 November 2026 · About the distributor

Turn the pressure limit into useful force

Working within a defined boundary encourages complete sizing. The required force may be achievable through actuator area, a different linkage or a revised movement sequence rather than higher pressure. The purpose of engineering is to find a supported combination that performs the duty, not to operate every component at its maximum number. Current model-specific documentation, such as the Water Hydraulics Company's pump information, is one input to that selection.

Translate the load into actuator force

Begin with the force the mechanism actually needs. Process pressure, weight, friction and linkage geometry can all contribute.

The ideal pressure-area relationship provides the starting calculation. OpenStax explains how pressure and area relate to force under simplified hydraulic assumptions. For a real double-acting cylinder, both chambers matter. The supply-side pressure force is opposed by pressure acting on the other effective area, together with friction and any other resisting forces.

See how bore changes force at the same pressure

Consider an illustrative extension calculation at 120 bar (1,740 psi) with negligible opposing pressure and friction. A 100 millimetre bore has an area of about 0.007854 square metres and produces approximately 94.2 kilonewtons of ideal force. An 80 millimetre bore has an area of about 0.005027 square metres and produces approximately 60.3 kilonewtons under the same assumptions.

The comparison shows why pressure is only one sizing variable. Increasing bore from 80 to 100 millimetres (3.15 to 3.94 in) increases area by a factor of 1.5625, or about 56 percent. If movement time must remain unchanged, the supply flow must increase accordingly before leakage and other effects are considered.

Check retraction separately

A conventional single-rod cylinder has less effective area on the rod side than on the cap side. Do not assume that a cylinder selected for pushing has the same pulling capability.

The area difference also affects speed and return flow. With the same supplied flow, the smaller rod-side area gives a higher ideal retraction speed.

Reserve capacity for real operating behaviour

Normal operation should not be assumed safe merely because a steady pressure reading is below the boundary. Switching, rapid deceleration, changing loads and trapped fluid can produce transient pressures. A design must account for these effects and the response of its protective arrangement. There is no universal instruction to set a relief valve at a fixed distance below 160 bar (2,321 psi) and consider the matter resolved.

Relief behaviour itself has conditions, including flow and response. A nominal setting does not mean every point in the circuit will be held exactly at that pressure during every event.

Do not use hydraulic protection as a structural calculation

Pressure limits help bound hydraulic forces, but they do not establish the strength of rods, pins, mounts or supporting structures. A long cylinder rod can require a buckling assessment when pushing.

Likewise, stopping a moving mass can create loads different from those in a steady push calculation. The structure and mechanism must support the intended acceleration and deceleration. Specify both the minimum force needed and any maximum force that must not be exceeded.

Examine the flow and power consequences

Hydraulic power depends on pressure difference and flow. At a fixed pressure, increasing flow to move a larger cylinder at the same speed increases required hydraulic power. The motor input must also account for losses and the selected duty. A larger actuator is therefore not a free alternative to higher pressure.

Consider the time pattern as well. A large flow needed briefly may lead to a different architecture from the same flow required continuously. An accumulator or staged movement may be evaluated where appropriate, but it introduces additional selection and safety questions. Do not assume energy storage reduces the total energy required to perform the work.

Use mechanical options when they genuinely improve the duty

A linkage change can alter the relationship between cylinder force and useful motion. Increasing mechanical advantage may reduce required cylinder force while increasing travel or changing speed.

Sometimes the process requirement itself deserves review. A rapid approach followed by a slower loaded movement may fit the operation better than demanding maximum force at maximum speed throughout the stroke. Any revised sequence must still meet process and safety needs.

Verify the limit at the installed system

Factory testing can establish selected package behaviour, but the installed mechanism and piping may create different loads and transients. Commissioning should verify relevant pressures and movements under representative conditions using an approved plan.

ISO 4413's public scope emphasizes general hydraulic-system safety across the equipment lifecycle, but the full applicable requirements and project engineering determine the specific verification work. Keep final settings, component limits and drawings together in the asset documentation. A system remains within its supported range through controlled design and operation, not through the presence of a maximum-pressure label alone.

Know when another arrangement is the better answer

If the required force and speed demand an impractically large cylinder or excessive flow within the supported pressure boundary, acknowledge the mismatch. A credible supplier should help identify that limit rather than imply that every duty can be accommodated by adjusting a pressure setting.

WaterQuest's value is a documented solution that performs the required job inside a supported operating envelope. The 160 bar (2,321 psi) boundary makes the design conversation concrete: what force is needed, what area and flow provide it, and how will peaks remain controlled? For the owner, it is assurance that the commercial argument rests on a complete design rather than a maximum number taken out of context.
