Designing Water Hydraulic Equipment Within 160 Bar

Turn the pressure limit into a complete force and flow specification.

Need useful force without hydraulic oil? Start with the load and movement you need, then see how cylinder size, motor displacement and flow determine what equipment operating within 160 bar can deliver.

Article 056Specification & Design10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Turn the pressure limit into useful force

Useful hydraulic force comes from pressure acting over an area. Within WaterQuest’s stated upper operating boundary of 160 bar (2,321 psi), cylinder size and motor displacement help determine the loads you can move. Select the whole circuit for its actual operating conditions, including pressure peaks and any components with lower ratings, so the available force becomes dependable work.

Working within a defined boundary encourages complete sizing. The required force may be achievable through actuator area, a different linkage or a revised movement sequence rather than higher pressure. Each choice has consequences for flow, space, structure and cost. The purpose of engineering is to find a supported combination that performs the duty, not to operate every component at its maximum number. Current model-specific documentation, such as the Water Hydraulics Company's pump information, is one input to that selection. [1]

Translate the load into actuator force

Begin with the force the mechanism actually needs. Process pressure, weight, friction and linkage geometry can all contribute. Identify the governing direction and position, because the most demanding point may occur only during part of the stroke. If the cylinder operates through a lever, the available mechanical advantage can change with angle. A force estimate made at a convenient mid-stroke position may miss the condition that determines the required actuator size.

The ideal pressure-area relationship provides the starting calculation. OpenStax explains how pressure and area relate to force under simplified hydraulic assumptions. [2] For a real double-acting cylinder, both chambers matter. The supply-side pressure force is opposed by pressure acting on the other effective area, together with friction and any other resisting forces. Use pressures at the actuator when evaluating available force. A pump outlet reading can overstate the pressure available after line and valve losses, particularly at the required movement flow.

See how bore changes force at the same pressure

Consider an illustrative extension calculation at 120 bar (1,740 psi) with negligible opposing pressure and friction. A 100 millimetre bore has an area of about 0.007854 square metres and produces approximately 94.2 kilonewtons of ideal force. An 80 millimetre bore has an area of about 0.005027 square metres and produces approximately 60.3 kilonewtons under the same assumptions. These are independent teaching calculations, not product capacities or allowable working loads for a complete mechanism.

The comparison shows why pressure is only one sizing variable. Increasing bore from 80 to 100 millimetres (3.15 to 3.94 in) increases area by a factor of 1.5625, or about 56 percent. It also increases the volume required for the same extension stroke by that factor. If movement time must remain unchanged, the supply flow must increase accordingly before leakage and other effects are considered. A larger cylinder can therefore solve a force problem while creating a pump, valve or installation-space problem. The full tradeoff should be assessed together.

Check retraction separately

A conventional single-rod cylinder has less effective area on the rod side than on the cap side. Retraction force at a given pressure is therefore different from extension force, and opposing cap-end pressure acts over the larger area. Do not assume that a cylinder selected for pushing has the same pulling capability. The governing duty may be opening a loaded process valve or recovering a mechanism rather than the movement that appears most important in the normal sequence.

The area difference also affects speed and return flow. With the same supplied flow, the smaller rod-side area gives a higher ideal retraction speed. During retraction, cap-end return flow can exceed the flow entering the rod chamber. Return valves and lines need to be selected for that actual flow, not only the pump's nominal output. An undersized return path can increase opposing pressure and reduce the force that the larger-bore selection was intended to provide. This is a system calculation, not simply a cylinder catalogue exercise.

Reserve capacity for real operating behaviour

Normal operation should not be assumed safe merely because a steady pressure reading is below the boundary. Switching, rapid deceleration, changing loads and trapped fluid can produce transient pressures. A design must account for these effects and the response of its protective arrangement. The appropriate operating setpoints depend on the components, control behaviour and required margin. There is no universal instruction to set a relief valve at a fixed distance below 160 bar (2,321 psi) and consider the matter resolved.

Relief behaviour itself has conditions, including flow and response. A nominal setting does not mean every point in the circuit will be held exactly at that pressure during every event. Isolated volumes may need separate consideration. The responsible designer should evaluate the relevant states and verify them with suitable methods. A slow gauge can miss a brief peak, so measurement capability should match the question. The objective is to keep the complete system within its supported envelope during the actual duty, including credible abnormal or transition states.

Do not use hydraulic protection as a structural calculation

Pressure limits help bound hydraulic forces, but they do not establish the strength of rods, pins, mounts or supporting structures. A long cylinder rod can require a buckling assessment when pushing. Side loading can damage guidance and seals even when axial force is acceptable. Mounting geometry influences how loads enter the structure throughout the stroke. These questions belong to the mechanical design and should be resolved with the actual dimensions, supports and operating conditions.

Likewise, stopping a moving mass can create loads different from those in a steady push calculation. The structure and mechanism must support the intended acceleration and deceleration. A larger bore may increase available force beyond what the driven equipment can tolerate if controls are not coordinated. Specify both the minimum force needed and any maximum force that must not be exceeded. This prevents a well-intended capacity increase from overloading a valve stem, gate or linkage during seating or obstruction.

Examine the flow and power consequences

Hydraulic power depends on pressure difference and flow. At a fixed pressure, increasing flow to move a larger cylinder at the same speed increases required hydraulic power. The motor input must also account for losses and the selected duty. A larger actuator is therefore not a free alternative to higher pressure. It may be an excellent choice, but it needs corresponding pump, drive, valve and piping selection. Space and utility availability can determine whether the arrangement is practical.

Consider the time pattern as well. A large flow needed briefly may lead to a different architecture from the same flow required continuously. An accumulator or staged movement may be evaluated where appropriate, but it introduces additional selection and safety questions. Do not assume energy storage reduces the total energy required to perform the work. It changes when energy is supplied. The preferred design should explain how the pressure boundary, required motion and available utilities fit together over the complete operating sequence.

Use mechanical options when they genuinely improve the duty

A linkage change can alter the relationship between cylinder force and useful motion. Increasing mechanical advantage may reduce required cylinder force while increasing travel or changing speed. A different mounting location may improve alignment or avoid a poor lever angle. These options can be valuable within a pressure-constrained design, provided the complete movement and structural consequences are assessed. They should not be treated as simple substitutions based on one position in the mechanism.

Sometimes the process requirement itself deserves review. A rapid approach followed by a slower loaded movement may fit the operation better than demanding maximum force at maximum speed throughout the stroke. Any revised sequence must still meet process and safety needs. Engineering dialogue with the owner can reveal flexibility that a terse equipment request hides. The goal is not to weaken the requirement to make a product fit. It is to understand which parts of the stated duty are essential and which can be satisfied through a better overall arrangement.

Verify the limit at the installed system

Factory testing can establish selected package behaviour, but the installed mechanism and piping may create different loads and transients. Commissioning should verify relevant pressures and movements under representative conditions using an approved plan. Record the measurement points, instrument capability and operating state. Check protective functions and the response to permitted abnormal-state tests. The evidence should demonstrate the design boundary rather than merely show that the actuator completed one unloaded stroke.

ISO 4413's public scope emphasises general hydraulic-system safety across the equipment lifecycle, but the full applicable requirements and project engineering determine the specific verification work. [3] Keep final settings, component limits and drawings together in the asset documentation. If a later change increases speed, modifies a valve or alters a load, review its effect on the established pressure envelope. A system remains within its supported range through controlled design and operation, not through the presence of a maximum-pressure label alone.

Know when another arrangement is the better answer

If the required force and speed demand an impractically large cylinder or excessive flow within the supported pressure boundary, acknowledge the mismatch. Another actuator technology, mechanical arrangement or process sequence may be more appropriate. A credible supplier should help identify that limit rather than imply that every duty can be accommodated by adjusting a pressure setting. This protects the owner and preserves confidence in the applications where water hydraulics offer a strong fit.

WaterQuest's value is a documented solution that performs the required job inside a supported operating envelope. The 160 bar (2,321 psi) boundary makes the design conversation concrete: what force is needed, what area and flow provide it, and how will peaks remain controlled? For engineers, it is a sizing and verification problem. For procurement, it is a clear condition of the offer. For the owner, it is assurance that the commercial argument rests on a complete design rather than a maximum number taken out of context.

From understanding to equipment selection

Use area before raising pressure

A larger piston area can meet a higher load at the available pressure, but it also increases stroke volume. Check the required cycle time and return flow before enlarging the cylinder; a force improvement can otherwise create a valve or pipework restriction.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the cylinder force calculator to compare extension and retraction with opposing pressure and the required load. Use the system pressure-drop calculator to combine component losses, elevation and a design allowance.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Review bore, rod, stroke and mounting options together with the valve and return path. Request a configuration that covers the loaded motion in both directions.

Explore water hydraulic cylinders and water hydraulic control valves.

How should the cylinder selection shape the maintenance plan?

Trend loaded extension and retraction separately. Increasing a relief setting to compensate for wear can exceed a weaker component's limit without correcting the underlying loss.

The force tool does not verify rod buckling, structural mountings or machine safety.
