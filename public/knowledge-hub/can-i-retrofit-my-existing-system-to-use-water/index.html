Can I Retrofit My Existing System to Use Water?

Find a practical route from an oil circuit to water hydraulics.

You may be able to keep parts of your machine while replacing its oil hydraulic circuit. Learn what to inspect, which components need water-service alternatives and how to plan the work around your production schedule.

Article 113Testing & Adoption10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Keep useful machinery and rethink the working circuit

A retrofit can address oil leaks or difficult fluid servicing while retaining suitable parts of the existing machine. Start by documenting its movement, load and controls. Review pumps, valves, seals and sliding surfaces for water service, then separate what can be retained from what needs replacement. This gives the project a clear equipment scope and a practical outage plan.

That distinction avoids an expensive false start. A machine can have an excellent mechanical structure and useful controls while its hydraulic components require replacement. Another machine may contain equipment with a documented water-compatible configuration. Each case needs an inventory and evidence rather than a blanket yes or no.

WaterQuest can help assess a defined equipment scope, but the conversion should begin with the owner's reason for changing. Identify the oil pathway, maintenance burden or process concern that makes the work worthwhile. That objective keeps the retrofit focused on a useful outcome while the engineering review determines how much of the original arrangement can responsibly remain.

Recover the actual machine configuration

Start with drawings, component identifiers, operating records and the installed equipment. Compare the records with the machine because repairs and modifications may have changed it over time. An old schematic can omit a bypass, substituted valve or control change that materially affects the retrofit.

List the complete wetted path, including hoses, fittings, instruments and small internal components where relevant information is available. Record the mechanical interfaces, power supply and controls separately. This lets the team assess what can be retained without assuming that every part of a single assembly shares the same compatibility.

Unknowns should become explicit review items. A part with no identifiable material or configuration is not automatically unsuitable, but it lacks the evidence needed for acceptance. The project can then decide whether to obtain documentation, inspect or replace it. This is more useful than allowing uncertain legacy components to disappear into a general statement that the system is mostly stainless.

Define the useful duty before selecting replacements

Document the required force or torque, movement, speed, duty cycle and holding behaviour. Include simultaneous demands and relevant changes in load through the mechanism. A replacement should meet the actual service requirement, not simply reproduce the nominal rating of an old component that may have been selected for a different duty.

Record the existing performance where it is useful. Cycle timing, electrical input and recurring service work can provide a baseline for the new design. Distinguish satisfactory behaviour worth preserving from problems the retrofit is intended to correct. Otherwise the project may unintentionally copy an inefficient or unreliable feature into the replacement.

This functional description also creates room for a better architecture. The preferred water arrangement may use different component sizes or controls while delivering equivalent useful work. It should be evaluated on that basis, with any changed interfaces and limitations clearly identified for the owner and responsible designers.

Classify components by evidence, not appearance

A practical assessment can place each item into one of three groups: supported for the proposed water duty, requiring replacement, or unresolved pending evidence. The categories should be backed by documentation for the actual configuration. Physical fit or a familiar trade name is not sufficient.

For moving components, compatibility includes internal contact behaviour and leakage as well as corrosion resistance. Seal selection also involves the fluid, temperature, motion and installation geometry. Manufacturer seal information illustrates why a generic material description is only part of the selection process. [3]

Do not infer that a component rated for pressure with oil is rated for operation with water. Pressure containment and functional durability answer different questions. The classification exercise should make that distinction visible so procurement can price a complete supported change rather than assuming that uncertain parts can remain simply because they are expensive to replace.

Decide whether to replace a component or a subsystem

Replacing individual components can appear economical, but it may leave a complicated mix of legacy interfaces. A complete subsystem replacement may sometimes provide a clearer support boundary, easier documentation and fewer unresolved interactions. The appropriate choice depends on the machine and the extent of the required change.

Compare both approaches where they are credible. Include installation labour, controls integration, spare parts and future service responsibility. A lower component bill can be outweighed by the effort needed to make a fragmented arrangement work and to diagnose it later.

The supplier's scope should state what it supports. If one party supplies the pump, another retains the valves and a third modifies the controls, someone must own the complete functional design. Clear integration responsibility is essential. Without it, a fault can become a series of component-level explanations while the owner remains without a working machine.

Assess the water supply and operating environment

The new arrangement needs an accepted working-water specification and supply architecture. Define the source, expected temperature and relevant condition management. The Water Hydraulics Company's pump information shows that purpose-designed water equipment still has configuration and supply requirements. [1] The actual selected documentation should govern the retrofit.

Consider the external environment and shutdown pattern as well. Outdoor exposure, cleaning chemicals, freezing conditions or long idle periods may affect the appropriate design. A machine that operated satisfactorily with oil can require a different preservation or fluid-management approach after conversion.

Include these requirements in the project budget and ownership plan. They are not incidental accessories to be decided after installation. A water circuit can be an attractive alternative while still needing filtration, condition checks and a practical response to the site's environmental conditions. The proposal is stronger when those responsibilities are explicit from the start.

Plan the transition between old and new equipment

The conversion programme should identify when the existing function becomes unavailable and what must be complete before service resumes. Resolve equipment identity, documentation and interface questions before entering the critical outage where possible. A delivery delay or unconfirmed part can otherwise turn a planned change into an extended interruption.

The treatment of residual oil and existing contamination requires a project-specific method. Do not assume that a quick water circulation establishes cleanliness or material compatibility. The responsible team and suppliers should define preparation and acceptance requirements for the parts being retained and the new components being introduced.

Hazardous-energy control remains necessary throughout the work. CCOHS provides general guidance on the forms of stored and supplied energy that maintenance programmes must address. [4] The site's authorised procedures must govern the actual isolation and restoration. This article is a planning guide, not an instruction to drain or modify a pressurised machine.

Reassess controls and protective functions

A fluid and component change can alter response, leakage and motion. Existing controls may therefore need review even when the electrical connections remain physically compatible. Confirm that commands, feedback and indications still correspond to the intended machine states.

Holding, emergency response and manual recovery deserve separate attention. The required behaviour after loss of normal power should be deliberate and validated for the completed arrangement. A previous oil-system function cannot be assumed preserved merely because the new actuator moves in the same direction during normal operation.

ISO 4413 provides relevant general hydraulic-system safety principles. [2] Application-specific requirements and the machine's risk assessment also remain important. The retrofit team should identify the documents and acceptance responsibilities that apply, so that changing the working medium does not leave an old assumption unexamined in a function that matters to people or process continuity.

Verify the completed machine against the requirement

Acceptance should compare the new installation with the functional duty defined at the start. Relevant checks may include movement, timing, holding, supply conditions and temperature under the required operating sequence. Record the configuration and the conditions of the test rather than relying on a brief statement that the machine ran.

Separate immediate acceptance from durability evidence. A successful commissioning sequence can establish that the installed function works under the tested conditions. It cannot by itself establish a multi-year maintenance interval. Use supplier evidence and a suitable operating record to address longer-term uncertainty.

Retain any differences from the original machine that operators need to understand. Revised indications, new water checks or changed service arrangements should appear in training and documents. The acceptance process is most useful when it produces a machine the owner can operate and maintain confidently, rather than only a completed contractor checklist.

Make the retained and replaced scope visible at handover

The final record should identify the retained components, new equipment and supporting evidence for each relevant compatibility decision. Update the drawings and spare-parts information to match the installed configuration. This prevents future service work from inadvertently returning the circuit to an unsupported mixture of parts.

Explain the benefit achieved at the actual boundary. The retrofit may remove hydraulic oil from one mechanism while leaving other lubricants elsewhere in the machine. State the changed inventory and operating responsibilities precisely. That gives the owner a defensible result and avoids a broad oil-free claim that the complete installation does not support.

A credible retrofit case combines useful existing assets with a supported water-hydraulic design. WaterQuest's role is to help make that combination technically clear and operationally practical. The question is not whether every old part can be saved. It is whether the completed change delivers the required service, solves the identified problem and leaves the owner with an understandable asset.

A staged decision can limit unnecessary expenditure. Complete the configuration and compatibility review first, then obtain a defined installed proposal. If a major uncertainty remains, use a targeted test before committing to the full conversion. This sequence gives procurement a progressively firmer basis while avoiding a premature promise that the existing machinery can be converted at minimal cost.

After return to service, track the measures that motivated the change. Maintenance interventions, energy and fluid consumption should be related to the actual operating schedule. Reviewing those results helps distinguish a successful conversion from one that merely completed installation, and provides evidence for any later proposal to retrofit similar machinery.

From understanding to equipment selection

Separate reusable machinery from fluid components

A retrofit survey should distinguish the machine frame and linkage from pumps, valves, hoses, seals and sliding interfaces. Record each retained component's water-service evidence; the presence of a stainless housing alone is insufficient.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the pump sizing calculator to estimate displacement, drive torque and shaft power from your flow, pressure and efficiency assumptions. Use the lifecycle cost calculator to compare capital, recurring service, replacement and residual costs over the same study period.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Compare a supported pump and power-unit arrangement with the retained machine. Include the compatibility review, controls, installation and recommissioning in the proposed scope.

What needs to be included in the retrofit outage and service plan?

Plan flushing, temporary isolation, spare parts and recommissioning within the outage. Compare the full conversion cost with replacement before ordering an apparently simple fluid change.

The sizing result does not authorise conversion of an existing oil-service component to water.
