Questions Engineers Ask About Water Hydraulics

Get the answers you need to evaluate water hydraulics.

Considering water hydraulics but unsure where to begin? Get clear answers about loads, water quality, materials, maintenance and conversion, so you can judge where an oil-free circuit could help your equipment.

Article 084Start Here10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Put water to work in a familiar hydraulic circuit

Water hydraulics gives you a practical way to move a gate, turn a motor or operate a valve without hydraulic oil in that working circuit. A drive powers the pump, valves direct the pressurised water, and an actuator turns that energy into useful movement. Start with the task your equipment needs to perform; the questions below explain how to make water work for it.

The phrase does not identify one universal circuit. An installation may recirculate its working water or use another defined supply arrangement. Fresh water and seawater also create different equipment requirements. The first useful question is therefore what exact fluid and architecture the supplier proposes. Once those are clear, engineers can assess the design, procurement can identify the complete scope and operators can understand what must be supplied and maintained. Ambiguous terminology should be resolved before comparing performance claims or making assumptions about water consumption.

Can water produce the same force as oil?

The ideal force relationship depends on pressure and effective area, with pressure on the opposing side included. It does not depend on choosing oil rather than water. [4] A supported water-hydraulic actuator can therefore be assessed for a specified force requirement using the same basic physics. Actual performance also includes friction, leakage, mechanical geometry and the conditions under which the load moves.

The question becomes more useful when it includes speed, travel and duty cycle. An actuator may reach a required force briefly yet be unsuitable for continuous operation or for the necessary movement rate. Likewise, a pressure measured at the pump may differ from the pressure available at the actuator because of losses. Ask for performance at the useful output boundary, including the full travel and relevant load conditions. That connects the fluid-power calculation to the job the owner actually needs performed.

Can an existing oil system simply be filled with water?

That should not be assumed. Oil-service components may rely on the original medium for lubrication, sealing behaviour or corrosion control. Pumps, valves, cylinders, instruments and accessories all require assessment for the proposed fluid. A familiar pressure rating or a stainless exterior does not establish internal compatibility. Reuse decisions need evidence for the actual assembly and operating conditions.

The Water Hydraulics Company's dedicated pump information illustrates that water-compatible machinery has configuration and installation requirements. [1] A retrofit review should classify existing items as supported for reuse, requiring replacement or unresolved pending further information. It should also consider contamination left from the previous service and changes to control behaviour. A credible conversion proposal explains the new equipment boundary and verification plan. Refilling without that review can create a poor test of water hydraulics because it is really testing an unsupported combination of components and fluid.

How are corrosion and wear controlled?

Corrosion and wear are different mechanisms and should be investigated separately. Corrosion depends on materials and their environment, including fluid chemistry, temperature and local conditions such as deposits or crevices. Wear concerns interacting surfaces under load and motion, influenced by material pairing, geometry, lubrication conditions and contamination. A material that resists one mechanism is not automatically suitable for every aspect of a component.

Outokumpu's corrosion guidance provides useful background on the environmental factors involved in stainless selection. [5] For purchasing, request the supported fluid range and the construction selected for the duty. For operation, preserve the approved configuration and service conditions. The answer is not that water needs no protection or that stainless steel never corrodes. It is that purpose-designed machinery uses an appropriate combination of materials, interfaces and operating control, supported by documentation and evidence for the intended application.

What water quality does the system need?

The answer belongs to the selected equipment and architecture. A municipal supply, treated process water, recirculated working water and seawater are not interchangeable descriptions. The supplier needs enough information to confirm suitability, including the constituents or contaminants relevant to its components. A clear-looking sample is not a complete specification, and potable source water does not guarantee that water remains potable after circulating through machinery.

Separate particle removal from treatment of dissolved substances or biological conditions. These are different tasks and may require different controls. The operating plan should identify the approved supply, filling procedure, inspection or monitoring requirements and the response to an unsuitable condition. Avoid adding chemicals informally, because a treatment intended to solve one issue can alter seals, clearances or environmental claims. A useful enquiry asks both what quality is needed at commissioning and how that condition will be maintained during service.

What happens in cold weather or during a power failure?

Cold exposure must be assessed through operation, standby, shutdown and maintenance. Remote tubing and small trapped volumes may be vulnerable even when the main package is sheltered. Insulation reduces heat transfer but cannot maintain temperature indefinitely without an appropriate heat source. Draining is effective only where the circuit geometry and procedure actually remove the relevant water. Any alternative fluid formulation requires supplier support.

Power loss creates a separate question about motion and stored energy. A load may remain capable of driving an actuator, and trapped pressure can persist after the motor stops. CCOHS guidance highlights stored and residual energy within hazardous-energy control. [6] The required response should be defined for each function: holding, controlled movement and maintenance isolation have different objectives. A professional proposal explains those responses instead of treating the working medium as a complete answer to either freezing or machinery safety.

Will the system use less electricity?

It may, but the result depends on the complete arrangement and operating schedule. Pump efficiency, drive efficiency, pressure losses, control strategy and standby consumption all contribute. A comparison must deliver the same useful work and include the same supporting services. An isolated efficiency value at one favourable point does not establish annual savings for the installed machine.

The Department of Energy's pumping-system guidance supports examining the whole system. [3] Ask the supplier to show active and idle states, operating hours and the basis for each consumption estimate. Identify which improvement comes from the water-compatible machinery and which comes from redesign, such as avoiding unnecessary circulation. If a claimed saving determines the investment decision, agree how it will be measured after installation. This approach gives finance a reviewable assumption and gives engineering a practical verification task rather than an unsupported percentage to defend.

Does removing oil eliminate contamination risk?

Removing hydraulic oil from a defined circuit removes that substance from the circuit's potential release. The benefit can be important near exposed product, waterways or difficult-to-clean equipment. Its significance depends on the inventory and the route a leak could follow. The claim should identify the affected circuit so that the owner can understand precisely what has changed.

Other risks remain. Working water can acquire contaminants, other machine lubricants may still be present, and a release can cause flooding or electrical damage. A water-hydraulic circuit is not automatically a hygienic process-water system or an approved discharge source. The sensible objective is to remove a particular contamination pathway while retaining controls for the remaining ones. That is a stronger and more useful benefit than a broad promise of zero contamination, because it can be documented and evaluated against the site's actual operating conditions.

What will maintenance and ownership cost look like?

Begin with tasks rather than a universal maintenance-saving percentage. Determine which oil-related purchases and handling activities disappear, which mechanical inspections remain and which water-management tasks are introduced. Include access, labour, consumables, planned replacements and the service support needed for the selected configuration. A component's purchase price does not describe the cost of restoring service when it fails in a difficult location.

Availability and logistics deserve their own review. Ask about spare identification, lead times, technician requirements and the work the owner's team can perform. Separate routine service from uncertain repair events. Where reliable failure data are unavailable, use clearly labelled scenarios instead of inventing precise expected annual losses. A water-hydraulic project can be worthwhile through a combination of operating capability and reduced exposure even when it does not have the lowest initial price. The financial comparison should make that combination visible.

What should be in a serious supplier enquiry?

Provide the load, movement, duty cycle, environment, working-water information and required fault response. Include existing drawings and site constraints where available. Ask for the proposed equipment boundary, schematic, supported operating limits, performance evidence, service instructions and acceptance method. Identify any intended reuse so that compatibility can be assessed before the scope is fixed.

Distinguish guarantees, relevant tests, estimates and unresolved assumptions. Approval documents should cover the actual equipment and intended use, while public standard abstracts are only orientation material. ISO 4413 provides hydraulic-system scope, not a certificate for a particular installation. [2] The enquiry should also assign integration responsibilities so that essential controls or utilities do not fall between suppliers. WaterQuest can help turn these inputs into an understandable proposal: where the technology fits, what benefit it offers and how the owner can verify the result.

At acceptance, keep the measurements that define normal performance, including the relevant load, temperature and operating sequence. Later observations can then be compared with a known condition. If production demands change, revise the basis rather than interpreting every changed result as deterioration. This record also helps distinguish a component problem from a changed process requirement, which can reduce unnecessary replacement and focus troubleshooting on the mechanism actually responsible for the difference.

A final review should include the people who will operate and maintain the machinery. Their questions about access, abnormal conditions and replacement parts often reveal practical gaps before installation and final commissioning. When those answers are recorded alongside the technical evidence, the owner has more than a sales comparison: it has the beginning of an operating basis that can remain useful throughout the equipment's service life.

From understanding to equipment selection

Turn the questions into a design brief

For a new gate or production machine, define the movement before selecting the power unit: required force or torque, travel, cycle time and what must happen when power is lost. Separate the peak demand from the normal duty so the supplier can assess both capacity and control.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the hydraulic power calculator to translate pressure and flow into hydraulic output and estimated electrical input. Use the system pressure-drop calculator to combine component losses, elevation and a design allowance.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Compare the power-unit arrangement with the actuator duty and control sequence. Send the load, motion and water specification together so the components can be assessed as one circuit.

Which operating measurements make future troubleshooting easier?

Record a commissioning baseline for cycle time, pressure and water condition. Those measurements give maintenance staff a reference when movement becomes slower or less consistent.
