Fluid Properties of Water vs Hydraulic Oil

Understand what changes when water becomes the working fluid.

Water and oil transmit pressure, but they behave differently inside a machine. Understand viscosity, lubrication, temperature and corrosion in plain terms, then see how those differences shape component and circuit design.

Article 008Performance Comparisons10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Turn fluid properties into useful design choices

Water’s low viscosity can reduce resistance in flow paths, and its heat capacity helps it absorb heat with a smaller temperature rise. Purpose-built equipment puts those properties to work while managing leakage, friction and corrosion. Connect each property to the component it affects, then assess the complete circuit at the required duty.

The comparison must also identify the fluids and conditions. Water properties change with temperature and pressure. Hydraulic oils differ by formulation and viscosity grade, and their properties also change with operating conditions. Comparing a value for one fluid at room temperature with another value defined at a different temperature can create a misleading impression even when both numbers are individually correct.

Use property data to support component selection and calculation, then evaluate the complete installation at its required duty. The IAPWS reference for liquid-water properties is useful because it provides a defined scientific basis and validity range. It does not establish an efficiency, corrosion or service-life result for an assembled water-hydraulic machine. Those outcomes require additional design and operating information. [1]

Separate dynamic viscosity from kinematic viscosity

Dynamic viscosity describes resistance to shear in a fluid. Kinematic viscosity is dynamic viscosity divided by density. They are related quantities with different units, so they should not be compared as if they were interchangeable. Dynamic viscosity may be expressed in pascal-seconds or millipascal-seconds, while kinematic viscosity is often expressed in square millimetres per second in fluid data.

An illustrative fluid has a kinematic viscosity of 20 square millimetres per second and a density of 900 kilograms per cubic metre (56.2 lb/ft³) at the same stated condition. Converting the viscosity to 0.000020 square metres per second and multiplying by density gives a dynamic viscosity of 0.018 pascal-seconds, or 18 millipascal-seconds. The example demonstrates the unit relationship; it is not a specification for a particular oil.

When reviewing a comparison chart, check the temperature, pressure, units and identity of the fluid behind each number. A viscosity-grade label is not a complete operating curve, and a value at one reference temperature does not describe a cold start or a warm continuous duty. Ask for data that cover the conditions relevant to the component being selected rather than building a conclusion from one convenient value.

Understand why viscosity affects both losses and leakage

Fluid moving through a passage encounters resistance associated with the flow conditions and geometry. Viscosity influences that behaviour. Within a precision component, the same property also affects flow through small internal clearances. A fluid that moves readily through a desired path may also move more readily through a leakage path. These effects are different parts of the system's power and volume balance.

This is why low viscosity cannot establish overall pump efficiency by itself. Internal leakage can reduce delivered flow, while mechanical friction and other losses influence input power. The component designer must manage the balance through clearances, materials and operating conditions. A water-service pump is assessed as a complete design, rather than as an oil pump whose fluid-friction term has simply been reduced.

For diagnosis, distinguish external losses in lines and valves from internal losses in the pump or actuator. Measure at boundaries that help separate them. If useful flow decreases as conditions change, investigate the component and circuit rather than assuming that the fluid has become unsuitable merely because its viscosity changed. The selected operating envelope should already account for the supported range of fluid properties.

Recognise that density affects pressure head and mass

Density connects volume to mass and appears in several hydraulic relationships. For a stationary liquid column, the pressure increase associated with depth is approximately density multiplied by gravity and height. Using an illustrative density of 1,000 kilograms per cubic metre (62.4 lb/ft³), a two-metre column produces about 19,620 pascals (2.85 psi), or 0.196 bar (2.84 psi), of hydrostatic pressure difference.

This calculation helps explain why a modest reservoir elevation may provide less pressure head than a casual description suggests. It does not establish the actual pump inlet condition during flow, because line losses and other effects must also be included. The pressure reference matters as well: gauge pressure and absolute pressure answer different questions when assessing the fluid at an inlet.

Density also matters when converting a volume of working fluid into a mass for a heat calculation or assessing structural loading from stored fluid. The result belongs to the stated condition and fluid. Avoid using a convenient rounded value beyond the accuracy appropriate to the decision. A preliminary estimate and a final component assessment can reasonably require different levels of property detail.

Distinguish heat capacity from heat rejection

Specific heat capacity describes the energy associated with raising the temperature of a unit mass. Thermal conductivity describes heat transfer through a material under a temperature gradient. These properties influence temperature behaviour in different ways. Neither one, by itself, tells an owner whether a power unit will remain within its operating temperature limit during a long shift.

For a teaching calculation, suppose 100 kilograms (220 lb) of fluid has an assumed constant heat capacity of 4.18 kilojoules per kilogram per degree Celsius. Raising its temperature by ten degrees would require 4.18 megajoules if other effects were neglected. In an operating machine, however, heat is generated and removed continuously. Reservoir area, ambient conditions, circulation and any heat exchanger influence the resulting temperature.

The useful design question is therefore whether heat rejection balances the relevant losses over the specified duty. A brief intermittent movement and continuous operation can have different thermal requirements even when peak pressure and flow are identical. Include motor and auxiliary effects where they fall inside the comparison boundary. Do not translate a favourable fluid property directly into a claim that the complete system needs no cooling assessment.

Account for compressibility and the compliance of the circuit

Liquids compress to a limited extent under pressure. Bulk modulus is a way of describing that relationship over an applicable condition range. A simplified constant-modulus model estimates fractional volume change as pressure change divided by bulk modulus. It can provide an introductory estimate, but a complete response calculation must also consider the construction and contents of the circuit.

For example, an assumed bulk modulus of two gigapascals and a pressure increase of 100 bar (1,450 psi) give a fractional volume change of about 0.005. Applied to ten litres, that corresponds to approximately 50 millilitres. This is an illustrative model using an assumed constant, not a quoted property value for a purchased fluid or a prediction for an installed machine.

Hoses and other boundaries can expand, structures can deflect and trapped gas can change the response. Those contributions may affect the relationship between supplied volume and observed actuator movement. A comparison that attributes the entire system's stiffness or response time to the fluid's bulk modulus therefore leaves out important information. Use fluid properties within a model of the actual circuit rather than as a replacement for it.

Consider vapour pressure at the pump inlet

Vapour pressure relates to the condition at which a liquid can form vapour at a given temperature. In a pump inlet assessment, the relevant local absolute pressure and losses must be considered against the selected equipment's requirements. A favourable outlet pressure reading does not establish satisfactory inlet conditions. The inlet and outlet serve different roles and can experience very different pressures.

Temperature changes are relevant because the fluid properties and vapour condition change. Line geometry, fluid level and flow also affect the pressure available at the pump. A design that appears satisfactory with a full cold reservoir may need review at minimum level and the demanding supported operating condition. The manufacturer's inlet requirements are essential to that assessment.

Water-service product information, such as the P3 documentation, illustrates the importance of selecting and connecting the supported pump configuration. Do not infer that a pump can lift water, accept any boosted supply or tolerate a restricted inlet from its maximum outlet rating. Resolve those conditions for the exact assembly and use the current operating instructions. [2]

Keep chemistry separate from physical-property comparisons

A viscosity or density table does not describe dissolved salts, particles or biological condition. Those characteristics can affect deposits, corrosion and equipment cleanliness. “Water” therefore needs both a physical-property basis for calculation and a practical specification for the actual source. Distilled, municipal, recycled and seawater supplies should not be treated as interchangeable merely because their main constituent is the same.

Likewise, an oil's viscosity value does not describe its complete formulation or suitability for a particular component. Comparisons should distinguish the physical relationship being explained from compatibility and maintenance requirements. This helps prevent a reader from assuming that matching one property makes two fluids interchangeable in an existing assembly.

For the owner, identify how the required fluid condition will be maintained after commissioning. Supply changes, service interventions and extended shutdowns can alter the exposure. A supported operating procedure connects the initial specification to the equipment's life in service. That connection is more useful than a static chart suggesting that the fluid choice alone determines reliability.

Compare the system output after applying the properties

Property data become valuable when they inform a real design and a measurable comparison. Define the task, operating schedule and measurement boundary. Then assess delivered movement or process output, input energy, fluid management and maintenance requirements under matching conditions. System-level pumping guidance supports this approach because it directs attention toward the complete duty and its losses. [3]

Water hydraulics deserve consideration where the required function can be delivered while removing an unwanted hydraulic-oil inventory. The physical properties explain why purpose-designed equipment is necessary and where design opportunities arise. They do not establish automatic superiority on every measure. From understanding to equipment selection

Use properties at the actual operating temperature

Density and viscosity affect velocity, friction and leakage assumptions. Use the working fluid's condition in line-loss calculations, and keep those calculations separate from the pump or valve manufacturer's permitted operating envelope.

Use the Reynolds number calculator to examine flow regime using water temperature, salinity, velocity and diameter. Use the pipe pressure-loss calculator to evaluate pipe friction and local losses using flow, internal diameter, length and water condition.

Why should temperature accompany performance measurements?

Track temperature with performance measurements. Comparing a warm operating test with a cold commissioning baseline can misidentify a property change as equipment deterioration.

Fluid-property and pipe-loss calculations do not establish component compatibility or a permitted temperature range.
