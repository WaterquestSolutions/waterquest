Clean Technology Tax Credits: Check Eligibility Before Counting Savings

Establish eligibility before adding a tax credit to the project case.

Tax credits can affect project economics when the equipment and expenditure meet the rules. Learn how to organise the project information for a qualified eligibility review before including an incentive in your business case.

Article 094Economics & Funding10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Keep the equipment case clear while eligibility is checked

A water hydraulic project can offer practical operating and environmental benefits. Whether it also qualifies for a tax credit depends on the property, claimant, expenditure and current programme rules. Prepare the equipment description and cost breakdown for a qualified review, and assess the project’s economics both with and without any unconfirmed assistance.

This article explains a screening workflow for engineers and buyers. It does not determine a taxpayer's entitlement or recommend a tax position. The useful contribution from the project team is an accurate description of the equipment, its function, ownership and costs. A qualified adviser can then assess that factual package against current tax requirements. Keeping these roles clear prevents a marketing statement from becoming an unsupported financial assumption.

Check current guidance rather than relying on the page address

Programme rules and administrative guidance can change. A saved article or a URL containing a past year does not establish which provisions apply to a current acquisition. Use the current CRA material and the relevant legislation or technical guidance for the transaction being assessed. Record the review date and the facts on which the assessment depends so that later changes can be identified.

The historical path of this Knowledge Hub article is retained for continuity, but its educational purpose is current eligibility screening. Do not infer a rate, deadline or automatic entitlement from the wording of the address. An engineering project can span design, purchase, installation and use over different dates, making the actual transaction timeline more useful than a broad statement that the project belongs to a particular calendar year.

Start with the property, not the desired saving

CRA's current guidance identifies defined types of clean-technology property and conditions applying to them. It does not list ordinary water-hydraulic machinery as a universal qualifying category. [1] A component's use of water to transmit power should not be confused with equipment generating electricity from water energy. Those descriptions concern different functions.

Prepare a functional description of what the proposed asset does. Identify whether it produces, stores, distributes or consumes energy, and how it relates to any larger system. Avoid beginning with a preferred tax category and rewriting the technical description until it appears to fit. An accurate classification exercise may conclude that an otherwise valuable hydraulic project does not qualify, which is a useful result before management commits to spending based on an assumed credit.

The claimant is a separate eligibility question

Property screening and claimant screening are distinct. An asset that appears technically relevant does not establish that every owner can claim the same incentive. CRA provides separate guidance on who can claim the Clean Technology ITC. [2] The actual purchasing or owning entity and its arrangement must be assessed, rather than substituting the supplier's status or a partner's eligibility.

This matters in projects involving municipalities, related companies, partnerships or leased equipment. The entity benefiting from the machinery may not be the entity incurring every cost. Draw a simple ownership and contracting map before seeking advice. It should show who buys the equipment, where it is used and who has the relevant contractual responsibilities. That factual clarity makes the adviser’s review more efficient and helps avoid assumptions that only become visible after procurement.

A component within a larger project needs a defined role

A water-hydraulic assembly may be supplied as part of a wider energy or industrial project. That relationship does not automatically mean the assembly inherits every tax attribute of the larger project. The relevant assessment must identify the property, its function and how the applicable rules treat it. Engineering should provide a clear equipment boundary and explain the interface without making the tax conclusion itself.

For example, an actuator operating an auxiliary mechanism is not necessarily classified in the same way as the primary energy-producing asset nearby. The drawing should distinguish those functions. A bill of materials and scope description can help the adviser understand whether items are separate property or part of a relevant assembly under the governing rules. The answer depends on the facts and requirements, not simply on their appearance within one supplier quotation.

Keep an equipment evidence package

A useful evidence package includes the quotation, technical description, drawings, selected model information and installation scope. It should identify what the equipment actually does and what is excluded. Generic brochures can provide context, but the offered configuration is more important than the broad capabilities of a product family. A supplier letter should state technical facts within the supplier’s knowledge rather than promise a customer’s tax result. If an adviser requests a clarification, answer the precise equipment question and retain the response with the reviewed configuration and current project scope. Preserve revisions when the design changes during procurement.

The package should also identify assumptions still awaiting confirmation. A preliminary concept may not yet establish the final ownership, installation location or equipment function. Those uncertainties can affect the assessment and should remain visible. This gives finance a realistic understanding of when a potential tax benefit can be evaluated with confidence rather than treating an early engineering sketch as a completed eligibility record.

Separate cost categories before applying any percentage

An equipment project can contain acquisition, installation, engineering, preliminary studies, training and other costs. The rules determine which amounts enter a relevant credit calculation; a percentage should not be applied casually to the total project budget. CRA's property guidance discusses capital-cost treatment and exclusions, which require project-specific review. [1]

For the engineering team, the practical step is to preserve the cost breakdown and purpose of each activity. Do not combine every invoice into one clean-technology total and expect an adviser to reconstruct the distinctions later. A well-organised budget supports both ordinary project control and tax analysis. It also prevents an apparently attractive credit estimate from being inflated by costs that have not been assessed as part of the eligible base.

Other assistance can affect the calculation

A project may be considering contributions, grants or other tax measures at the same time. Their interaction must be reviewed under the applicable rules. It is unsafe to add several headline percentages and call the sum a guaranteed discount. The relevant cost base, timing and treatment of assistance can differ, so a combined scenario needs an explicit financial method.

Keep a register of support requested, expected, awarded and received. Those are different statuses. Provide the register to the adviser and update it when an application changes. This helps avoid double counting and makes the cash-flow model more realistic. A potential credit can be economically valuable while still arriving at a different time from equipment payments, which matters to the owner's need for financing during delivery.

Timing deserves a project timeline

Engineering programmes contain several dates: contract award, progress payments, delivery, installation, testing and the start of intended use. Tax requirements can depend on particular events or conditions, so preserve the actual timeline rather than compressing it into a single project year. The adviser can identify which dates matter under the rules being applied.

Changes can also occur after the initial assessment. A delayed installation, revised equipment function or different ownership arrangement may require another review. Build that check into project control where the tax benefit is material to the investment. This is more effective than discovering at financial close that the facts no longer match the assumptions used in the original board paper.

Model the project before and after potential assistance

The base case should show the engineering investment without unconfirmed support. A separate scenario can show the effect of assistance if it is established. This makes the dependency visible: management can see whether the project remains worthwhile without it and how much uncertainty the proposed benefit introduces. The approach is particularly useful when eligibility review is still underway.

Use the same technical assumptions in both scenarios unless the funded project scope genuinely differs. Otherwise, the assisted case may appear better partly because it assumes different operating performance. Separate the equipment's value from the financing effect. A water-hydraulic project can have a sound business case through performance or reduced exposure even where this particular credit is unavailable; equally, a potential credit should not rescue machinery that does not meet the duty.

Do not confuse a purchase with experimental development

A team may investigate other support when the equipment does not fit a property-based credit. That does not make an ordinary purchase eligible for research assistance. NRC IRAP and SR&ED have their own purposes and requirements. [3] [4] The project must be described honestly, distinguishing application of established knowledge from work intended to resolve a genuine scientific or technological uncertainty.

If real development work exists, document it separately from routine procurement and installation. The presence of uncertainty about price, delivery or supplier selection is not itself evidence of technological advancement. This distinction helps the owner pursue appropriate support while preserving the credibility of the technical work. It also prevents the financial model from moving an unsupported assumption from one programme column to another.

Make the tax review a defined decision point

Assign responsibility for obtaining the project-specific assessment and identify what information is still needed. Record the conclusion, its assumptions and the conditions that would require reconsideration. The purchasing team should know whether the credit is confirmed for modelling purposes, still under review or excluded from the case. Ambiguous language such as likely covered can conceal a material financial dependency.

WaterQuest can provide accurate equipment information and help explain the hydraulic function. The taxpayer and its advisers determine the tax treatment. A responsible proposal makes that boundary clear while supporting the review with useful facts. The resulting decision is stronger: engineers can focus on machinery suitability, procurement can compare the full cost, and executives can consider any incentive without mistaking an environmental benefit for an automatic tax entitlement.

From understanding to equipment selection

Keep the equipment decision independent of an assumed credit

Build an unsubsidised cost comparison first, then use a separate scenario for any confirmed incentive. Identify the qualifying equipment and project costs through the appropriate current review rather than applying a percentage to the whole installation.

Connect the calculation to equipment
