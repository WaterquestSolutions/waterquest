Sizing Cylinders for Water Hydraulic Applications

Connect cylinder force to stroke, stability and load guidance.

Cylinder sizing starts with the load throughout its travel. Learn how bore and rod areas determine push and pull force, then include return pressure, stroke, stability and movement time in the selection.

Article 040Specification & Design10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Size for the hardest part of the stroke

A cylinder must deliver the required force where the mechanism needs it most. Map the load, linkage geometry, friction and acceleration through travel, then check extension and retraction areas at the available pressure difference. That connects water hydraulic cylinder selection to dependable movement, rather than a single force figure at a convenient position.

Describe both directions of movement and any holding duty. A gate may require more effort to unseat than to travel, while another mechanism may encounter its most difficult geometry near the end. Identify the governing load cases before choosing a bore or rod diameter.

Water hydraulics use the same pressure-and-area principles that make hydraulic actuation useful generally. [1] The selected cylinder must additionally be supported for the working water and operating conditions. The sizing process should therefore produce a mechanical and hydraulic requirement that can be matched to a suitable component, rather than assume that any pressure-rated oil cylinder can be reused.

Use a simple area calculation as a first screen

For an initial idealised calculation with one effective pressure difference, required area equals force divided by pressure. Suppose a hypothetical mechanism needs 40 kilonewtons and the preliminary pressure difference is 100 bar (1,450 psi). The resulting area is 0.004 square metre, corresponding to a circular bore of approximately 71.4 millimetres (2.81 in).

That is a calculated screening minimum, not a selected standard cylinder. It excludes the unequal-area effect of return pressure in a single-rod cylinder, friction and the engineering allowances required by the application. It also says nothing about rod stability, mountings or the required flow.

The value is useful because it shows the scale of the geometry needed. The next step is to examine available supported configurations and perform the complete force balance. Do not round the screening result into a purchase instruction without checking the other conditions that determine whether the actuator can perform the actual mechanism duty.

Include pressures on both piston faces

A double-acting single-rod cylinder has a full piston area at the cap end and a smaller annular area at the rod end. The net hydraulic force is the driving pressure contribution minus the opposing contribution, using the area on which each pressure acts. Return pressure therefore matters even when it is much lower than supply pressure.

Consider a hypothetical 80-millimetre bore and 40-millimetre rod. With 100 bar (1,450 psi) at the cap end and eight bar at the rod end, ideal extension force is about 47.25 kilonewtons. This is below the approximately 50.27 kilonewtons obtained by ignoring the opposing pressure.

The example remains ideal: friction and mechanical effects are excluded. Its purpose is to show why the pressures at the actuator belong in the calculation. A pump-outlet setting alone cannot establish usable cylinder force when the connecting circuit and opposite chamber create additional pressure conditions.

Check retraction separately from extension

The rod reduces the effective area available for retraction. Using the same hypothetical 80-millimetre bore and 40-millimetre rod, with 100 bar (1,450 psi) on the annular side and eight bar opposing at the cap end, ideal retraction force is approximately 33.68 kilonewtons.

That result is important if the original requirement was 40 kilonewtons in both directions. The geometry may exceed the preliminary extension requirement while failing the retraction screen before friction or allowances are included. Increasing rod diameter can improve some mechanical characteristics while further reducing the annular area.

The preferred geometry therefore depends on the complete duty. Do not select the rod only after finalising the bore, and do not assume equal push and pull capability. A clear force table for both directions helps the supplier and mechanism designer see which case governs and whether another geometry or architecture should be considered.

Convert movement time into flow demand

Ideal linear speed is delivered flow divided by the effective area receiving that flow. For a specified travel time, the required volume follows area multiplied by stroke. These relationships connect cylinder selection to the pump and valve arrangement.

For the illustrative 80-millimetre bore and 40-millimetre rod, a 500-millimetre stroke with eight litres per minute supplied to the active chamber takes approximately 18.85 seconds to extend and 14.14 seconds to retract ideally. The different times follow from the different chamber volumes. Leakage and control transitions are excluded.

A larger bore can provide more force at a given pressure but requires more flow for the same speed. The designer must revisit pump demand and valve capacity when the cylinder geometry changes. A force calculation completed in isolation can otherwise produce an actuator that meets the load requirement but cannot satisfy the process timing with the available supply.

Examine the mechanical advantage through the stroke

A cylinder often acts through a lever or linkage. The relationship between cylinder force and useful output changes with the angles and distances in that mechanism. Near certain positions, a small change in geometry can produce a large change in required actuator force.

The mechanical assessment should identify the load at the cylinder attachment for the full relevant travel. Include process forces and any externally applied loads that the application requires. A drawing showing the critical positions is often more informative to the supplier than a single mass value.

This is also where required travel is established. Cylinder stroke and useful mechanism movement are related through geometry, not necessarily equal. The selected installation should avoid relying on an assumed end position that the actual linkage cannot reach. Confirming the geometry early prevents a correctly sized hydraulic component from being paired with an unsuitable mechanical arrangement.

Assess rod stability and the supporting structure

A rod carrying compression needs a stability assessment appropriate to its length, guidance and end conditions. The cylinder's pressure capability does not establish its resistance to buckling in every mounting arrangement. The connected brackets, pins and structure also carry the actuator forces.

Side loading should be addressed through the mechanism and suitable guidance rather than assumed acceptable to the cylinder. Misalignment can affect both mechanical loading and the condition of dynamic seals. A larger bore does not solve an unsuitable load path and may increase the forces imposed on the supports.

These checks belong to the responsible mechanical and structural design. The equipment enquiry should provide the mounting arrangement and relevant geometry so the supplier can assess its component scope. Keeping the responsibilities explicit prevents a catalogue force rating from being mistaken for approval of the complete structure that the actuator will drive.

Plan how motion stops and how position is held

Moving mass carries energy that must be managed when motion stops. The required deceleration, end-of-stroke behaviour and any external stops should be considered with the mechanism. Pressure capacity alone does not establish a suitable stopping arrangement.

Holding is another distinct function. The required duration and acceptable movement depend on the application, while leakage and external loads affect behaviour. A cylinder's ability to generate sufficient force during travel does not automatically establish an acceptable hold or emergency response.

ISO 4413 provides general hydraulic-system safety context, but the application determines its specific protective requirements. [2] The designer must connect those requirements to the actual circuit and mechanism. A water-compatible actuator may be an appropriate component, while load control, position confirmation and recovery still require deliberate integration and validation.

Match the materials and seals to the selected duty

The final cylinder must be supported for the actual working water, temperature and environment. Wetted materials, dynamic seals and counterfaces all matter. An external stainless appearance or a suitable pressure rating does not establish every internal compatibility question.

Describe external exposure separately. A cylinder may use freshwater internally while its rod sees washdown, salt spray or contamination from the process. The design should address how that environment interacts with the moving interface and what maintenance requirements follow.

Purpose-designed water equipment provides a practical technology basis; the Water Hydraulics Company's pump information is one example of a defined water-service configuration. [3] It does not certify an arbitrary connected cylinder. Obtain the relevant actuator documentation and evidence for the proposed geometry, materials and duty rather than extending another component's suitability across the assembly.

Turn the calculation into an accepted selection

A useful cylinder schedule records the load basis, geometry, pressure assumptions, flow demand and mechanical interfaces. Identify which values are calculated, which are supplied ratings and which require confirmation. This allows procurement to compare complete offers and helps reviewers understand the governing case.

Acceptance should verify the relevant installed movement, timing and holding requirements under the agreed conditions. Retain the configuration and observations as a baseline. If the load, linkage or control arrangement later changes, the original sizing basis should be reviewed rather than assumed to remain valid.

The illustrative calculations in this article show why selection is iterative. An 80-millimetre bore can look suitable from an extension calculation, yet the same cylinder's retraction force, travel time or mounting condition can govern the decision. Each check contributes a different piece of evidence and should remain visible in the final recommendation.

A concise enquiry can accelerate that process. Provide the mechanism drawing, critical positions, required push and pull duties, travel and timing, available pressure and water conditions. This gives the supplier information needed to assess an actual solution instead of asking it to endorse a bore selected from an incomplete calculation.

Also identify whether movements can coincide with other actuators on the same HPU. A cylinder's individual flow requirement may be acceptable while the combined demand exceeds the supply arrangement. The final selection belongs to the complete operating sequence, so changes to one actuator should be checked against the rest of the circuit.

WaterQuest's opportunity is to deliver predictable water-powered motion in applications where removing oil has practical value. Sound cylinder sizing connects that benefit to pressure balance, mechanical geometry and the required timing. The result should be an actuator whose useful performance is demonstrated at the actual mechanism, not merely a large force number on a datasheet.

From understanding to equipment selection

Size flow after choosing effective area

Once force establishes a candidate bore and rod, calculate stroke volume and the flow needed for the required time in each direction. Check the return path, especially where annular and cap-end flows differ.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the cylinder force calculator to compare extension and retraction with opposing pressure and the required load. Use the system pressure-drop calculator to combine component losses, elevation and a design allowance.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Review bore, rod, stroke and mounting options together with the valve and return path. Request a configuration that covers the loaded motion in both directions.

Explore water hydraulic cylinders and water hydraulic control valves.

How should the cylinder selection shape the maintenance plan?

Record full-stroke time under the agreed load. A future change in cycle speed should prompt a check of valves, return losses and mechanical guidance as well as pump flow.

The force tool does not verify rod buckling, structural mountings or machine safety.
