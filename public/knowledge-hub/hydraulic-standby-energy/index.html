Performance Comparisons

Your Hydraulic Unit's Biggest Energy Bill May Be the Waiting

In this worked example standby uses half as much energy as movement. Cutting standby saves 2.6 times more than a 10 % gain in motion efficiency, a design opportunity for any new water HPU.

Written by WaterQuest Solutions · Updated 2 November 2026 · About the distributor

Use power when the process needs it

Begin an assessment by describing the service being delivered: gates positioned, tonnes handled, products processed or minutes of controlled movement. Then identify which equipment belongs inside the measurement boundary.

The US Department of Energy's pumping sourcebook advocates examining the pumping system as a whole. That principle is useful here, although its examples do not establish the performance of a particular water hydraulic power unit. The comparison must still be built around the selected machine and its actual operating cycle.

Separate instantaneous power from accumulated energy

Power is a rate. Energy records how long that rate persists. A ten-kilowatt event lasting one minute consumes about 0.167 kWh. A modest 0.1 kW (0.134 hp) standby load maintained for a full day consumes 2.4 kWh.

An illustrative system draws 4 kW (5.36 hp) during 500 annual operating hours and 0.3 kW (0.402 hp) during 3,500 standby hours. Its annual use is 2,000 plus 1,050, or 3,050 kWh. Cutting moving-state consumption by ten percent saves 200 kWh. Cutting standby consumption in half saves 525 kWh.

Keep a state table that distinguishes movement, holding, unloading, shutdown and restart. The hours must add up to the observation period.

Use hydraulic power to locate losses

At a hydraulic boundary, power in kilowatts is pressure difference in bar multiplied by flow in litres per minute, divided by 600. For example, 100 bar (1,450 psi) across a device at 12 L/min (3.17 US gpm) corresponds to 2 kW (2.68 hp). The pressure must be the difference relevant to that device, not whichever gauge value happens to be available. At a cylinder, both chamber pressures and the different chamber areas can matter.

Suppose a control valve drops 20 bar (290 psi) while passing 12 L/min (3.17 US gpm). Its hydraulic dissipation is approximately 0.4 kW (0.536 hp) at that point.

Construct a loss map rather than applying one catalogue efficiency to the whole installation. Motor and drive losses, pump losses, distribution losses and actuator losses occur at different boundaries.

Match supply behaviour to intermittent demand

A fixed-displacement pump running continuously can supply more flow than an intermittent actuator needs. The preferred strategy must satisfy the required response rather than simply minimize idle power on a test bench.

For a remote gate, waiting several seconds for pressure to become available may be acceptable during normal positioning but unacceptable during a specified emergency action.

Purpose-designed water pumps provide a component-level starting point for assessing a proposed drive strategy. The Water Hydraulics Company's P3 information describes an identified pump and its supported operating context; it does not establish the best control mode or annual energy figure for a complete gate installation. Request configuration-specific speed, inlet and operating limits, then check that the proposed stopping, restarting or speed-control sequence remains within those limits throughout the duty.

Treat holding and stored energy as separate duties

A stationary load does not necessarily require continuous hydraulic power, yet safe holding depends on the circuit and the mechanism.

An accumulator can supply short bursts of flow and change the pump's required operating pattern. It does not create free energy. The available volume between the permitted pressure limits must be sufficient for the defined action, and the stored-energy hazards must be managed independently of the energy-saving calculation.

Where pressure must be maintained, measure how often the supply replenishes it and investigate the reason.

Measure a representative range of operation

Collect electrical power alongside pressure, flow, temperature and the process state. Time alignment matters: a pressure spike and a power peak recorded on unrelated clocks cannot reliably explain each other.

A motor nameplate gives a rating, not a record of actual energy consumption. Similarly, estimated shaft load is not equivalent to measured electrical input.

Measure representative production rates, water temperatures and simultaneous demands.

Convert the technical result into a useful cost comparison

Apply the site's tariff to the measured load profile. An energy charge based on kWh and a demand charge based on the highest qualifying power interval answer different questions.

For an illustrative flat energy price of $0.15 per kWh, the earlier 3,050 kWh estimate represents $457.50 annually in energy charges alone.

Relate consumption to delivered output as well as annual hours. A year with lower electricity use may also have fewer movements or lower production.

Test improvements without losing the service requirement

Define acceptance criteria before changing the control strategy. Energy savings that compromise the asset's service duty are not an improvement.

Use a comparable baseline.

Water hydraulics deserve consideration when a compatible system meets the duty and removes an unwanted oil inventory from the selected circuit.
