What If the Water Freezes?

Choose a cold-weather strategy for the entire circuit.

Water expands when it freezes, putting trapped fluid and exposed components at risk. Learn how to plan protection for normal operation, standby and loss of power so cold-weather behaviour is addressed before installation.

Article 057Specification & Design10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Keep the required movement available through winter

Water-filled equipment exposed to freezing needs a deliberate protection plan. Ice can block passages and damage components, so assess the smallest lines and trapped volumes as well as the reservoir. Heating, insulation, drainage and monitoring should cover operation, standby and interruptions, with restart checks that help the operator return the system to service.

Cold-climate suitability is a property of the installation and operating plan, not a universal verdict on water hydraulics. A protected indoor circuit and an exposed remote valve block face different challenges. A seasonal asset that can be drained and taken out of service differs from an emergency actuator that must remain ready. Define the required availability and the site conditions first. Then evaluate the protection method, its dependencies and the evidence needed to show that it can support the intended service.

Find the vulnerable locations rather than using an average

The coldest part of the installation may be a small line, an inactive branch or a valve mounted away from the heated reservoir. Wind exposure, contact with cold structures and local shielding can create different thermal conditions across the circuit. A sensor near a heater may report a reassuring temperature while a remote section is cooling. Review the full fluid path and identify where water can remain during standby, including small cavities and instrument connections.

This is a physical-layout task as much as a calculation. Trace outdoor runs, enclosure penetrations, low points and components that cannot drain readily. Consider whether access for inspection remains practical during winter. The resulting exposure map gives the designer a basis for locating temperature measurements and protective equipment. It also helps the operator understand why one central reading may not represent the whole system. The aim is to protect the required function, which can be lost through one blocked branch even when most of the fluid remains liquid.

Distinguish insulation from heating

Insulation slows heat transfer; it does not generate heat. An insulated circuit can still approach ambient temperature during a sufficiently long shutdown. Its benefit depends on the initial condition, surrounding environment, geometry and time. Treating insulation as permanent freeze protection without considering duration can create false confidence. A thermal assessment should explain what the insulation achieves and which operating states require an additional heat source or another protective method.

Heating introduces its own requirements. The arrangement must be appropriate for the component, enclosure and environment, and it needs a way to confirm that the vulnerable locations remain protected. A heater's electrical rating alone does not establish the fluid temperature everywhere in the circuit. Control, sensing and failure response belong to the design. Excessive local heating can also create an unsuitable condition, so the objective is a supported temperature range rather than simply making one point as warm as possible.

Decide what must happen during a power interruption

If freeze protection depends on electrical power, evaluate loss of that power for the duration relevant to the site. The machinery may also depend on electricity for movement, alarms and communications. These dependencies should be considered together. A system that safely stops production may still need preservation to avoid damage while stopped. An emergency function may require a different arrangement because it must remain available despite the same interruption that threatens the heating system.

Possible responses depend on the application and approved design. They may include a protected location, monitored backup arrangements or a controlled drain-down for equipment that can be removed from service. Do not assume that an alarm alone solves the problem if nobody can respond before vulnerable sections cool. Establish who receives the indication, what action is authorised and how long the protection remains effective. The important outcome is a credible sequence from the initiating event to a maintained safe condition or an orderly withdrawal from service.

Drain-down is a method that must be demonstrated

Removing water can be an appropriate preservation approach for some out-of-service equipment, but a drain valve at the reservoir does not prove that the complete circuit empties. Check trapped volumes, low points, valve cavities, hoses and actuator chambers. The method must suit the installed geometry and the manufacturer's instructions. If a component retains water internally, the preservation plan needs to address it rather than assume that gravity reaches every passage.

A practical procedure should identify the operating state, isolation requirements, connections and verification needed. It should also explain return to service, including refilling and any necessary checks. Frequent drain-down may create additional maintenance work and opportunities for contamination, so include those consequences in the choice of protection method. For a seasonal asset, a well-designed procedure may be reasonable. For equipment required without warning, the time and actions needed to refill may make the same approach unsuitable. Availability requirements determine whether the method fits.

Antifreeze changes the working fluid

Adding an antifreeze constituent is not merely a temperature adjustment. It changes fluid properties and can affect seals, polymers, lubrication behaviour, pressure losses and environmental statements. The concentration, formulation and condition matter. A mixture familiar from another type of equipment should not be introduced into a water-hydraulic circuit without support for the actual components. The relevant question is whether the complete offered configuration is approved by its manufacturer for the proposed fluid and operating envelope.

The Water Hydraulics Company's pump information directs users to configuration-specific advice for lower-temperature and alternative-fluid conditions. [2] That is a reason to obtain current written instructions, not a licence to select a mixture independently from a general web statement. The owner should understand any effect on filtration, maintenance, disposal and performance. If the project objective is described as pure-water operation, a changed formulation also needs to be reflected accurately in the specification and communications. The operating fluid should always match the fluid used in the technical assessment.

Include cold starts and changing viscosity

The permitted temperature range affects more than whether the water is frozen. Fluid properties vary with temperature, which can influence pressure losses, leakage and pump performance. Seals and other components also have their own operating limits. A system that is free of ice may still be outside a component's supported range. Water-property references such as IAPWS provide scientific data within stated conditions, while the equipment manufacturer's instructions establish the supported application of the selected assembly. [1]

Define the start-up conditions that must be achieved before movement is permitted. A warm reservoir does not necessarily prove that remote components have reached the required state. The control arrangement may need to distinguish ready, warming and unavailable conditions. Avoid forcing operation as a way to generate heat in an unverified cold circuit. The correct sequence should be established during design and commissioning, with enough indication for operators to understand why the system is or is not ready for the demanded function.

Treat suspected freezing as an abnormal event

An actuator that will not move in cold conditions should not be forced by increasing pressure or repeatedly starting the pump. A blockage or damaged component may be present, and the resulting pressure state may not be obvious. Follow the site's authorised isolation and investigation procedure. CCOHS guidance explains the importance of controlling hazardous energy, including stored and residual energy, rather than assuming that stopping a machine removes every hazard. [3]

Thawing does not by itself establish that the pressure-containing parts remain sound. The event may require inspection, component assessment and a controlled return-to-service plan appropriate to the equipment. Preserve information about temperatures, alarms and attempted operation so that the cause can be understood. The corrective action should address both the immediate condition and the failure of the protection strategy. Simply restoring heat without investigating why protection was lost can leave the same asset vulnerable during the next interruption.

Verify the protection strategy under relevant conditions

Commissioning should establish that temperature sensing, heating controls and relevant alarms function as intended. The verification plan should address the vulnerable locations identified in the design, not only the easiest sensor to access. Where the protection depends on a response sequence, confirm that the responsible people understand it and that the required equipment is available. Testing must follow an approved plan and should not deliberately expose the system to damaging conditions merely to demonstrate a limit.

Retain the design assumptions and maintenance requirements with the asset records. Insulation can be damaged, enclosure doors can be left open and heating equipment can fail. Changes to pipe routing or duty can create a new cold location. Periodic review should focus on these practical ways the installed protection can diverge from the original design. The value of a thermal strategy lies in keeping it effective through ownership, including ordinary repairs and modifications, rather than treating it as a one-time calculation completed before the first winter.

Evaluate winter protection as part of the ownership case

Include the capital, energy and maintenance consequences of freeze protection when comparing technologies. A water circuit that needs continuous winter heating should not be described using an energy estimate that excludes that necessary function. A protected installation may have modest additional requirements, while an exposed remote application may be more demanding. Make the comparison for the actual location and required availability. This allows management to see whether the oil-removal benefit justifies the complete supported arrangement.

WaterQuest can help define the equipment requirements and coordinate the questions that the thermal and site design must answer. The credible message is that water hydraulics can be considered in cold climates when temperature protection is designed, monitored and maintained for the duty. It is neither helpful to ignore freezing nor to assume it rules out every application. A clear plan gives engineers a design boundary, operators an understandable readiness condition and owners a realistic view of the service they are buying.

From understanding to equipment selection

Assess freezing in shutdown as well as operation

A system protected while running may still contain vulnerable trapped water when power is lost. Identify low points, closed valves and exposed instruments, then choose a supported protection or drain-down strategy.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the energy savings calculator to compare annual electricity and demand costs using measured or explicitly assumed input power. Use the lifecycle cost calculator to compare capital, recurring service, replacement and residual costs over the same study period.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Discuss enclosure, water management and preservation with the power-unit and cylinder suppliers for the stated cold-weather duty. Include all exposed and trapped volumes in the review.
