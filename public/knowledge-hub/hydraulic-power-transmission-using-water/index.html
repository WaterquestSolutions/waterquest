Hydraulic Power Transmission Using Water

Follow the energy from the drive to useful hydraulic work.

Water hydraulics carry power from a pump to a cylinder or motor. Follow the path from electrical or mechanical input to useful movement, and see how pressure, flow and losses determine the equipment required.

Article 017Start Here10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Follow the power from the drive to the load

A motor or other prime mover supplies the energy; the pump transfers it to water; valves direct the flow; and an actuator produces force and movement. Water carries that power through the circuit. Following each stage shows where useful work is delivered and where losses occur, helping you select equipment that serves the required task.

Thinking in boundaries makes the arrangement easier to evaluate. At the electrical connection, measure electrical input. At the pump shaft, consider torque and rotational speed. At a hydraulic connection, consider pressure difference and flow. At the load, consider force and velocity, or torque and angular velocity. Each quantity describes the same energy transfer from a different viewpoint, with losses between boundaries. Mixing measurements from different locations can produce a misleading efficiency or an apparently impossible power balance.

Pascal's principle explains transmission of pressure through a confined fluid under appropriate conditions. It establishes the basis of hydraulic force multiplication, not a source of additional energy. A larger piston can provide greater force at the same pressure, but it requires more fluid volume to move the same distance. That exchange between force and travel is fundamental to understanding hydraulic machinery. [1]

Connect pressure and flow to the required movement

The convenient hydraulic power relationship is P = Δp × Q / 600 when P is in kilowatts, pressure difference is in bar, and flow is in litres per minute. An illustrative 120 bar (1,740 psi) pressure difference with 15 L/min (3.96 US gpm) represents 3 kW (4.02 hp). A different combination, such as 60 bar (870 psi) and 30 L/min (7.93 US gpm), also represents 3 kW (4.02 hp). Those two operating points do not necessarily perform the same task with the same actuator.

Actuator geometry connects the hydraulic operating point to motion. Ignoring friction and opposing pressure for a simple illustration, a piston area of 0.002 m² (0.0215 ft²) at 100 bar (1,450 psi) develops 20,000 N (4,496 lbf). Flow of 12 L/min (3.17 US gpm) is 0.0002 m³/s (0.00706 ft³/s), giving a piston velocity of 0.1 m/s (0.328 ft/s). Multiplying force by velocity gives 2,000 W (2.68 hp), which agrees with 100 × 12 / 600 = 2 kW (2.68 hp). This independent mechanical check helps reveal unit mistakes.

A real double-acting cylinder needs both chamber pressures included, and its rod reduces the effective area on one side. Friction, leakage and mechanical leverage further modify the useful output. Use the simplified calculation to understand the relationship, then develop the detailed model for the actual mechanism. A single pump-pressure value cannot establish the net force available throughout a loaded stroke.

Understand what the pump supplies

A positive-displacement pump delivers a theoretical volume per revolution. Its actual delivery depends on speed, internal leakage and the operating condition. The load and the circuit determine the pressure needed to pass the flow or move the actuator. If the intended outlet path becomes blocked, a pressure-protection function is necessary; the pump should not be treated as a device that safely selects the required pressure by itself.

This distinction helps interpret a stall. A cylinder that stops moving may have reached a mechanical obstruction, a force limit, a control limit or an unavailable flow path. Increasing pump capacity without understanding the cause can add heat or pressure exposure without restoring useful motion. Trace the command, the available flow, the chamber pressures and the mechanical load before changing the supply design.

Purpose-designed water pumps provide the physical machinery for this transmission process. Manufacturer information for the Janus P3 is one example of equipment designed for water service. It should be read for the selected configuration and supported operating envelope, not generalized into a claim that any positive-displacement oil pump can deliver the same service with water. [4]

Account for pressure changes along the network

Pressure at the pump outlet is not automatically pressure at the actuator. Flow through pipework, fittings, filters and control valves produces pressure differences. The distribution loss generally changes with flow, fluid condition and geometry. A branch that behaves well during slow positioning may show a larger loss during rapid travel. Assess the operating range instead of assigning one unexplained percentage to every line.

The return path also affects available force. On a cylinder, return-side back pressure acts against the desired movement through the relevant chamber area. A restrictive return filter, a long small-bore line or a shared return connection can therefore change both force and behaviour. Place measurement points so the engineer can distinguish supply limitation from return restriction; one gauge at the power unit leaves much of that story invisible.

Physical layout deserves early attention. Shorter routes, appropriate line sizes and fewer unnecessary restrictions can reduce distribution losses, but larger lines add volume, cost and installation space. Supports, accessibility and draining arrangements matter as well. The preferred pipe size is a system decision that balances operating performance with the practical requirements of installing and maintaining the network.

Explain why a liquid circuit still has compliance

Water is much less compressible than a gas under ordinary hydraulic conditions, yet an installed hydraulic circuit is not perfectly rigid. The water compresses to a finite extent, hoses expand, structures deflect and trapped gas can contribute substantial compliance. The observed response belongs to the complete arrangement. Calling water incompressible is a useful elementary approximation, but it can conceal important behaviour in a long or rapidly switched circuit.

During a command change, some supplied volume may initially change pressure and elastic deformation rather than immediately producing the expected actuator travel. This can affect position response and the interpretation of leakage tests. A pressure decline after stopping the pump does not have one universal cause; temperature, elastic relaxation, gas and leakage may need to be distinguished. Establish a controlled test condition before drawing a maintenance conclusion.

Rapid changes in flow also involve momentum. Abrupt valve action or stopping a moving load can create transient pressures that a slow gauge fails to capture. Component pressure limits and suitable protection must cover the relevant events. Predicting and measuring those events is separate from the steady-state power calculation, even though both are necessary to understand the same installation.

Manage branches that share a supply

A central power unit can serve several actuators, but total installed cylinder capacity is not the same as simultaneous flow demand. Build an operating sequence that identifies which branches may move together, which take priority and which can wait. An honest simultaneity assumption can reduce oversizing. An optimistic assumption that operations cannot enforce can leave the plant short of flow at the moment it matters.

Branches with different loads present another challenge. A common pressure supply may be set by the highest-pressure duty while a lower-pressure branch dissipates the excess through its controls. That arrangement may be justified by simplicity or intermittent use, but the loss should be visible. Alternative zoning, local units or different control strategies can then be evaluated against the actual frequency and duration of the mismatch.

Define how one branch's fault affects the others. An open return path, an unexpected demand or an isolated section can change network behaviour. Functional isolation, monitoring and priorities should be designed around the required service continuity. This is where a hydraulic architecture becomes an operating system rather than merely a group of components connected to a pump.

Compare central and local power arrangements

A central HPU may consolidate servicing, spare parts and instrumentation. It can also place the main equipment in a protected location. The trade-off is a larger distribution network with more routes and interfaces to inspect. A local unit shortens those hydraulic connections but creates additional electrical, environmental and maintenance responsibilities at each location. Neither architecture is inherently superior without the site layout and duty.

Consider a facility with several widely separated gates. The rarely operated gates may not justify continuously available flow across a long network, while a group of frequently operated mechanisms might benefit from shared capacity. Access restrictions can reverse an apparently attractive choice: a compact local unit positioned where service staff cannot safely reach it may create more difficulty than the pipework it eliminates.

The DOE pumping sourcebook provides a useful system-assessment perspective when examining distribution and control losses. Its guidance does not replace the water-component supplier's operating requirements or the project-specific comparison between central and local architectures. Use it to frame the questions, then quantify the options using consistent boundaries and a credible operating schedule. [2]

Verify useful work and the response to interruption

Commissioning should connect the measurements back to the task. Verify loaded movement, speed, holding behaviour, temperature and the interactions between branches. Record pressures at meaningful locations and retain the control settings used during the test. A pump running smoothly with its outlet unloaded proves much less than an installed mechanism completing its required sequence under representative conditions.

Loss of electrical supply, communication or pilot pressure can interrupt the chain at different points. The intended response might be to hold, close, open or move to another defined position, depending on the hazard assessment. Water as the medium does not establish that response. ISO 4413's public scope recognizes hydraulic systems as a design and safety discipline; the full applicable requirements and approved project design govern the implementation. [3]

The value of water hydraulic transmission is the opportunity to deliver hydraulic force and control while removing oil from a defined working circuit. A strong proposal explains the energy path, the distribution arrangement and the verified mechanical result. It gives an engineer enough information to assess performance and gives an owner a clear account of what will move, how it will be supplied and what happens when normal operation is interrupted.

From understanding to equipment selection

Keep power and energy distinct

Power describes the rate of useful work during movement; energy includes how long each operating state lasts. Calculate the required pressure and flow at the load, then include losses and operating hours when selecting the drive.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the hydraulic power calculator to translate pressure and flow into hydraulic output and estimated electrical input. Use the system pressure-drop calculator to combine component losses, elevation and a design allowance.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Compare the power-unit arrangement with the actuator duty and control sequence. Send the load, motion and water specification together so the components can be assessed as one circuit.

Explore water hydraulic power units and water hydraulic cylinders.

Which operating measurements make future troubleshooting easier?

Record both peak demand and accumulated energy. A unit that meets peak output may still have avoidable annual cost if it runs continuously through long idle periods.

Use the calculated duty as an enquiry input; efficiency assumptions are not verified equipment ratings.
