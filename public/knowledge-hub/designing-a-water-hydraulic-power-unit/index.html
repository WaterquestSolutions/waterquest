Designing a Water Hydraulic Power Unit

Bring flow, protection and controls together around the load.

A well-matched power unit gives your actuators the flow and pressure they need, when they need it. Learn how to size the pump, reservoir, filtration and controls around the machine’s complete operating sequence.

Article 038Specification & Design9 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Supply the movements your machine actually makes

A water hydraulic power unit brings the pump, drive, reservoir, valves and controls together to serve your actuators. Its job is to provide the required flow and pressure through movement, holding and standby. Build a schedule of those demands first, then select water-service components and controls that meet the sequence without unnecessary pumping between productive movements.

Start with the demand schedule of the equipment it serves. Identify which actuators move, the loads they encounter, the required speed and the duration of each state. Include simultaneous movements and long waiting periods. These conditions determine different parts of the design, so one maximum pressure-and-flow pair is rarely a complete specification.

The result should be a package whose operation is clear through the complete cycle. WaterQuest can connect the benefit of removing hydraulic oil to the pressure, flow, water management and service features the machine needs. Size and drive selection follow from those requirements.

Build a demand schedule before adding flows

Several actuators do not necessarily require their maximum flows at the same time. Adding all maxima can oversize the supply where the operating sequence prevents coincidence. Assuming they never coincide can undersize it if the controls or process allow simultaneous demand.

List each operating state and the functions active during it. Record their flow and load-pressure requirements, including movements that can occur together. Where the design relies on a sequence, identify how the controls enforce it so the supply matches the machine’s permitted operation.

Consider a hypothetical package with two movements requiring four and six litres per minute. If they can occur together, the simultaneous flow demand is ten litres per minute before relevant losses and allowances. If an accepted sequence permits only one at a time, the governing movement demand may be six. These are teaching values, not a recommended package size.

Translate useful demand into pump and drive requirements

Pressure and flow describe hydraulic power at a defined boundary. In commonly used units, pressure difference in bar multiplied by flow in litres per minute and divided by 600 gives kilowatts. An illustrative 100 bar (1,450 psi) difference at ten litres per minute represents about 1.67 kilowatts (2.24 hp) of hydraulic output.

The electrical input will be higher because the pump, motor, drive and any included auxiliaries have losses. Do not select a motor by treating the ideal hydraulic output as the required electrical rating. Use supported equipment performance and the relevant operating conditions for the actual package.

Check starting and intermittent operation against the chosen pump and drive data. Add internal leakage and supporting flows to the calculated actuator demand, and record the allowances used. This makes the selected capacity clear and provides a basis for checking any future actuator additions.

Design the inlet as carefully as the pressure outlet

The pump depends on suitable conditions at its inlet. Source pressure, elevation, connecting losses and temperature can influence the condition during operation. A larger drive cannot compensate for an inlet arrangement outside the selected pump's requirements.

The Water Hydraulics Company's P3 information distinguishes supporting conditions for identified configurations. [1] Use current documentation for the ordered equipment and check the actual supply path under the governing demand. A favourable static reading upstream does not necessarily establish what the pump receives while delivering flow.

Include casing and drain requirements in the layout where applicable. Combining lines for convenience can change back pressure or fluid routing. The design should show each required connection and its accepted condition, allowing installation and commissioning teams to verify the arrangement without guessing which small auxiliary line can be treated as interchangeable with another.

Choose the circuit's water balance deliberately

Determine whether the working water recirculates or is discharged after performing its function. That choice affects supply, inventory, treatment and operating cost. The design should identify make-up needs, expected losses and any intentional discharge rather than using the word closed as a substitute for a flow balance.

For recirculation, the reservoir or other supply arrangement must support the actual volume changes. A single-rod cylinder displaces different volumes on its two sides, and simultaneous functions can alter the water held outside the reservoir. The inventory assessment should reflect those movements.

Size the reservoir from the water balance, temperature behaviour and equipment requirements. Include the volume moving into and out of actuators and the water needed for reliable supply. This gives the installation a tank suited to its own operating cycle.

Provide a route for heat to leave

Losses in the pump, controls and other components become heat within the relevant system boundary. A fluid that transports heat effectively still needs somewhere to reject it. The package should be assessed for the intended duty and surrounding environment, including extended operation where relevant.

Distinguish peak input power from average heat load over the cycle. An intermittent movement and a continuous bypass condition can create very different thermal behaviour. The demand schedule developed earlier helps determine which states govern the assessment.

DOE's pumping guidance encourages system-level consideration of operating conditions and losses. [2] Apply that principle by identifying where energy is dissipated and how the installation reaches an acceptable thermal condition. Cooling, enclosure ventilation or another supported arrangement should be selected from that assessment, rather than added only after the first long run reveals excessive temperature.

Assign each valve and protective function a purpose

Directional control, speed control, pressure limitation and load management are different functions. Some assemblies combine them, but the design should still show what each function does in each relevant state. A valve centre position should not be assumed to provide every required holding and service-isolation function.

Identify pressure regions that can become isolated from the normal supply protection path. External loads or temperature changes can affect those regions independently of the pump. The responsible designer should establish appropriate protection and the means of returning the equipment to a controlled service state.

ISO 4413 provides a general framework for hydraulic-system safety considerations. [3] The application determines the specific requirements, including any safety-related function. A water-compatible valve or accumulator can be part of the architecture, but its presence alone does not establish the required machine response after a defined failure.

Make contamination control part of the flow design

Filtration must protect components while preserving their required supply conditions. Element performance, housing materials and location all matter. A restrictive element placed without regard to the inlet arrangement can create a new operating problem while attempting to improve cleanliness.

Parker's filtration handbook provides context for filter performance and contamination sources. [4] The actual water HPU should use the requirements of its selected components and a supported arrangement. A generic micron label does not fully describe capture performance or assembly compatibility.

Include clean filling, sampling and service access in the package design. These provisions help the owner maintain the accepted condition without improvised handling. The best filtration specification has limited value if routine maintenance makes it difficult to install the correct element or exposes the opened circuit to avoidable contamination.

Give operators measurements they can act on

Choose instrumentation around operating questions. Inlet condition, temperature, filter condition, water balance and actuator completion may reveal different problems. Pump outlet pressure alone cannot explain the complete HPU or confirm that a remote mechanism reached its required state.

Place measurements where they represent the relevant condition and label them clearly. Associate trends with operating states so normal changes in load or command are not mistaken for deterioration. A small number of reliable observations can provide more value than a crowded display with ambiguous numbers.

Every alarm needs a defined response and owner. Advisory monitoring should also remain distinct from validated protective functions. The operator should understand whether an indication is information for maintenance or part of an engineered action required to protect the machine or process. That distinction belongs in both controls documentation and training.

Arrange the package for its service life

The physical layout should allow inspection, identification and replacement of the parts likely to need attention. Filter access, coupling access and component removal routes can matter more over time than a small reduction in skid footprint. Review the assembled package with the people who will maintain it.

Commissioning should verify the relevant supply, motion and thermal conditions under the agreed duty. Retain the configuration and accepted observations as a baseline. Later modifications should be assessed against that record so the HPU does not gradually depart from the arrangement its performance evidence describes.

For procurement, state the package interfaces explicitly. Electrical supply, remote actuators, water source and control responsibilities may belong to different parties. An interface schedule should identify who confirms each condition and how it will be checked. This closes gaps that can otherwise appear when individually suitable components are assembled by several contributors.

A factory test and an installed acceptance test establish different boundaries. The factory can verify the supplied package under its test conditions, while site checks confirm the actual water source, remote connections and mechanism. State which evidence covers each interface so that a successful package test is not mistaken for verification of equipment that was never part of that test.

A useful HPU proposal explains what each part does for the operator: delivering pressure and flow, managing water and heat, showing equipment condition and providing access for service. These features make oil removal part of a complete, maintainable supply system.

Size a power unit around simultaneous demand

List which actuators move together and for how long. Add concurrent flows, assess the governing pressure and account for return flow; summing every actuator's peak demand can oversize a unit that operates sequentially.
