Performance Comparisons

Efficiency in Purpose-Designed Water Hydraulic Equipment

See how purpose-built components make water hydraulics work.

Efficiency comes from how the equipment works together. Learn how purpose-designed pumps, sliding surfaces, clearances and controls manage water’s low viscosity, and how to assess performance over the complete operating cycle.

Article 055Performance Comparisons10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Build efficiency through the whole energy path

An efficient water hydraulic installation matches pump flow to useful demand, limits unnecessary pressure loss and manages leakage and friction inside the components. Purpose-built surfaces and clearances support water service; the drive and controls determine what happens between movements. Review all of those elements against your operating cycle to identify and verify worthwhile energy improvements.

Water's low viscosity can reduce some forms of resistance while making leakage control more demanding. That is an engineering tradeoff rather than a verdict for or against the medium. A design intended for water can address the tradeoff through geometry, material pairing and supported operating conditions. Simply substituting water into equipment intended for another fluid does not preserve the original balance. The useful question is how the selected assembly delivers the required output and where the remaining input energy goes.

Identify what counts as useful output

A pump supplies hydraulic power, while an actuator delivers mechanical work. These are different boundaries. Hydraulic power can be calculated from pressure difference and flow; useful mechanical power depends on the force and speed at the driven mechanism. Losses between the pump and load include piping, valves, leakage and friction. A pump that performs well on a test stand cannot establish the efficiency of the complete machine without those additional effects being considered.

For an owner, the ultimate output may be a completed valve movement or a controlled process cycle rather than continuous shaft power. In that case, energy per successful cycle and annual consumption can be more informative than an instantaneous efficiency. Define the required load and motion before comparing alternatives. A system that uses less energy because it moves more slowly or cannot complete the same duty is not an equivalent comparison. Performance and energy must be reported together so that a favourable number has a meaningful operational interpretation.

Understand the cost of internal leakage

Internal leakage allows some flow to move through paths that do not contribute to the intended output. In a pump, it can reduce delivered flow relative to the theoretical displacement and speed. In a valve or actuator, it can affect holding or require replenishment. Leakage depends on design and operating conditions, including pressure difference, temperature and wear. It should be assessed for the selected component rather than assumed from the fluid name alone.

A small flow can have a measurable power consequence. As an independently calculated example, 0.5 litres per minute (0.132 US gpm) crossing a 100 bar (1,450 psi) pressure difference corresponds to about 0.083 kilowatts (0.111 hp) of hydraulic power. This uses pressure in bar times flow in litres per minute divided by 600. It is an illustrative loss calculation, not a leakage rating for any product. Its significance depends on duration. A brief event and a continuous loss can have very different annual consequences even when their instantaneous power is the same.

Balance leakage reduction against friction

Reducing a clearance can limit leakage, but a moving interface must still accommodate manufacturing variation, temperature and operating loads. Excessive contact or inadequate support can increase friction and wear. The goal is not simply the smallest possible gap. It is a stable design that controls leakage while allowing the intended motion across the supported envelope. This is why component geometry and material selection cannot be separated into independent purchasing decisions.

Engineered polymer elements can contribute to suitable sliding interfaces, but their behaviour depends on grade, counterface and conditions. Victrex's tribology information distinguishes formulation choices and the factors affecting friction and wear. [2] A low-friction result from one material test does not establish a pump's overall efficiency. The assembled component still contains multiple losses and operates under a specific load history. Ask how the material choice supports the design and what component-level evidence is available for the proposed duty.

Trace pressure losses through the circuit

Pressure difference across a flowing restriction represents hydraulic power that is not delivered to the downstream load. Some restrictions are necessary for control, but their cost should be understood. A circuit with a well-selected pump can still dissipate substantial energy through an unsuitable valve arrangement or unnecessarily restrictive piping. Review both supply and return paths. Return pressure can reduce useful actuator force and change the pressure required at the supply, so it belongs in the energy assessment.

An illustrative flow of 8 litres per minute (2.11 US gpm) through a 15 bar (218 psi) pressure drop dissipates 0.20 kilowatts (0.268 hp). The calculation does not imply that every such loss is avoidable or unacceptable. A control function may require a pressure margin, and a larger component can introduce cost or control tradeoffs. The engineering task is to identify losses that serve a necessary function and losses caused by poor matching. This makes improvement targeted rather than treating all pressure drop as waste that can be removed without affecting behaviour.

Match pump operation to the demand pattern

A pump selected for the largest instantaneous flow may spend much of its life serving a smaller demand or no demand at all. What happens during that time matters. Continuous circulation through a relief or bypass path can consume energy without productive motion. Starting, stopping, unloading, variable operation or storage may offer alternatives, depending on the equipment and duty. Each option needs a supported design and an assessment of response, reliability and maintenance, not simply an assumed energy benefit.

The Water Hydraulics Company's pump information provides configuration-specific performance context, while the complete installation determines how that equipment is used. [1] Request data at relevant pressure, speed and temperature conditions. Do not select from a maximum-flow point if the machine mainly operates elsewhere. A smaller pump with an appropriate architecture may serve an intermittent duty well; another duty may need continuous capacity. The preferred answer follows the demand schedule and the required response rather than a general rule that bigger equipment provides a safer margin.

Count idle energy separately from active efficiency

An efficient active movement can be surrounded by long periods of avoidable consumption. Consider an illustrative eight-hour shift with 15 minutes of movement and the remaining time in standby. The standby period lasts 31 times as long as the movement period. This arithmetic does not determine the energy result because the powers differ, but it shows why a small standby load can matter. Measuring only the active stroke may miss the dominant opportunity in an intermittent application.

Define operating states and measure or estimate each state's power and duration. Include control equipment, cooling, heaters or water-management auxiliaries where they belong within the comparison boundary. If winter protection is necessary, do not omit it from a claim about annual installed energy. The DOE's pumping-system guidance supports examining the operating system rather than relying on a single component number. [3] The result should explain where energy is used across the calendar and which changes would actually affect the owner's electricity consumption.

Read efficiency curves as conditional information

A curve applies to the tested configuration and conditions. Pressure, speed, temperature and fluid influence performance. Check whether the reported quantity is volumetric efficiency, mechanical efficiency, overall pump efficiency or electrical-to-hydraulic efficiency. These terms have different boundaries and cannot be compared interchangeably. If a supplier provides a percentage without identifying the inputs and outputs, ask for the definition before using it in a business case or tender evaluation.

Also check whether the proposed operating point lies within the available data. Interpolation within an appropriate range and extrapolation beyond tested conditions carry different levels of confidence. A curve for one fluid or seal option may not represent another configuration. Where the proposed duty is important and data are limited, an agreed test may be the right next step. The objective is not to demand impossible certainty, but to prevent a useful catalogue measurement from being promoted into a guarantee that it was never intended to provide.

Measure with enough accuracy to support the conclusion

Efficiency combines measurements, so uncertainty in pressure, flow, speed, torque or electrical power affects the result. Instrument location matters as well. A pressure taken upstream of a restrictive valve does not represent the actuator inlet. A flow reading that includes a bypass may not represent useful delivery. Define the measurement points on a schematic and record the operating condition. This makes the calculation reproducible and helps explain disagreement between a bench test and an installed measurement.

Small claimed improvements deserve particular care. If the difference between two alternatives is comparable to measurement uncertainty or normal duty variation, presenting it as a definitive advantage is misleading. Repeatability under representative conditions may be more informative than extra decimal places. Report the observed result with an appropriate qualification and explain any excluded auxiliaries. Engineers and procurement managers can then distinguish a demonstrated improvement from a promising prediction that still needs confirmation at the site.

Combine energy performance with the actual application value

Energy is one part of the ownership case. Water hydraulics may also address an oil-exposure problem that is important to the plant even where energy consumption is similar to an alternative. Conversely, an energy benefit should not hide an unsupported service arrangement or difficult maintenance requirement. Compare the complete duty, installed scope and operating burden. This allows decision-makers to see which benefits are established, which are conditional and which are not central to the application.

WaterQuest's strongest efficiency argument shows a coherent energy path: an appropriate pump, compatible moving interfaces, sensible control losses and an operating strategy matched to demand. It then verifies useful output and input under stated conditions. That is more convincing than claiming that water automatically makes machinery efficient. For the owner, the result is a system whose performance can be understood and improved. For the technology, it is a credible case that purpose-designed equipment can provide useful hydraulic service while removing mineral oil from the selected working circuit.

From understanding to equipment selection

Locate the losses behind the efficiency claim

Separate leakage, mechanical friction, line losses and control losses when evaluating a water pump circuit. Equipment designed for water can address these mechanisms, but an additive-free fluid does not itself establish total system efficiency.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the pump sizing calculator to estimate displacement, drive torque and shaft power from your flow, pressure and efficiency assumptions. Use the hydraulic efficiency calculator to separate volumetric, mechanical and overall efficiency using measured operating data.
