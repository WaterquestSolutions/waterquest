Selecting Stainless Steel for Water Hydraulic Systems

Match the stainless grade to the actual water and component.

“Stainless steel” covers materials with different strengths and corrosion resistance. Learn how water chemistry, temperature, fabrication and component function guide the grade selection for a water hydraulic system.

Article 009Components & Materials10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Choose stainless steel for the water and the location

A suitable stainless grade can combine structural strength with corrosion resistance in water hydraulic equipment. Freshwater circuits, seawater service and exterior splash zones create different selection conditions. Review chemistry, temperature, deposits and material contacts together so the alloy supports the component’s intended function through its operating life.

Start by describing where the metal will be used. Is it a pressure-containing body, a moving counterface, a fastener or a support? Will it contact the working water continuously, experience occasional drainage, or remain outside the circuit? What temperature range, cleaning fluids and deposits are credible? The answers distinguish a material-selection problem from a surface-finishing, fabrication or maintenance problem.

This is more useful than asking whether one alloy is universally better than another. A selection must satisfy the component's full duty, and unnecessary upgrading can carry cost without resolving an unsuitable joint geometry or an undocumented internal part. A disciplined specification identifies the exposure, chooses a supported material and preserves the conditions on which that selection depends.

Know what a grade designation does and does not tell you

A stainless grade identifies a defined material composition and related requirements under an applicable specification. The broad families include austenitic, ferritic, martensitic and duplex steels. Their properties are not interchangeable, and even within a family different grades address different service needs. Outokumpu's overview is a useful starting point for understanding these families rather than treating all stainless products as the same material. [3]

For purchasing, a familiar designation is only the beginning. The order should also identify the relevant product specification, form and condition where those matter. Plate, bar, castings and finished components may require different acceptance information. A drawing that says only “stainless” cannot reliably establish whether the delivered part matches the designer's intent, especially when an assembly contains several internal materials.

The specification should be precise without inventing requirements that do not improve the application. Ask which material records the equipment supplier normally provides and which additional verification is justified by the consequence of failure. The objective is traceability to the approved design, not collecting certificates whose meaning nobody on the project has defined. A clear material schedule is often more useful than a large unstructured document package.

Separate corrosion resistance from mechanical capacity

Corrosion resistance describes how a material behaves in an environment. Mechanical design addresses stresses, deformation, fatigue and other load-related conditions. A component needs both assessments. Selecting a more corrosion-resistant alloy does not automatically make an existing geometry suitable for a higher pressure. Equally, satisfying a pressure calculation does not establish acceptable behaviour in the intended water chemistry.

Consider a fitting connected to a vibrating machine. Its material may be appropriate for the fluid, while its installation introduces an unacceptable mechanical load through the pipework. Changing the grade alone would not resolve that problem. The connection geometry, support and pressure duty must be reviewed together. This separation helps an investigation avoid blaming corrosion for a failure driven primarily by mechanical conditions.

Moving surfaces introduce another requirement. A cylinder rod or valve element may need a particular surface condition and contact behaviour in addition to bulk strength and corrosion resistance. The component manufacturer may use a material combination that is more specific than the housing description. Do not replace a documented internal grade with the nominal housing alloy solely to make the material list look uniform.

Assess local conditions that a bulk-water sample cannot show

The water measured in a reservoir represents a useful part of the exposure, but local conditions can differ at a joint or beneath a deposit. Pitting and crevice corrosion are reasons to consider geometry, temperature and the local environment rather than relying only on an average corrosion-rate value. Outokumpu's corrosion guidance explains why the mechanism of attack matters to the choice of control. [1]

A narrow instrument branch illustrates the issue. The main circuit may receive regular flow while the branch holds water during long idle periods. A threaded joint or seal interface can also retain a small volume that behaves differently from the bulk fluid. Identify such locations during design and consider whether they can be reduced, drained, inspected or maintained without compromising the component's pressure and sealing functions.

This is not an argument that every crevice inevitably fails. It is an argument for describing the actual configuration before claiming suitability. The designer may select a different joint arrangement, a different grade or a different operating practice. The preferred response should address the identified exposure. An alloy substitution made without that explanation is difficult for a future maintenance team to evaluate when conditions change.

Use corrosion tables as screening evidence

Published corrosion tables can help narrow a list of candidate materials, but their test conditions are part of the result. Concentration, temperature, aeration, surface condition and the form of the test specimen can affect how a result should be interpreted. Outokumpu explicitly describes limitations to its tabulated information. A favourable entry is useful evidence within its boundary, not approval for every assembly containing that grade. [2]

When reviewing a table, first ask whether the environment resembles the proposed service. A process stream containing several constituents cannot always be represented by choosing a row for just one of them. Next ask whether the mechanism of concern is covered. Data describing general metal loss may not resolve the performance of a crevice, a stressed component or a fabricated joint.

If the comparison is uncertain, record what additional information would change the selection. That may be a better water analysis, manufacturer experience with a similar configuration or representative testing. The response should be proportionate to the application. A readily replaceable noncritical item and an inaccessible pressure boundary may justify different evidence requirements even when they see similar water.

Preserve surface condition through manufacture and installation

Material selection is not finished when the raw stock is ordered. Fabrication and handling determine the condition of the finished surface. Contamination, deposits, damage and unsuitable processing can create a result different from the clean material assumed during selection. The project should therefore identify any surface-treatment and cleaning requirements that form part of the supported component design.

Installation practices can undermine that work. Keep fluid connections protected while adjacent fabrication is occurring, prevent debris from entering open ports and use approved assembly consumables. If a component is temporarily removed, record how it is stored and protected before reinstallation. These are practical controls that can prevent an otherwise appropriate material from entering service in an avoidably poor condition.

A repair procedure deserves the same attention. Cleaning to remove loose contamination is not necessarily the same operation as a treatment intended to restore or modify a surface. Select procedures that are compatible with the complete assembly, including seals and polymers. If a specialist treatment is required, define its acceptance criteria and any steps needed before the equipment returns to water service.

Review dissimilar materials at the interfaces

A stainless assembly often connects to other metals or to equipment whose internal construction is different. Review those boundaries rather than assuming that the principal material controls the whole result. Dissimilar-metal contact in an electrolyte can create galvanic conditions; the exposed areas and actual environment affect the severity. The right question is how the connected assembly behaves, not merely whether each individual item has performed acceptably elsewhere.

Possible responses include a compatible material change or an engineered isolation arrangement. Any proposed isolation must preserve pressure integrity and the required electrical safety provisions. A modification that reduces one corrosion path while compromising bonding or creating a weak connection does not solve the system problem. Coordinate the disciplines that own these different functions.

Trace the interface decision into the spare-parts list. If a specific fitting or isolating arrangement is required, make it identifiable so that a routine replacement does not restore the original exposure. A procurement substitution should trigger review of the function being protected. This is particularly important at sites where apparently similar fittings are held in general stores for several different services.

Write a material schedule that can be maintained

A useful schedule links each component or assembly to its approved wetted materials and supporting information. It should distinguish confirmed materials from items awaiting supplier clarification. Include exceptions rather than concealing them inside a broad claim that the entire system is one grade. Small internal parts can matter to reliability even when their contribution to the purchase price is minor.

The equipment supplier may provide an assembly-level approval rather than every proprietary manufacturing detail. In that case, define the operating medium and limits covered by the approval and the conditions for replacement or repair. Protecting legitimate proprietary information does not require leaving the intended service ambiguous. The buyer needs a supportable statement of suitability and a way to preserve the approved configuration.

For a water-hydraulic installation, that schedule should connect to the actual water specification. The Water Hydraulics Company's P3 documentation illustrates that construction and fluid conditions can be configuration-dependent. A product's general association with water does not establish suitability for every source or every cleaning exposure. Resolve the selected configuration during enquiry and carry it through the purchase and commissioning records. [4]

Evaluate value over the installed service life

The lowest raw-material cost is rarely the whole purchasing question. A more suitable material or joint arrangement may reduce intervention at a difficult location, while an unnecessary upgrade may add expense without changing the governing failure mechanism. Evaluate alternatives using installation, inspection, replacement access and availability as well as initial price. State the assumptions so that the comparison remains useful when the operating context changes.

The compelling case for stainless water-hydraulic equipment is therefore specific. The selected assembly delivers the mechanical duty, its materials suit the defined exposure, and the owner has a practical way to maintain that condition. Removing hydraulic oil can then be evaluated as a separate operational benefit where a release would create a meaningful problem. WaterQuest can help connect these requirements into a supported configuration; a universal claim that one stainless grade is essential everywhere would make that engineering case weaker.

From understanding to equipment selection

Specify stainless steel by service conditions

Describe water chemistry, temperature, deposits and external exposure before selecting the grade and assembly. Include fasteners, springs, shafts and fittings in the review; a stainless body does not define every wetted material.
