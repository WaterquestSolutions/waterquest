Electric Actuators vs Hydraulic Actuators: The Real Engineering Trade-Offs

Compare electric and hydraulic motion on the same duty.

Electric and hydraulic actuators solve different combinations of force, speed and control. Compare the complete installation, including holding, recovery, space and maintenance, to choose the approach that best serves your equipment.

Article 036Performance Comparisons9 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Compare the complete route from power to movement

Electric actuators use a motor and mechanical transmission; hydraulic actuators use a pressurised liquid supply. Water hydraulics adds the option of hydraulic force without an oil-filled working circuit. Compare each arrangement against your load, movement, holding and recovery needs, then include installation space, energy use and servicing over the same operating schedule.

Water hydraulics are one hydraulic option, with materials and fluid management selected for the intended water duty. Their particular value can include removing mineral oil from a circuit where its presence creates a burden. Electric actuation may remove the need for hydraulic distribution altogether in some applications. A fair comparison allows each architecture an appropriate design and includes its supporting equipment. The owner's decision concerns the complete installed service, not an isolated actuator displayed without the utilities and controls that make it work.

Translate the load through each mechanism

A hydraulic cylinder's ideal force follows pressure acting on effective area, adjusted for opposing pressure and mechanical resistance. [1] An electric linear actuator often uses a screw or another transmission to convert motor torque into linear force. The transmission ratio connects force, speed and motor requirements. A large force rating does not imply that the actuator can produce that force at every speed or for every duration. Evaluate the complete load profile rather than selecting from a peak number.

An independent ideal screw example illustrates the tradeoff. With a 10 millimetre lead, 10 newton metres of torque corresponds to about 6.28 kilonewtons of axial force if losses are ignored, using force equal to two pi times torque divided by lead. Real efficiency, friction and limits change the result. The example is not a product selection. It shows why transmission geometry, like hydraulic piston area, changes the relationship between force and motion. The useful comparison must include both the required load and the speed at which it is delivered.

Check duty cycle and thermal limits

An actuator suitable for occasional isolation may not be suitable for continuous modulation or frequent reversals. Electric motors, drives and transmissions have thermal and mechanical limits that depend on the duty. Hydraulic pumps, valves and fluid also generate heat through losses. Each alternative needs a supported operating schedule, including starts, movement, holding and idle periods. A successful single stroke does not establish continuous production capability or repeated operation without adequate recovery time.

Ask for the conditions behind the offered rating. Ambient temperature, load, movement duration and frequency can influence allowable operation. A nominal motor power is not a complete duty description, and neither is a hydraulic maximum pressure. The comparison should identify where the thermal burden appears and how it is managed. Cooling, enclosure requirements or control strategies can change installed cost and maintenance. Include these functions in the offer rather than comparing bare actuator prices while leaving necessary supporting equipment outside one side of the assessment.

Treat precision as an installed performance requirement

Electric actuation can offer useful direct control and feedback, while hydraulic actuation can provide effective motion control through suitable valves and sensors. Actual precision depends on backlash, compliance, friction, feedback location and controller behaviour. A transmission may have lost motion; a hydraulic circuit may have leakage or fluid and structural compliance. Neither architecture should receive an automatic precision label. Define the accuracy, repeatability, minimum speed and settling behaviour the process needs.

Measure at the relevant mechanism. A motor encoder does not always establish the final driven position after a transmission or flexible linkage. A cylinder sensor may likewise omit movement elsewhere in the load path. Select feedback according to the process requirement. For simple end-to-end motion, elaborate intermediate control may add little value. For demanding positioning, the complete assembly needs verification under representative load. This prevents the comparison from rewarding catalogue resolution that the installed mechanism cannot use or overlooking a simpler design that fully meets the required function.

Understand holding and back-driving behaviour

Some mechanical transmissions resist back-driving under certain conditions, while others can be driven by the load. Do not assume that every electric screw actuator is self-locking or that a brake automatically satisfies every protective requirement. Hydraulic valves can restrict flow and contribute to holding, but leakage and other circuit paths remain relevant. The required stopped state must be defined for the actual load, duration and consequence of movement.

Separate ordinary position holding from a protective function. A mechanism that must remain secure during maintenance may require a different arrangement from one holding position during normal production. Gravity, springs and external process forces can store or supply energy in either architecture. The risk assessment should identify what must be restrained, isolated or verified. A technology comparison is incomplete if one offer includes the necessary brake, lock or load-control arrangement while the other is priced only for normal powered motion.

Plan how moving energy is absorbed

A moving load carries energy that must be managed when it slows or stops. An electric drive may dissipate or recover energy depending on its configuration and operating conditions. A hydraulic circuit may control deceleration through valves and other selected features, with energy converted or stored according to the architecture. These capabilities are not automatic. Specify the moving mass, speed, stopping requirement and any load that can drive the actuator rather than simply resist it.

An overrunning load deserves particular attention because removing drive power does not necessarily stop it. The complete control and protective arrangement must keep the motion within the required envelope. Abrupt hydraulic valve closure can create pressure transients; an electric transmission can see mechanical loads during rapid stopping. The designer should assess these states and verify the relevant behaviour. Comparing only steady force or torque misses the conditions that may govern both the equipment selection and the safety of the completed mechanism.

Compare distribution and installation space

A single remote electric actuator may be attractive because it needs suitable power and control connections without a hydraulic supply and return route. A group of hydraulic consumers may benefit from a shared power source, depending on layout and duty. The preferred architecture depends on distances, simultaneous demand, local space and the consequences of a common supply failure. Evaluate the installation rather than assuming that either cables or pipework are always simpler in every facility.

Include access, supports, enclosures and field integration. Electric equipment needs suitable environmental protection and electrical arrangements. Water hydraulics need compatible fluid management, valves and piping, with thermal or freeze protection where required. A centralised supply can simplify some maintenance while creating shared dependencies. Local packages can isolate faults while increasing the number of service locations. These are design tradeoffs that should be visible to the owner. A component-only comparison conceals them and can lead to an attractive purchase that is difficult to install or support.

Define the response to loss of normal energy

The process may require an actuator to open, close, hold or allow supervised recovery after a supply interruption. Each architecture needs a supported way to achieve the selected response. Springs, batteries, stored hydraulic energy or a separate manual arrangement may contribute, but their capacity and sequence must be verified. The presence of a backup component does not establish the complete function if the controls or mechanical path cannot use it under the initiating failure condition.

Hydraulic-system safety context is provided by ISO 4413's published scope, while the complete project must establish applicable requirements for both alternatives and their installation. [2] Define readiness indications and the action required if the protective function is unavailable. Acceptance should address the relevant interruption through an approved test plan. A normal powered movement does not prove the loss-of-power response. Keeping this requirement explicit makes offers comparable and ensures that the selected technology serves the process when normal operation is disrupted.

Compare energy and maintenance over the same calendar

Mechanical work is force times distance, and power relates to force times speed for a straight-line movement. The input required to perform that work includes the architecture's losses and supporting functions. Standby, holding, heating and cooling can matter as much as the active stroke in an intermittent application. The DOE's pumping guidance supports system-level energy assessment for the hydraulic option. [3] Apply an equally complete boundary to the electric alternative rather than compare one device's input with another system's useful output.

Maintenance differs in content rather than disappearing. Electric transmissions may require inspection or service of screws, gears, brakes and seals according to their design. Water systems require appropriate fluid condition, filters, seals and hydraulic-component support. Use current manufacturer instructions and the site's access and labour conditions to compare ownership. A familiar service network can be valuable, while a new arrangement may need training and spares. State these requirements honestly instead of assuming that removing a hydraulic circuit or changing its fluid makes an actuator maintenance-free.

Choose the arrangement that meets the complete duty

A useful comparison records the required behaviour, offered configuration, evidence and uncertainty for each option. Include installed scope, fault response and support. Where an unresolved performance issue could change the decision, define a representative test before committing to widespread adoption. The preferred answer may differ between a single remote valve, a continuously modulating mechanism and a group of high-force industrial consumers. That variation is a sign of application-based engineering rather than an inconsistency.

WaterQuest should recommend water actuation where its hydraulic performance and removal of a defined oil inventory address the owner's needs. Electric actuation remains a credible alternative and may be the better fit for another duty. Engineers need a verified load and control envelope, procurement needs complete comparable offers and operators need a maintainable installation. The strongest technology argument is therefore specific enough to test: this arrangement performs this task under these conditions, with these ownership consequences. That is more useful than declaring either architecture the universal future of actuation.
