Selecting Pumps for Water Hydraulic Systems

Article 39 · Practical Engineering Guides · WaterQuest Solutions

Pump selection is the most consequential single component decision in a water hydraulic system design. An appropriately selected pump delivers the required flow and pressure at acceptable efficiency, while an inappropriate selection results in excessive energy consumption, shortened component life, or outright system failure. Unlike mineral oil systems where pump technology has converged around well-established designs, water hydraulic systems demand careful attention to material compatibility, cavitation resistance, and the unique tribological challenges of water as a working fluid.

This article provides a complete pump selection guide for water hydraulic systems: pump type selection based on pressure and flow requirements, displacement and speed sizing, material requirements for water service, and system integration considerations including inlet conditions, speed control, and load sensing.

Pump Type Selection: The First Decision

The appropriate pump type for a water hydraulic system depends primarily on the operating pressure range, with secondary considerations including flow requirements, efficiency targets, and whether variable displacement is required.

Axial Piston Pumps: The High-Pressure Standard

Axial piston pumps are the workhorse of industrial water hydraulics above 100 bar. In this design, a cylinder block containing multiple pistons rotates around a drive shaft. The pistons reciprocate as they follow a swashplate or bent axis, drawing water in during the extending stroke and discharging under pressure during the retracting stroke. The number of pistons (typically 7 or 9 in water hydraulic designs) and their displacement determine flow rate at a given rotational speed.

The axial piston design achieves high pressure capability (up to 350 bar in purpose-built water hydraulic units) through distributed loading across multiple pistons and precision-machined contact surfaces. The tribological challenge in water service is addressed through hard-faced materials: carbon-reinforced PEEK pistons running against 316 stainless steel cylinder blocks, with ceramic or carbide valve plates. These material combinations provide the wear resistance and corrosion immunity necessary for reliable water hydraulic operation.

  • Pressure range: 100 to 350 bar continuous, 400 bar intermittent
  • Flow range: 5 to 500+ L/min depending on displacement and speed
  • Volumetric efficiency: 92 to 96% at rated conditions
  • Displacement types: Fixed or variable (load-sensing, pressure-compensated)
  • Best for: All industrial and municipal applications above 100 bar

Gear Pumps: The Low-Pressure Option

External gear pumps, consisting of two meshing gears rotating within a close-tolerance housing, are the simplest positive displacement design. Water enters at the inlet side, is carried around the housing periphery in the spaces between gear teeth, and discharged at the outlet as the teeth mesh again. The design provides fixed displacement with flow proportional to rotational speed.

Water hydraulic gear pumps are appropriate for low-pressure applications, including dust suppression systems, wash-down circuits, and auxiliary flows in industrial equipment. The pressure limitation (typically 100 bar maximum continuous) arises from the side loading on gear shafts and the difficulty of maintaining close running clearances as pressure increases. Material selection is critical: 316 stainless steel gears and housing with ceramic or carbide bushings for the gear shafts.

  • Pressure range: Up to 100 bar continuous
  • Flow range: 1 to 200 L/min
  • Volumetric efficiency: 80 to 90% at rated conditions
  • Best for: Low-pressure services, dust suppression circuits, auxiliary flows

Radial Piston Pumps: The Specialist Option

Radial piston pumps, in which pistons are arranged radially around a central drive shaft and reciprocate against an eccentric cam, represent a specialist pump type for water hydraulics. The design provides very high pressure capability (up to 700 bar in specialist water hydraulic configurations) through superior piston load distribution, but at lower flow rates than comparable axial piston units.

  • Pressure range: Up to 700 bar (specialist designs)
  • Flow range: 0.1 to 50 L/min (typically lower flow than axial piston)
  • Best for: High-pressure specialist applications, precision servo, low-flow high-pressure services

Sizing the Pump: Flow and Pressure Requirements

Step 1: Calculate Peak Flow Demand

The pump must deliver the peak simultaneous flow required by all actuators that operate concurrently. For a hydraulic cylinder extending at velocity v with piston area A, the required flow is:

CYLINDER FLOW

Q_cyl (L/min) = A_bore (cm²) × v_piston (m/s) × 600 / 1000

Example: 100mm bore (A = 78.54 cm²) extending at 0.1 m/s requires 4.7 L/min

Sum all simultaneously operating cylinder flows to get system peak demand. Add 15% margin for pump inefficiency and minor circuit flows.

Step 2: Verify Pressure Capability

The pump outlet pressure must overcome the maximum actuator load pressure plus all circuit losses from valves, fittings, and piping. For a cylinder pushing force F against piston area A:

SYSTEM PRESSURE

P_pump = P_load_max + ΔP_circuit

Circuit losses (well-designed system): 10 to 25 bar

Circuit losses (poorly designed system): 50+ bar

Select a pump whose continuous pressure rating exceeds the required pump outlet pressure by at least 15%. This margin accommodates transient pressure spikes during rapid directional changes or load impact.

Step 3: Determine Displacement and Speed

Once flow requirement Q and maximum speed n are established, calculate the required pump displacement:

DISPLACEMENT SIZING

V_disp (cc/rev) = Q (L/min) × 1000 / n (rpm)

Example: 50 L/min at 1500 rpm requires 33 cc/rev displacement

Water hydraulic pumps typically operate between 1000 and 1800 rpm, with higher speeds increasing wear rates and cavitation risk. Where possible, select a larger displacement pump running at lower speed rather than a smaller displacement unit at maximum speed.

Material Requirements for Water Service

Water's corrosive nature and poor lubricity demand materials fundamentally different from those used in mineral oil hydraulic pumps. All wetted surfaces must resist corrosion in the operating environment (tap water, seawater, or other aqueous media), while sliding contact surfaces must provide wear resistance without the boundary lubrication provided by oil.

Corrosion-Resistant Structural Materials

Pump housings, ports, manifolds, and structural components must be fabricated from corrosion-resistant alloys. The minimum acceptable material is 316 stainless steel, providing adequate corrosion resistance in fresh water and municipal supply applications. For seawater service or aggressive aqueous solutions, super-austenitic stainless steels (254 SMO, AL-6XN) or nickel alloys (Hastelloy C-276) may be required.

Carbon steel or ductile iron housings are unacceptable in water hydraulic service, even with protective coatings. Coating failure leads to rapid corrosion and contamination of the hydraulic circuit with iron oxide particles.

Tribological Contact Materials

Sliding contact surfaces, where pump components move relative to one another under pressure, represent the most challenging material selection decision. The absence of oil's boundary lubrication film means these surfaces must provide inherent wear resistance and low friction through material properties alone.

The proven material combination for water hydraulic pump internals is carbon-reinforced PEEK (polyetheretherketone) running against hardened 316 stainless steel. PEEK provides low friction, good wear resistance, and excellent chemical resistance, while carbon reinforcement improves mechanical properties and reduces thermal expansion. The stainless steel counterface is hardened (typically to HRC 58 to 62) through nitriding or similar surface treatment.

Alternative materials include:

  • Silicon carbide (valve plates and wear surfaces): Excellent wear resistance but brittle
  • Tungsten carbide (high-pressure contact areas): Superior hardness but expensive
  • Ceramic materials (specialist high-pressure applications): Very hard but sensitive to impact

Inlet Conditions and Cavitation Prevention

Cavitation, the formation and collapse of vapour bubbles in the working fluid, is the most common cause of premature pump failure in water hydraulic systems. Water's high vapour pressure (compared to mineral oil) makes water hydraulic pumps inherently more susceptible to cavitation damage. Preventing cavitation requires careful attention to inlet conditions.

Net Positive Inlet Pressure (NPIP)

The pump inlet must maintain sufficient pressure above water's vapour pressure to prevent bubble formation. This requirement is expressed as Net Positive Inlet Pressure:

NPIP REQUIREMENT

NPIP = P_inlet - P_vapour > NPIP_required

Where P_vapour ≈ 0.02 bar at 20°C (increases with temperature)

Typical pump NPIP requirement: 0.5 to 2.0 bar above vapour pressure

The pump manufacturer specifies the required NPIP for a given pump model at rated speed and flow. The system designer must ensure this NPIP is available under all operating conditions, including maximum fluid temperature (which increases vapour pressure) and minimum reservoir level (which reduces hydrostatic head at the pump inlet).

Reservoir Pressurization

Where gravity head alone cannot provide adequate NPIP, reservoir pressurization becomes necessary. A low-pressure charge pump or compressed air maintains 1 to 3 bar gauge pressure on the reservoir free surface, ensuring positive pressure at the main pump inlet even under worst-case conditions. The pressurization system must include pressure relief protection to prevent reservoir overpressure.

Inlet Line Sizing

Inlet line pressure drop directly reduces available NPIP. Size inlet lines for maximum velocity of 1.0 m/s, well below the 2.0 m/s typical in oil hydraulic systems. The larger line size reduces friction loss and turbulence, improving inlet conditions and pump service life.

Variable Displacement and Load Sensing

Variable displacement pumps adjust output flow by changing the swashplate angle (in axial piston designs), delivering only the flow required by the system rather than maximum displacement at all times. This capability provides substantial energy savings in applications with varying flow demand.

Pressure-Compensated Control

The simplest variable displacement control maintains constant outlet pressure by reducing displacement as system pressure approaches the set point. When system pressure reaches the compensator setting, the pump destrokes to minimum displacement, delivering only enough flow to maintain pressure against system leakage. This control mode suits systems with frequent idle periods between work cycles.

Load-Sensing Control

Load-sensing control adjusts pump displacement to maintain a constant pressure margin (typically 15 to 25 bar) above the highest load pressure in the system. A pilot line connects the pump controller to the load-sensing port on system valves, communicating the current load pressure. The pump delivers exactly the flow demanded by the system at a pressure just sufficient to operate the loads, minimizing energy consumption across all operating conditions.

Load sensing provides the highest efficiency in systems with varying loads and flow requirements, but adds complexity and requires compatible valves with load-sensing ports.

System Integration Considerations

Drive Speed and Control

Fixed-speed AC motors are the standard drive for industrial water hydraulic pumps, with variable frequency drives (VFDs) providing speed control where flow demand varies. VFD control allows the pump to operate at the minimum speed necessary to meet current flow requirements, reducing energy consumption and extending pump life through reduced wear rates.

For mobile or off-grid applications, diesel or petrol engines provide pump drive power. Engine speed governors maintain constant pump speed under varying loads.

Filtration Requirements

Water hydraulic pumps require clean inlet water to prevent abrasive wear of close-tolerance surfaces. Install a suction strainer (40 to 100 mesh) at the reservoir outlet to catch large particles, and a 10 to 25 micron absolute filter in the pump inlet line. High-pressure filters (3 to 10 micron) on the pump discharge protect downstream components from wear particles generated during pump operation.

Starting and Shutdown Procedures

Unlike oil hydraulic pumps that can be started under pressure, water hydraulic pumps should be started against an unloaded circuit. Open all system valves to tank before energizing the pump drive motor, allowing the pump to accelerate to full speed with minimal outlet pressure. This practice reduces starting torque and prevents pressure surges that could damage pump internals.

At shutdown, close all system valves before stopping the pump to prevent reverse flow through the pump as pressure decays. In systems with vertical actuators or other gravity loads, install pilot-operated check valves to hold loads during pump shutdown.
