Designing Fail-Closed Functions and Manual Overrides

Design the response your process needs when normal operation stops.

What should your equipment do when power is lost? Learn how stored energy, valve positions and manual controls can support a planned response, with the complete mechanism assessed for its intended safety function.

Article 125Specification & Design10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Plan the movement after power is lost

A valve may need to close after a power failure; another mechanism may need to hold or remain open. Define the response that protects your process first. Stored energy, valve configuration and manual controls can then be arranged to support that response, with enough force and flow to complete the required movement under the assessed conditions.

Begin by naming the initiating conditions and the required outcome. Loss of normal electrical power, loss of a control signal and a hydraulic fault are different events. A design that responds appropriately to one may not achieve the same result after another. The requirement should state which events it covers.

This article explains the engineering questions behind fail-closed functions and manual recovery. It does not provide a construction circuit or operating procedure. The responsible designers must determine the architecture, applicable requirements and validation for the actual mechanism. Water-compatible equipment can support that design, but the working fluid does not choose or prove the safe state.

Distinguish moving closed from remaining in place

A load-holding arrangement aims to limit movement under defined conditions. A fail-closed arrangement must create or permit the required closing movement after its specified trigger. These functions have different energy and control needs. A blocked flow path may hold an open actuator without providing any means to close it.

Likewise, releasing a restraint can allow movement where gravity or another external force acts, but the resulting speed and final position still require assessment. The mechanism may need controlled movement rather than an unrestricted release. The relevant behaviour depends on the load path and process consequences.

Use precise language in the specification and controls description. Terms such as safe, locked and closed should refer to identifiable states. This helps prevent a supplier from offering a holding valve in response to a closure requirement or an operator from interpreting a stopped motor as confirmation that the process isolation has been achieved.

Identify the source of emergency movement energy

If normal pump power is unavailable, a required movement needs another usable energy source. Depending on the application, a design may use a spring, gravity, stored hydraulic energy or an alternative supported supply. Each option has a different relationship with the load and the initial state.

The design must show that enough usable energy and force are available throughout the required travel. A nominal spring rating or accumulator volume alone cannot establish that result. The changing mechanism geometry, opposing pressure, friction and other relevant effects belong in the assessment.

The pressure-and-area relationship explains hydraulic force, while the mechanical arrangement determines how that force acts on the process element. [3] These calculations should be tied to the least favourable relevant condition, not only a convenient nominal case. Emergency functionality is valuable precisely when normal conditions may no longer be available.

Evaluate the entire stroke and required timing

The force needed to close a valve or gate can vary through its travel. A linkage may change mechanical advantage, and the process load may change as the opening reduces. A design that initiates movement successfully may still lack the required capability near the final position.

Timing matters too. Closing too slowly may fail to meet the process objective, while closing too rapidly can introduce mechanical or fluid-system consequences. The required movement profile should therefore come from the process and machinery assessment, not from a general preference for the fastest possible closure.

Define what successful closure means for the process as well as the actuator. A position signal may not prove that the gate has closed if a linkage is damaged. Check the entire stroke: a gate may need its greatest force near the end, when an emergency energy source has less pressure available. The test must demonstrate the required closure under those conditions.

Account for the least favourable initial condition

An emergency source may not always begin at its most favourable state. Stored energy can change with normal operation, leakage or maintenance condition. A mechanism may be at any position when the initiating event occurs. The design should identify which starting conditions it must cover.

For a stored-energy arrangement, usable capacity matters more than a nominal container size. Pressure range, available flow and the required movement determine whether the source can complete the function. Those quantities need to be assessed for the actual selected equipment and circuit.

Monitoring can help establish readiness, but an indication must correspond to a meaningful condition. A general power-on lamp cannot prove that an emergency movement source is available. The owner should understand what readiness checks demonstrate and what limitations remain, particularly after service or a period of inactivity.

Examine failures beyond normal power loss

A function described only as closing on power loss may not address a broken mechanical connection, unavailable hydraulic path or invalid feedback. The relevant failure assessment should identify which events the architecture is intended to tolerate and which require another protective measure.

This does not mean every conceivable failure must be solved by one actuator. It means the scope of the function must be explicit. A complete machinery or process design can distribute responsibilities among mechanical, hydraulic, electrical and operational measures where appropriate.

ISO 4413 provides a general hydraulic-system safety framework. [1] Application-specific functional requirements and validation may go further. The project should identify the governing basis rather than assuming that a component described as fail-safe establishes the performance of the whole mechanism under all failures.

Design position confirmation around the real outcome

A command signal indicates intent. A valve-coil indication may confirm an electrical state. Neither necessarily proves that the process element reached its required position. The design should determine what feedback is needed to confirm the outcome and how unavailable or inconsistent feedback is handled.

Consider the physical relationship between the feedback device and the controlled element. An actuator-position signal may be useful, but its relevance depends on the linkage and mechanism. A sensor can also fail or become misadjusted, so the acceptance and maintenance plan should address its role.

Clear indications help operators avoid making a process decision on an ambiguous status. The user interface should distinguish commanded closure, movement in progress and confirmed state where those distinctions matter. This is an operational design issue as much as an instrumentation choice, because the information must support the action the operator is expected to take.

Give manual override a defined purpose

A manual override may support recovery, maintenance or a required alternative operating method. Its purpose should be stated before selecting the hardware. An emergency recovery function has different availability needs from a local adjustment intended only during controlled service.

Assess access and effort under the conditions in which the override must be used. A hand-operated arrangement can be impractical if it is obstructed, located in the affected hazard area or requires an unrealistic action from the available staff. The operating method should be compatible with the mechanism and the site's authorised procedures.

An override should not quietly defeat a necessary protective function. The design must establish who can use it, how its state is identified and how the normal arrangement is restored. This prevents a useful recovery feature from becoming an undocumented bypass that remains engaged after the immediate task is finished.

Preserve control of stored energy during recovery

Manual operation does not imply absence of hydraulic or mechanical energy. A person may be interacting with a loaded mechanism, retained pressure or an emergency source. CCOHS identifies these energy forms within the broader hazardous-energy control context. [2]

The actual procedure must be developed for the installed arrangement by the responsible parties. It should account for the condition of the load and the effect of each authorised action. This article does not prescribe how to release pressure, operate a bypass or disconnect a component.

The design can make recovery easier by providing clear identification, suitable access and an understandable sequence. The owner should not have to infer the behaviour of several similar valves during an abnormal event. Good engineering reduces the need for improvisation and makes the available recovery function credible in the conditions for which it was provided.

Validate the complete response and its restoration

Testing should demonstrate the required response under the relevant conditions using a safe, agreed method. The record should identify the initial state, load, configuration and observed result. Where direct demonstration is not appropriate for every condition, the responsible team should identify the supporting analysis or other evidence.

Include restoration after the event. A mechanism that closes successfully but cannot be returned to a known operating state without an improvised intervention leaves an important ownership gap. The recovery and readiness checks should be understood by the people who will operate and maintain the asset.

The maintenance programme should preserve the function's readiness. Relevant checks may concern the emergency source, mechanical linkage, indications and authorised override state. The exact requirements come from the accepted design. A successful commissioning test should not become an assumption that readiness remains unchanged indefinitely.

Document modifications carefully. A different actuator, changed valve characteristic or revised control sequence can affect closing behaviour even if normal operation still appears satisfactory. The owner needs a change-review process that recognises the emergency function as a defined requirement, ensuring that routine improvements do not unintentionally remove the capability the original design was intended to provide.

The fail-closed claim earns confidence through a complete energy path, appropriate control and observable outcome, supported by validation rather than by the fluid name or a single component symbol.

From understanding to equipment selection

Size the movement after power loss

Define the load and travel needed for the safe state, then identify the energy source that remains available. If a gas accumulator is proposed, assess usable volume between the actual maximum and minimum operating pressures, not its shell volume alone.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the accumulator calculator to estimate nominal gas volume from usable water demand, pressure limits and precharge. Use the water-hammer calculator to screen pressure-change assumptions for a specified line and closure event.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Review stored energy, pressure control and isolation as part of the power-unit and valve arrangement. Identify the function the accumulator must perform before requesting a size.

What service provisions are needed for stored hydraulic energy?

Include periodic verification of stored-energy availability and controlled recovery after a trip. A manual override must be accessible and must not unexpectedly release a suspended or restrained load.

The accumulator model does not verify a complete emergency function; the water-hammer result is a screening estimate, not a full transient-network analysis.
