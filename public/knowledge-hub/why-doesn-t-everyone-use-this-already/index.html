Why Doesn't Everyone Use This Already?

Find the applications where a move to water makes sense.

Water hydraulics are a practical option for some duties, with adoption shaped by equipment fit, service support and conversion cost. Learn what to evaluate so you can make a confident decision for your own application.

Article 111Start Here10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Make adoption practical for the equipment owner

A useful alternative must fit the machine, the operating team and the support available over its life. Water hydraulics offers a clear reason to reconsider circuits where oil creates a burden. Starting with a suitable duty and a well-defined package lets the owner assess that benefit alongside installation, training and maintenance before expanding its use.

The question is worth asking without turning it into a claim that established practice is irrational. Oil hydraulics have a large installed base, familiar components and experienced service networks. Those are real ownership advantages. Water hydraulics must demonstrate where their particular benefit outweighs the costs and uncertainties of changing the supported design.

A productive discussion therefore asks where the technology fits, what prevents adoption in that application and what evidence would resolve the obstacle. This is more informative than either dismissing water because it is less familiar or assuming that all resistance is merely reluctance to innovate.

Familiarity reduces the work of making a decision

Engineers and maintenance teams accumulate practical knowledge around the equipment they already use. They know which spare parts are stocked, which suppliers respond and what a familiar fault looks like. A new technology can require additional learning even when its basic operating principle is straightforward.

That learning has a cost. A project may need new component identification, different fluid checks and revised service instructions. The owner must also consider whether the support arrangement remains credible after the initial installation. These questions are particularly important for remote or continuously operating assets.

A supplier can reduce the burden by providing clear limits, practical documentation and a realistic support scope. An unexplained claim that the equipment is maintenance-free does the opposite: it leaves experienced staff unable to judge how the asset will behave when something changes. Confidence grows when the new system is understandable enough to become ordinary machinery in the owner's maintenance programme.

Water requires components designed for water

The pressure-transmission principle is familiar, but the internal component design cannot simply be assumed interchangeable with oil equipment. Fluid properties affect leakage and moving contacts, while materials must suit the exposure. A conversion can therefore involve more than draining one fluid and filling with another.

Commercial water-service products show that appropriate designs exist. The Water Hydraulics Company's pump range is a primary example of equipment developed for water operation. [1] Its existence establishes a practical option, while the selected configuration and current operating information determine suitability for a particular duty.

This distinction explains part of the adoption barrier. An owner may have a useful existing oil actuator or valve that has no supported water conversion path. The alternative might require replacement of that equipment and associated integration. The project can still be attractive, but the complete installed change needs to be compared rather than only the purchase price of a pump.

Some applications have a stronger reason to change

Removing an oil inventory has particular value where a release could affect a sensitive process, receiving environment or difficult-to-clean location. In those cases, the fluid choice is connected to a burden the owner already manages. The benefit can be described in terms of an identified pathway and inventory.

In another location, the existing circuit may be well contained, accessible and operating reliably. The same water alternative may offer less incremental value. That does not make the technology ineffective; it means the application does not present the same problem. Adoption should follow the strength of the fit rather than a desire to change every asset uniformly.

An effective screening exercise looks for both a meaningful reason to remove oil and a duty that supported water equipment can perform. Starting with those intersections allows the owner to learn where the proposition is strongest. It also avoids spending early effort forcing the technology into a case where another architecture is more practical.

Compare complete alternatives at the same useful duty

A water proposal should be compared with a supported oil proposal and, where relevant, other ways to perform the function. The useful requirement may be force, torque, movement time, holding or process output. Each alternative should satisfy that requirement before its ownership benefits are ranked.

Installed cost includes integration, controls, supports, access and commissioning as well as equipment. Energy comparisons need a consistent boundary and duty schedule. DOE's pumping guidance supports examining system operation rather than judging a decision from an isolated component value. [3]

This approach can reveal that the best opportunity lies in the architecture rather than the fluid alone. A revised standby strategy may reduce electricity consumption in either a water or oil design. Water may add a separate exposure benefit. Explaining those contributions individually makes the proposal stronger because the owner can see which improvement depends on which engineering change.

Evidence must match the consequence of the decision

A low-consequence pilot and a critical production replacement do not require the same level of confidence. The owner needs evidence proportionate to the function, integration and consequences of failure. That may include component information, representative tests and a credible service arrangement.

A video of a successful movement can introduce the concept but does not establish endurance, approval or performance under every load. Likewise, an impressive material property does not prove the reliability of the complete machine. The evidence should be connected to the actual uncertainty the decision-maker faces.

Suppliers can help by naming the evidence level honestly. Distinguish demonstrated performance, supported manufacturer limits, calculated expectations and unresolved questions. This makes it easier for an engineer to recommend the next step. Overstated claims may appear persuasive initially but often make adoption harder by forcing the reviewer to spend time separating useful information from unsupported conclusions.

Standards and approvals can create legitimate constraints

Some applications have specific approval or validation requirements. The working fluid does not remove them. A component may be suitable for water while the complete function still needs review under the relevant machinery, marine, food or fire-protection framework.

ISO 4413 provides general hydraulic-system safety guidance, but citing it does not establish every application-specific requirement. [2] The project must identify the governing documents and responsible parties for the actual installation. Early clarification helps prevent a technically promising proposal from reaching procurement with an unresolved acceptance route.

This can affect development time and cost. A supplier may have a well-supported component yet lack the evidence needed for a particular regulated function. The appropriate response is to identify the gap and determine whether it can be resolved. It is not to imply that the owner should disregard the requirement because the alternative fluid has an environmental attraction.

Procurement can unintentionally exclude useful alternatives

A specification copied from an existing oil system may prescribe features that are not essential to the useful function. This can prevent a different architecture from being considered even when it could satisfy the owner's underlying need. Reviewing those inherited requirements is a worthwhile part of renewal planning.

Describe the required service and explain any desired reduction in oil exposure. Retain genuinely necessary constraints, including interfaces, availability and acceptance evidence. Then allow suppliers to show how their complete proposals meet them. This gives alternatives a fair opportunity without lowering the standard of proof.

Evaluation criteria should also distinguish substantiated benefits from slogans. A candid proposal with clear operating limits may be more dependable than one promising universal savings. Procurement helps adoption when it rewards a supported solution to the actual problem, rather than either automatically preserving the old arrangement or favouring novelty for its own sake.

A pilot should reduce a decision-relevant uncertainty

A well-chosen pilot tests something that prevents the owner from proceeding. It might establish motion performance on a representative load, clarify maintenance effort or verify water management under the intended schedule. The learning objective should be stated before the equipment is installed.

Select an appropriate boundary and retain enough baseline information to compare the result. Record the configuration, operating conditions and interventions. A pilot with no defined success criteria can produce an attractive demonstration while leaving the original purchasing uncertainty unresolved.

Also define the next decision. A successful trial may justify broader engineering, a second application or a period of monitored operation. It does not automatically establish that every site should convert. Transfer the result only where the relevant conditions match, and explain the differences where they do not. That is how experience becomes useful evidence rather than an overextended success story.

Adoption grows when the ownership proposition is clear

The strongest water-hydraulic opportunities combine a real application problem, supported equipment and a practical path to ownership. The owner should understand what changes, what remains, what the project costs and how the result will be maintained. Those answers matter to consultants and executives as much as the underlying pressure and flow calculations.

WaterQuest can help by making the alternative concrete. Identify the circuit and oil pathway, define the duty and present the compatible equipment scope with its evidence. Explain the service responsibilities and the conditions that would require reassessment. This reduces the effort required for a cautious buyer to evaluate the proposal fairly.

The fact that a technology is not universal does not make it marginal, and the fact that it offers a benefit does not make it universal. The useful question is whether this application has a problem that water hydraulics can solve credibly. Where the answer is supported by engineering and ownership evidence, there is a sound reason to consider changing established practice.

A wider rollout should preserve the lessons from the first installation. Update the equipment standard, training material and spare-parts plan using observed experience. This makes subsequent projects easier to review and prevents each new site from repeating the same learning effort. Consistency becomes an advantage earned through documented use, rather than a claim made before the organisation has operated the equipment.
