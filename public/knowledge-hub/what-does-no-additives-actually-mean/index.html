What Does "No Additives" Actually Mean?

Define the water specification behind an additive-free operating claim.

“No additives” describes the working-fluid formulation, not a promise that any water will do. Learn how water quality, material compatibility and equipment instructions define a practical additive-free hydraulic circuit.

Article 088Safety & Compliance10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Understand the water the equipment is designed to use

Some water hydraulic equipment is designed to operate with an approved water supply without an added hydraulic-fluid formulation. That can simplify fluid purchasing and handling. The water still has a specification: dissolved constituents, particles and operating conditions must suit the equipment. Confirm that supply and the supported configuration when selecting an additive-free arrangement.

This distinction matters to engineers and operators because an imprecise claim can lead to an inappropriate substitution. Municipal water may already contain minerals and treatment residuals. Seawater has a very different composition. A carefully treated process supply presents another condition. The useful specification identifies the permitted medium and any necessary preparation, rather than inviting the reader to assume that all liquids called water behave identically in machinery.

Added formulation and existing constituents are different

A formulated hydraulic fluid may contain ingredients selected to influence properties or service behaviour. Water supplied to a site can contain constituents originating from its source and treatment without being a hydraulic formulation. Calling the latter additive-free does not erase those constituents. For component selection, what matters is the actual composition and condition reaching the equipment.

The purchase enquiry should therefore avoid relying on a marketing phrase alone. Provide available water information and identify any treatment before the pump or reservoir. If the source varies seasonally or can be switched, describe that variability. A supplier can then determine what additional information is needed. This turns a vague fluid description into a compatibility question that can be answered for the selected assembly.

Purpose-designed machinery makes the distinction possible

Water-compatible machinery uses an appropriate combination of materials, clearances, seals and operating conditions. Its suitability does not arise because water has somehow acquired the same properties as an oil formulation. The component design accommodates the intended medium. The Water Hydraulics Company's pump information illustrates that the fluid and construction need to be identified together. [1]

That is why no additives required should not be interpreted as permission to refill an oil-service component with plain water. The old component may rely on properties supplied by its original fluid. A conversion requires a complete compatibility review, including valves and supporting equipment. The simplification offered by a supported water medium is valuable only when the machinery is actually designed and documented to operate with it.

Filtration removes some things and leaves others

Particle filtration addresses suspended material within the capabilities of the selected filter. It does not generally remove all dissolved constituents or establish microbiological suitability. The filter's efficiency, capacity, compatibility and location matter. A statement that the water is filtered is incomplete unless it identifies what the filtration is intended to control and how the equipment is maintained.

This has practical consequences for troubleshooting. A recurring deposit or corrosion concern may not be resolved by fitting a finer particle element, while an inadequate inlet arrangement can be worsened by an inappropriate restriction. The component supplier's installation requirements should guide the design. Treatment choices should respond to a defined mechanism rather than being added because cleaner sounds universally better without considering pressure loss or changes in water chemistry.

Softening and demineralisation are separate processes

Water treatment methods change different characteristics. Softening, demineralisation and desalination should not be treated as interchangeable labels. The resulting water may behave differently with materials and may require different handling. More treatment is not automatically better for every component, because the relevant question is whether the resulting medium matches the supported operating specification.

If a facility already has treated water available, identify its actual condition before assuming it is the preferred hydraulic supply. The manufacturer may need to confirm the selected seals or construction. Likewise, a seawater pump configuration cannot be inferred from a freshwater model simply because both are intended for water. Dedicated saline-service documentation demonstrates that the application deserves its own review. [2]

Antifreeze changes the working medium

Freezing risk can tempt operators to add a familiar antifreeze product. That is a change to the fluid specification, not a harmless convenience. The formulation can affect viscosity, material compatibility, clearances and environmental statements. It may also change the conditions under which the equipment is supported. The correct route is to obtain approval for the exact medium and concentration from the relevant equipment suppliers.

Other freeze-protection approaches also require engineering. Heating depends on power and control, insulation only slows heat transfer, and draining depends on the ability to remove trapped water. The project should select a supported strategy before the cold exposure occurs. A no-additives claim should not prevent a necessary design discussion, nor should a later unrecorded addition leave operators believing they are still running the originally specified medium.

Corrosion inhibitors are not a substitute for diagnosis

A corrosion concern should lead to investigation of the material, exposure and local mechanism. Water chemistry, temperature, stagnant areas, deposits and construction details can all matter. Outokumpu's guidance explains that stainless corrosion behaviour depends on the environment and conditions, not merely the stainless label. [5] Adding a chemical without understanding the cause can leave the original problem unresolved.

If a supplier-supported formulation is proposed, evaluate it as a revised operating condition for the whole circuit. Seals, sliding interfaces, instruments and discharge arrangements may be affected. Record the reason for the change and how its effectiveness will be checked. This approach preserves accountability: the treatment is a defined engineering decision with a purpose, rather than an undocumented attempt to make a symptom disappear.

Biological control has its own objective

A recirculating water circuit can have conditions that require attention to biological contamination, depending on its design and service. The appropriate response must consider the machinery and the intended use of the water. A biocide selected for another facility system should not be introduced casually into hydraulic equipment. Its compatibility, handling and effect on the working medium need assessment.

Hydraulic fluid suitability is also distinct from suitability for contact with food or another sensitive process. CFIA guidance considers water in relation to its intended use and contamination pathways. [3] A hydraulic circuit does not become sanitary merely because its original fill came from a potable supply. If the machinery is near exposed product, the contamination-control assessment should consider the condition of the circulating water and the route of a potential leak.

Service can introduce substances unintentionally

The working medium can change without anyone deliberately adding a treatment chemical. Cleaning residues, assembly products, wear particles and contamination from filling equipment can enter during service. A hose previously used for another fluid can create an unexpected introduction. The operating plan should therefore address how the circuit is filled and restored after maintenance, not only what liquid appears on the purchase specification.

Good identification helps prevent these problems. Label the approved medium, keep compatible service equipment and make the relevant instructions accessible. After a component has been cleaned or replaced, use the authorised restart procedure and preserve a record of the work. The aim is to maintain a known operating condition so that later performance changes can be investigated without first guessing what may have entered the circuit.

Fluid properties still depend on temperature and pressure

Even without an added formulation, water's viscosity, density and other properties change with conditions. Scientific references such as IAPWS describe these relationships within stated ranges. [4] No additives does not mean constant properties or unlimited temperature capability. The component's supported envelope still governs how the machinery should be operated.

This distinction helps explain why a system may behave differently during a cold start or after warming. The fluid and solid components can both influence leakage, friction and response. Monitoring should focus on the variables relevant to the selected design rather than assuming that an unchanged chemical name means an unchanged operating condition. A precise fluid specification therefore belongs alongside temperature, pressure and duty-cycle information, not in place of them.

Write the operating instruction in unambiguous language

An effective instruction identifies the approved fluid, the source requirements and prohibited substitutions. It states who must approve a change and what information is needed. If a supplier permits more than one medium, record which configuration and operating limits apply to each. Do not leave a technician to infer compatibility from a general brochure or from the fact that another machine uses a particular product.

The same care should appear in commercial wording. A statement that a selected configuration requires no added hydraulic-fluid formulation is narrower than saying all water-hydraulic equipment needs no chemicals under every condition. The narrower statement can be useful and compelling when supported. It tells the owner what handling task may be simplified while preserving the need to manage supply quality and the machinery's operating environment.

Understand the practical benefit without overstating it

A supported system that uses an available water supply without a separate hydraulic formulation can simplify fluid purchasing and reduce a defined oil-related exposure. The value depends on the complete installation, including any treatment, monitoring or maintenance it still requires. A simpler medium is an opportunity to improve the operating arrangement, not proof that the arrangement is maintenance-free.

WaterQuest can help connect the intended water supply to compatible equipment and clear instructions. The educational goal is to make the phrase no additives useful: identify what is not required, what is already present and what changes would require review. When those distinctions are clear, engineers can select appropriate machinery, procurement can compare the real supporting scope and operators can preserve the condition that the supplier has agreed to support.

For a final review, trace a sample of working water from its source through filling, operation, service and eventual removal. At each stage, identify how its condition could change and who controls and records that specific change. This simple exercise often reveals a missing instruction or responsibility before it becomes a recurring maintenance problem.

From understanding to equipment selection

Define which additions are excluded

Distinguish the working-water specification from external lubricants, approved cleaning agents and shutdown preservation. State the source water and component requirements so no-additives language does not obscure necessary water-quality management.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the system pressure-drop calculator to combine component losses, elevation and a design allowance. Use the lifecycle cost calculator to compare capital, recurring service, replacement and residual costs over the same study period.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Request filtration and water-quality requirements for the supplied pump and power unit, including the rating method, filter location and service criteria.

Explore water hydraulic pumps and water hydraulic power units.

What should filter and water-condition records tell the operator?

Record any cleaning or preservation treatment and the return-to-service procedure. Obtain compatibility guidance before introducing an unapproved chemical to solve deposits or biological growth.

A pressure-loss budget can include filter loading, but it does not select filter media or predict water chemistry and fouling.
