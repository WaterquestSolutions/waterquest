Marine and Food Approvals: What Evidence to Ask For

Material certificates, component approvals and installation acceptance answer different questions. A clear matrix speeds approval of an oil-free installation.

Safety & Compliance7 min read

Download the worksheet for this guide (editable text) · Open the specification requirement register (CSV)

Written by WaterQuest Solutions · Updated 2 November 2026 · About the distributor

Get the evidence that fits your application

Three different kinds of approval evidence

Ask for material traceability, configuration-specific component approval and installation acceptance as separate records. A material certificate or a supplier’s quality-management certificate does not replace class, flag-state or food-process acceptance.

A supplier should therefore respond with identified documents and their scope rather than a broad assurance.

This distinction helps buyers ask a productive question: what evidence is needed for this installation, and which part of it does the supplier provide? The result is a clearer procurement process and a more credible case for using water where its oil-removal benefit is valuable.

Distinguish material evidence from equipment approval

A material certificate may establish the identity or properties of a supplied material. It does not necessarily establish the suitability of the complete component for a particular function.

Ask what each document actually covers.

The review should also distinguish supplier declarations from independent certification and from site acceptance. Each may have a legitimate role, but they are different forms of evidence.

Define the marine function and vessel context

Marine requirements depend on the vessel and the role of the equipment. A noncritical deck mechanism and a function essential to vessel safety can have different review needs. Identify the vessel category, operating context and machinery function before asking whether a component is approved. Transport Canada's construction and equipment framework provides relevant context, including differences in applicability. Its small-vessel guidance addresses another part of the requirements landscape. These sources help frame the enquiry, while the responsible vessel professionals determine the actual route for the project.

Classification, owner requirements and the integrator's scope may also affect the evidence package. A supplier's experience with marine customers can be useful, but experience alone does not identify which approval applies to the specific vessel function or which acceptance tasks remain after the component is delivered.

Read approval scope rather than relying on a logo

An approval mark or logo can indicate that a document exists, but it does not explain its boundaries.

Pay attention to attachments, limitations and the difference between a component and a complete assembly. A certificate for one pump does not automatically cover an HPU assembled with unrelated valves, controls and accessories.

This is not a reason to reject modular equipment. It is a reason to assign integration responsibility and identify the evidence required at each level.

Identify the food-process exposure pathway

Food applications need a similarly precise boundary. The word food use does not describe them adequately.

Map where the hydraulic equipment is located and where a release could go.

CFIA's preventive-control guidance places equipment and operations within the food business's broader controls. The facility's responsible team must determine the applicable requirements and evidence for its process. Removing hydraulic oil can address a particular contamination pathway, but the project still needs to assess the proposed water circuit and the way it interacts with the food environment.

Do not equate potable supply with sanitary machinery

Water entering from an accepted potable source does not automatically retain that status after circulating through industrial equipment. The circuit's suitability must be assessed at the actual point of potential contact.

CFIA's water guidance explains the importance of suitable water for its intended food-related use. That principle should not be extended into an unsupported statement that every water-hydraulic discharge is acceptable for product contact. A hydraulic pump's operating-fluid specification answers a different question from a food process's water requirements.

Likewise, stainless construction is not synonymous with sanitary design. Accessibility for cleaning, crevices, external surfaces and the complete arrangement can matter.

Use a requirements matrix to expose gaps

A simple matrix can connect each project requirement to the evidence and responsible party. For a marine project, entries might concern equipment identity, function, environmental exposure and the applicable acceptance route.

For each requirement, record whether evidence is supplied, under review or still required. Avoid filling an unresolved cell with a broad brochure statement.

This approach also helps procurement compare suppliers fairly. It gives the owner a practical way to decide which questions must be resolved before ordering and which belong to later integration acceptance.

Keep component and system responsibilities explicit

The supplier may provide component data while an integrator designs the complete hydraulic arrangement. Another professional may be responsible for the vessel or food-process assessment. These scopes can work well together if the interfaces are explicit.

Identify who confirms fluid compatibility, who establishes protective functions and who submits or reviews the complete installation evidence. General hydraulic guidance such as ISO 4413 can inform the system design, but it does not replace the application's specific acceptance requirements.

A responsibility gap often appears when each participant assumes another has checked the same interface. This is especially useful for custom assemblies where individually documented parts must still be shown to work together within the required operating and approval basis.

Preserve approval status when the configuration changes

A later substitution can affect the relevance of the original evidence. A different seal, instrument, coating or control arrangement may appear minor but still require review.

The handover documents should identify the accepted configuration and the process for assessing changes. Maintenance staff need reliable part identification so that an apparently equivalent item is not installed without the necessary compatibility or approval check.

Do not assume that a certificate in the asset folder remains sufficient indefinitely regardless of modifications.

Ask for evidence that supports a clear answer

A useful supplier enquiry names the equipment, application, working fluid, operating conditions and required evidence.

Where documentation is available, WaterQuest should present it with its scope. Where confirmation is needed, the proposal should identify the next action and who is responsible.

Water hydraulics deserve consideration where a supported design removes a meaningful oil exposure while meeting the required service.

For executives, this clarity reduces commercial uncertainty. The organization can see whether the project is ready for procurement, needs a targeted review or requires a different configuration.

Retain the final evidence register with the operating documents.
