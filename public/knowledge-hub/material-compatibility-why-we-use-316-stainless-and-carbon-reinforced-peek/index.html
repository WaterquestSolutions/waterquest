Material Compatibility: Why We Use 316 Stainless and Carbon-Reinforced PEEK

Build reliability at the interfaces between materials.

Reliable water hydraulics depend on the surfaces that carry loads and slide against each other. Learn how 316 stainless steel, carbon-reinforced PEEK and suitable seals can work together, with material selection tied to the actual water and operating conditions.

Article 053Components & Materials10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Pair structural strength with surfaces designed for water

Stainless steel can provide the structure and corrosion resistance a water hydraulic component needs. Carbon-reinforced PEEK can serve selected bearings, guides or wear interfaces. Their value lies in the pairing: the exact grades, contact surfaces, load and working water must suit one another. This is how material selection supports dependable motion without relying on oil in the working circuit.

The title's reference to 316 stainless steel and carbon-reinforced PEEK describes an important design approach, not a claim that every WaterQuest product contains the same pair. Exact construction varies with the component and duty. Buyers should request the material schedule for the offered configuration. This is particularly important when comparing freshwater, seawater and chemically treated water. A visible stainless housing and a general polymer description cannot establish the composition of hidden springs, sliding parts, seals or fasteners that may govern service suitability.

Understand the structural role of stainless steel

A pressure-containing body must withstand its mechanical duty while maintaining the geometry required by the moving parts inside it. Material strength, product form, machining and assembly all matter. A component may need to resist repeated loading, local stresses and external mounting forces as well as internal pressure. The pressure rating of the completed design comes from more than the alloy name. It depends on dimensions, manufacturing quality and the applicable assessment of the assembly.

Stainless steel can also provide corrosion resistance through its surface behaviour, but that resistance depends on the environment and grade. Outokumpu describes several corrosion mechanisms and the conditions that influence them. [1] For a hydraulic designer, the practical implication is to examine the actual water, temperature, crevices and deposits. A grade that performs well in one freshwater installation may not be the appropriate choice for continuous saline exposure. The material must be selected for the service, and the component must still be designed as a pressure assembly.

Treat 316 as a candidate with a defined envelope

The 316 family is widely recognised, which can make it easy to specify without enough context. The designation does not mean that every form, fabrication condition or assembled geometry behaves identically. Threads, stagnant pockets and gasketed joints can create local conditions unlike the freely exposed surface of a test specimen. External salt deposits may create another exposure distinct from the internal working fluid. A useful enquiry identifies these conditions instead of asking only whether the main body is stainless.

Where the environment is demanding, another alloy or a revised geometry may be appropriate. The selection should explain the mechanism being addressed rather than simply rank materials by cost. For example, avoiding a deposit trap may be as important as changing the nominal grade. Request the complete wetted path and identify any dissimilar-metal contacts. This helps ensure that a small overlooked component does not undermine an otherwise carefully selected assembly. It also gives maintenance teams a clear basis for choosing approved replacements rather than visually similar alternatives.

PEEK is a family of grades and formulations

PEEK is an engineering thermoplastic used in demanding components, but the name does not identify a single set of properties. Unfilled, fibre-reinforced and wear-modified grades can behave differently. Carbon reinforcement can alter stiffness and strength, while other additives may be selected for friction and wear. Victrex's technical information distinguishes these formulations and their test conditions. [2] A buyer should therefore ask for the exact supported grade or controlled material specification where that information is relevant to the component's function.

Do not assume that a carbon-reinforced grade automatically has the best friction behaviour for every water-lubricated contact. Reinforcement, wear additives, manufacturing and counterface interact. A property measured on a standard specimen answers a narrower question than whether an assembled sliding part will remain stable through the required duty. The supplier's design and component-level evidence bridge that gap. This is why a material substitution that appears stronger on a datasheet can still alter clearance, wear or counterface behaviour in an established hydraulic design.

Separate fluid pressure from contact stress

The pressure of the working water is not necessarily the pressure between two touching solid surfaces. A bearing or sliding element supports forces through its contact geometry. Local contact stress depends on load distribution, area, alignment and deformation. Edge loading can create a more demanding condition than a simple average calculation suggests. The material selection must address that mechanical contact, not merely the nominal hydraulic pressure printed on the component's catalogue page.

An independently calculated example illustrates the distinction. A 1,000 newton load distributed uniformly over 100 square millimetres gives an average contact pressure of 10 megapascals (1,450 psi). The example is not a permitted limit for PEEK or a WaterQuest component. Real contact may be non-uniform and change during motion. Its purpose is to show why contact geometry belongs in the material review. The designer needs the relevant load, speed, temperature and support conditions to judge a polymer interface, even when the surrounding water pressure is already known.

Evaluate the pair rather than the polymer alone

A sliding material works against another surface. Counterface hardness, finish, shape and condition influence friction and wear. A rough or damaged surface can change performance even when the polymer grade is unchanged. Conversely, a polished appearance does not by itself establish the correct functional finish. The required surface specification belongs to the component design and should be preserved during manufacture and repair. Reworking a counterface without that specification can change the interface in ways a visual inspection does not reveal.

The fluid and contamination are also part of the contact system. Particles can enter a clearance, damage a surface or become involved in the wear process. Water condition and filtration therefore support the material design rather than replace it. A well-chosen pair cannot compensate indefinitely for uncontrolled debris or misalignment. For procurement, ask how the assembled interface was evaluated and under which conditions. The answer should connect the materials to the operating duty instead of relying solely on a general statement that the polymer has low friction.

Allow for temperature and time-dependent behaviour

Materials change dimensions and mechanical response with temperature. A polymer element and its metallic support may not expand at the same rate. The resulting clearance can influence leakage, friction or assembly stress. The relevant temperature is the operating condition of the component, including local heating and cold starts, not only the nominal ambient temperature. Selection must consider the combined geometry over the permitted range rather than compare isolated room-temperature properties.

Time under load matters as well. Polymer deformation can depend on duration, temperature and stress. A brief material test does not automatically establish dimensional stability during a long hold. Victrex's properties guide provides grade-specific data with stated conditions, which should be interpreted for the question being asked. [3] For a hydraulic component, the designer must determine whether the supported geometry and operating cycle remain acceptable. A material's high headline temperature capability should not be mistaken for permission to operate an entire pump, seal system or valve at that temperature.

Manufacturing turns a material selection into a component

The route from raw material to finished part affects what is delivered. Machining, moulding, reinforcement orientation and surface preparation can influence dimensions and behaviour. The supplier should control the processes relevant to the design rather than treat the material purchase as the end of quality assurance. For critical parts, traceability should connect the specified material and manufacturing requirements to the actual component. The extent of documentation should match the application and contractual requirements.

Assembly adds further considerations. A correctly made polymer part can be damaged by inappropriate installation force or placed against a contaminated surface. A stainless part can acquire surface damage or contamination during handling. These are practical reasons to use controlled procedures and approved service parts. They also explain why field substitution is not simply a matter of ordering a similar-looking ring in the same named polymer. The completed interface includes dimensions, finish and assembly condition that may not be evident from a spare part's appearance.

Ask for evidence at the level of the claim

Different evidence answers different questions. A material datasheet supports understanding of a grade under its test conditions. A drawing defines geometry and tolerances. A component test can demonstrate performance for an assembled design under stated duty. Operating experience adds information about a particular installation and maintenance history. None should be used to imply more than it establishes. A credible technical submission explains how these layers support the proposed service and identifies material uncertainty that still needs resolution.

The Water Hydraulics Company's purpose-designed pump information illustrates that water-service construction is selected as an assembly rather than through a generic fluid substitution. [4] For a WaterQuest enquiry, request current configuration-specific support for the intended fluid and duty. If service life is important to the business case, ask how it was assessed and what operating conditions apply. Avoid accepting an unlimited-life implication from a corrosion-resistant metal or a high-performance polymer. The useful promise is a supported application with understandable limits and maintenance expectations.

Preserve the material decision throughout ownership

Record approved water conditions, cleaning methods and replacement parts with the asset information. Review a changed water source or treatment chemical before treating it as an ordinary consumable substitution. Keep inspection evidence when wear or corrosion appears so that the cause can be investigated. The response may involve contamination, alignment, fluid conditions or an incorrect part rather than a failure of the material family itself. Good records help distinguish these possibilities and prevent repeated replacement without learning.

The commercial value of stainless steel and engineered polymer pairing is practical: it can enable hydraulic machinery to work with water where removing oil has value. That case is stronger when the exact component configuration and operating envelope are clear. WaterQuest should help buyers connect material names with the jobs those materials perform, the evidence supporting them and the procedures that preserve the design. The result is an informed selection based on the complete interface, not confidence borrowed from two familiar material labels.

From understanding to equipment selection

Specify chemistry and contact together

Assess the wetted metals, polymer grade and sliding counterface as an assembly. State chloride exposure, temperature, deposits and electrical contact between dissimilar metals; naming 316 stainless and PEEK alone leaves important design conditions undefined.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the surface-finish calculator to convert finish units and examine an explicitly assumed Ra-to-Rz relationship. Use the lifecycle cost calculator to compare capital, recurring service, replacement and residual costs over the same study period.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Review the offered wetted materials and sliding interfaces on the pump or cylinder configuration, then provide the actual water chemistry and external exposure.

What should inspection reveal about the selected material pairing?
