The Musqueam Hydrogen Vessel Concept: An Engineering Discussion

Explore the auxiliary-equipment questions behind a vessel concept.

Early vessel concepts benefit from clear engineering questions. Use this discussion to explore where water hydraulics could fit a hydrogen-vessel concept, with proposed functions kept separate from demonstrated performance or approved designs.

Article 076Industry Applications10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Explore the vessel’s energy and auxiliary duties together

A hydrogen-vessel concept creates an opportunity to consider how auxiliary equipment will move, as well as how the vessel will be powered. Water hydraulics could remove oil from selected deck or valve circuits while providing controlled force. This discussion explores those design questions; it is a concept, not a record of a commissioned, funded, approved or tested Musqueam installation.

The distinction matters because a community name carries responsibilities beyond a technical diagram. Project status, ownership, participation and endorsement should come from authorised project records. A concept can help an owner examine possibilities, but it should not be used to infer the decisions or intentions of Musqueam or any vessel operator. The discussion below therefore considers a hypothetical fishing-vessel architecture and the evidence that would be needed to advance it responsibly.

Separate the propulsion decision from the auxiliary decision

Propulsion moves the vessel through water. Auxiliary machinery may operate fishing gear, lifting equipment, steering-related mechanisms or service systems. Changing the propulsion energy source does not automatically determine how those auxiliary functions should work. A vessel can have electric propulsion and oil hydraulics, conventional propulsion and water hydraulics, or a different combination. The best arrangement depends on the duty and interfaces of each function.

This separation prevents a misleading all-or-nothing debate. If removing hydraulic oil from one deck circuit addresses an important contamination pathway, that option can be evaluated on its own merits. Conversely, selecting hydrogen as part of an energy strategy does not prove that every hydraulic function should use water. The engineering team should identify where the decisions interact through electrical capacity, machinery space, cooling, controls and operating procedures, then assess those interactions explicitly.

Trace energy through the complete chain

In one possible concept, stored hydrogen supplies a fuel-cell system, electricity reaches a distribution system, and electric drives operate propulsion or auxiliary equipment. Other architectures differ. Each conversion stage has losses and operating limits, while storage and supporting equipment occupy space and add mass. The vessel's required range and service schedule must be assessed using the complete architecture rather than only the efficiency of one component.

A water-hydraulic auxiliary circuit adds another energy path: an electric motor drives a pump, pressure and flow reach control equipment, and an actuator moves the load. The mechanical work at the end of that path is smaller than the electrical input because losses occur along the way. Water does not supply free energy; it transmits energy supplied by the drive. This is a basic but essential distinction when discussing a low-emission vessel concept.

Define the fishing duty before choosing machinery

A fishing vessel's auxiliary demands may be intermittent, highly variable and operationally important. A hauler can experience a different load pattern from a winch, hatch or handling mechanism. The design brief should describe the load, speed, direction, holding requirement and duration of each movement. Peak demand and simultaneous operation matter because they determine the supply capacity and can affect other onboard services.

The operating crew should contribute to this definition. Their experience can reveal awkward recovery situations, deck obstructions, service constraints and practices that a simple equipment schedule misses. The goal is not to preserve every inherited arrangement, but to understand the function it serves. A new technology should improve a well-understood task rather than ask the crew to compensate for an incomplete specification through improvisation at sea.

Hydraulic force still follows pressure and effective area

For a linear actuator, ideal force depends on pressure acting over effective area, with opposing chamber pressure included. Changing the working medium does not change that relationship. The actual delivered force also depends on friction, mechanical geometry and operating conditions. OpenStax provides the underlying pressure-and-force explanation. [4]

This makes water hydraulics a credible candidate for suitable force-intensive functions, but it is not proof that an existing oil-service actuator can be refilled. Pumps, valves, seals and sliding interfaces need support for the intended water duty. The Water Hydraulics Company's water-pump information illustrates that dedicated equipment has fluid and installation requirements. [3] A concept should identify candidate equipment families and unresolved compatibility questions before presenting a compact drawing as a complete solution.

Identify the oil pathway that would actually disappear

Deck machinery can place hydraulic lines and actuators near catch, working surfaces and the surrounding water. Removing hydraulic oil from a selected circuit changes the substance that can escape from that circuit. The value of the change depends on the inventory, leak location and potential destination. A circuit above an exposed handling area presents a different opportunity from a well-contained system in a machinery space.

The boundary should be stated plainly. Converting a deck circuit does not mean the entire vessel is free of lubricants, fuels or other potential contaminants. Gearboxes, bearings and separate machinery may retain their own lubrication systems. This precise description is more useful to owners and crews than an unqualified oil-free label, because it shows both the improvement and the remaining inspection, containment and housekeeping responsibilities.

Fresh water and seawater are different engineering services

A vessel has ready access to seawater, but that does not make seawater automatically suitable as the hydraulic working medium. Salt content, biological material, suspended contamination and temperature affect construction and operation. A freshwater circuit may require storage and management, while a seawater design requires explicit support for its exposure. The choice influences the complete system, including instruments, valves and service practices.

A recirculating arrangement also differs from a once-through arrangement. The former retains and manages a working inventory; the latter creates a continuous supply and discharge question. Water consumption, contamination control and the route of a leak must be evaluated for the chosen architecture. The vessel concept should not hide these differences behind a single blue line labelled water, because those differences determine practical installation and operating requirements.

Stored energy and suspended loads remain important

A pump stopping does not necessarily remove energy from a hydraulic system. Raised gear, a loaded mechanism, a pressurised chamber or an accumulator can continue to influence motion. The required behaviour after loss of power must be defined for each function. Holding, controlled lowering and moving to a safe position are different objectives, and one arrangement may not serve every condition.

The design must also consider crew access during recovery and maintenance. Manual controls should not create an unexpected movement or expose someone to a suspended load. These issues belong to the complete machinery and operating assessment. Removing hydraulic oil can address one hazard pathway while leaving the mechanical consequences of force and stored energy essentially unchanged. A responsible concept keeps both ideas visible instead of treating a non-oil medium as a complete safety argument.

Hydrogen introduces its own integration questions

Hydrogen storage, delivery, ventilation, detection and emergency arrangements require specialist assessment within the selected vessel architecture. The hydraulic supplier should not claim that water-based auxiliary machinery resolves those requirements. The interfaces may include power availability, machinery-space layout, control signals and operating restrictions, but each system retains its own design responsibilities.

An owner should ask which organisation coordinates the overall integration and which approvals apply to the vessel category and intended service. Transport Canada's construction and equipment guidance illustrates the importance of determining the applicable framework rather than assuming one rule covers every vessel. [1] Small-vessel construction guidance is separately relevant where that category applies. [2] The cited construction-and-equipment bulletin expressly excludes fishing vessels and pleasure craft from those regulations, illustrating why category matters. These public resources orient the enquiry; they do not establish approval of the hypothetical concept described here.

Build an evidence sequence before promising outcomes

A practical development sequence begins with a duty study, proceeds through an architecture review and resolves critical uncertainties in controlled tests. Bench testing may assess a component or circuit under representative loads. Shore-based integration can examine controls and power interactions. Any onboard trial requires the appropriate authorised plan, responsibilities and operating limits. Success at one stage should not be reported as proof of every later stage.

For the hydraulic portion, useful evidence includes motion performance, leakage behaviour, temperature, energy consumption and maintainability over a relevant cycle. For the vessel, additional evidence is needed for stability, range, operational capability and the complete safety arrangement. The test programme should define what would count as an acceptable result and what would trigger redesign. A concept becomes stronger when uncertainty is converted into specific questions that can be answered.

Evaluate operating capability alongside emissions

An emissions discussion needs a stated boundary. The vessel's onboard operation, the production and delivery of its energy carrier, equipment manufacture and maintenance are different accounting scopes. It is possible to improve one without having established the result for all the others. The concept should identify the information required for the intended claim instead of describing an entire lifecycle through a single propulsion label.

Operating capability is equally important. Consider fuel availability, turnaround time, spare parts, technician access and the consequences of an unavailable component during the fishing season. System-level energy assessment helps connect machinery choices to actual duty rather than isolated peak-efficiency values. [5] An arrangement that fits the owner's service pattern and support network has a stronger adoption case than one that depends on optimistic assumptions about every future operating condition.

Give the owner a decision rather than a promise

A useful concept package would show the proposed functions, energy paths, equipment boundaries, unresolved questions and staged verification plan. It would also state what is already known, what is estimated and what has not been authorised. That lets the owner decide whether to investigate further without confusing educational discussion with a committed project or commercial guarantee.

WaterQuest's relevant contribution is to explain where purpose-designed water hydraulics could perform a specified auxiliary function and reduce a defined hydraulic-oil exposure. The wider vessel strategy belongs to the owner and its authorised engineering partners. Respecting that distinction supports a serious conversation about cleaner marine machinery. The opportunity is compelling when it joins practical deck performance with credible evidence, clear community and ownership context, and an honest account of the work still required.

From understanding to equipment selection

Use a vessel concept as a design exercise

For a hydrogen-vessel concept, separate propulsion and fuel-system requirements from hydraulic deck functions. Define a specific hatch, ramp or winch duty before considering water components, without treating the concept as verified fleet performance.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the cylinder force calculator to compare extension and retraction with opposing pressure and the required load. Use the lifecycle cost calculator to compare capital, recurring service, replacement and residual costs over the same study period.

Connect the calculation to equipment

For linear actuation, review cylinder configurations; for a seawater pumping duty, review the seawater pump range. Confirm the working fluid for each circuit separately.

How should marine exposure and lay-up affect maintenance?

Evaluate crew access, spare parts and lay-up preservation for the proposed circuit. Keep expected benefits labelled as design estimates until operating measurements are available.
