Duplex Stainless Steel: Benefits in Marine and Corrosive Environments

Understand where duplex materials can support marine service.

Marine material selection must balance strength, corrosion resistance and practical fabrication. Learn where duplex stainless steel can offer advantages and how the exact grade and service conditions guide the decision.

Article 067Components & Materials9 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Select an alloy for the exposure it will face

Selected duplex stainless grades combine strength with corrosion resistance that can be useful in demanding water service. Their ferritic and austenitic microstructure distinguishes them from other stainless families. Review the exact grade against the working water, temperature and external exposure, then apply that selection across the relevant components and connections. [2]

Start with the exposure the material must resist. Internal seawater, salt deposits, warm stagnant water and treated freshwater create different demands. Add the component’s mechanical job, such as containing pressure or supporting a moving surface. These conditions guide the exact duplex grade, fabrication requirements and supporting material data.

Understand why strength can be valuable

Higher material strength can offer design opportunities, but the benefit depends on which limit governs the component. A pressure-containing part may be governed by stress, stiffness, fatigue, geometry or a manufacturing requirement. A stronger alloy does not automatically permit a thinner wall or higher operating pressure. The completed design must be reassessed using the applicable properties and requirements. Simply substituting an alloy in an existing drawing can leave other limiting features unchanged.

For example, a valve body's minimum section may be constrained by machining, thread engagement or dimensional stability rather than a basic stress calculation. A cylinder component may need stiffness to maintain alignment even when its strength is ample. The material advantage should therefore be evaluated at component level. Ask what design objective duplex is intended to improve and what evidence demonstrates that improvement. This turns a broad statement about strength into a useful engineering decision instead of assuming that a stronger material always produces a better hydraulic assembly.

Match corrosion resistance to the actual mechanism

Corrosion can be general or local, and different mechanisms respond to different conditions. Chloride-related pitting, crevice conditions and stress corrosion concerns are not interchangeable. Outokumpu's corrosion guidance explains the importance of environment and mechanism in stainless selection. [1] A grade may offer improved resistance to a relevant mechanism while remaining vulnerable under a different exposure. Identify the actual concern rather than treating corrosion resistance as one number that ranks every material for every service.

Temperature, deposits, stagnation and local geometry deserve particular attention in water equipment. A clean exposed surface does not experience the same condition as a narrow joint or a pocket beneath a deposit. The design should minimise avoidable adverse geometries where practical and select the material for the conditions that remain. This is a coordinated decision. Upgrading the alloy while preserving an unnecessary trap may be less effective than addressing both the local environment and the material. The preferred solution should explain which mechanism it is intended to control.

Specify the grade and delivered condition

Duplex grades differ in composition and intended performance. The product form and delivered condition also matter. Procurement should identify the exact specification required for the component rather than allow a generic duplex substitution. Where traceability is important, define the documentation and identification needed to connect the supplied material to the requirement. A certificate should be reviewed for the actual product and relevant properties, not accepted merely because it contains the word stainless.

The completed component may contain several materials. A duplex body can still include springs, plugs, seats, seals or fasteners with different exposure limits. Review the entire wetted path and the external connections. The governing limitation may be a small internal part rather than the main pressure body. A complete material schedule makes this visible and gives service teams a basis for approved replacement. Without it, an apparently harmless maintenance substitution can change the compatibility that justified the original selection.

Fabrication must preserve the intended properties

Material selection does not end when stock arrives. Forming, machining, welding and heat treatment can influence the delivered component. Duplex fabrication requires appropriate procedures for the selected grade and product, particularly where thermal processing affects the material condition. The project should rely on qualified manufacturing practice and relevant inspection rather than assume that a familiar stainless fabrication method transfers unchanged. The exact requirements depend on the component and applicable design framework.

This is a reason to assess the supplier's capability as well as the alloy's properties. Ask how critical fabrication requirements are controlled and which records accompany the part. Avoid prescribing unsupported numerical process limits from a general article; use current grade-specific technical information and the qualified procedure. For a buyer, the practical point is straightforward: a material with attractive reference properties only provides the intended value if the manufactured component retains the condition on which the design relies. Quality assurance should follow that chain from specification to finished assembly.

Surface condition and geometry remain important

A component's surface can be affected by fabrication residues, mechanical damage and contamination during handling. Appropriate finishing and cleaning should follow the material and equipment requirements. A rough or damaged region may also influence a sliding or sealing interface independently of corrosion. Do not assume that improved alloy selection makes functional surface specifications unnecessary. The component must still provide the geometry and finish needed by the seal, bearing or moving part it supports.

Design access for inspection and cleaning where deposits are expected. The ability to maintain a surface can influence lifecycle performance. A hidden crevice that cannot be inspected presents a different ownership problem from an accessible connection, even if both use the same grade. Consider how the equipment will be installed and serviced, including contact with other metals and process residues. The goal is to preserve the intended environment and surface condition throughout use, not merely deliver a clean-looking component on the day of installation.

Interpret reference data with its test conditions

Corrosion tables and laboratory results can help screen materials, but they do not directly predict the life of an assembled pump or valve. The tested solution, temperature and exposure conditions may differ from the site. Real components add crevices, stress, deposits and operating cycles. Outokumpu explicitly discusses limitations in using corrosion-table data for actual service. [3] Treat the data as evidence for a defined comparison, then determine what additional configuration-specific support is needed.

A successful installation elsewhere also has a boundary. Ask whether it used the same grade, water, temperature, geometry and maintenance approach. A broad statement that duplex works in seawater omits too much to support a consequential purchase. Where uncertainty is important, a suitable test or monitored application can provide evidence for the actual conditions. Define the observation and acceptance criteria in advance. This lets the owner address uncertainty systematically without either dismissing a promising material or accepting an unsupported service-life claim.

Keep dissimilar materials and replacement parts in view

A duplex component may connect to other stainless grades, copper alloys, coated parts or different fasteners. The combination can introduce corrosion and mechanical considerations that require review. Evaluate the actual electrical, fluid and exposure paths rather than relying on a simple hierarchy of noble materials. Any isolation or protection measure must remain suitable for the pressure, load and service arrangement. A corrosion solution that compromises connection integrity is not a complete engineering solution.

Maintenance documentation should identify permitted replacements and any restrictions on substitution. A readily available fitting may match size and thread while changing the material combination. The same applies to repair methods and welding work. Establish who reviews changes that affect the original basis. This is particularly important at remote sites where urgent repairs encourage improvisation. A clear approved-parts strategy helps preserve compatibility while making service practical, reducing the chance that a carefully selected duplex component becomes part of an unassessed mixed-material assembly.

Compare lifecycle value with realistic assumptions

Duplex may have a higher or lower total ownership cost than another supported option depending on material availability, fabrication, inspection and service consequences. Do not judge only the raw material price. A material that reduces a credible corrosion burden may justify additional initial cost, while an unnecessary upgrade may add complexity without meaningful benefit. The comparison should identify the problem being solved and the conditions under which the benefit is expected.

Keep expected recurring costs separate from uncertain failures. If longer service is part of the justification, state the evidence and remaining uncertainty. Include replacement availability and the local ability to perform approved repairs. A component that is technically excellent but difficult to replace during an outage may require a spare strategy. The lifecycle case becomes useful when it connects material performance to the owner's actual maintenance and availability needs rather than turning an attractive alloy property into an unverified financial saving.

Make the material decision traceable to the application

A complete enquiry should provide internal fluid, external exposure, temperature, pressure, mechanical loads and expected operating pattern. Identify stagnant periods and cleaning or preservation methods. Ask the supplier to explain the offered grade, fabrication condition and supporting evidence. Record any limits and unresolved items before manufacture. This gives the project team a defensible basis for selecting duplex where its properties address the real duty.

Duplex stainless steel can be a valuable option in selected marine and corrosive environments, but its benefit comes from correct grade selection, manufacture and installation. Engineers need a mechanism-based assessment, procurement needs exact identification and operators need maintainable construction. The persuasive argument is therefore a complete material decision that improves the application, not a claim that one stainless family eliminates corrosion or makes every water circuit suitable for seawater.
