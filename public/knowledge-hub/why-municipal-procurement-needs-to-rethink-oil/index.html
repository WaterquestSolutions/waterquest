Why Municipal Procurement Needs to Rethink Oil

Make room for water hydraulics in a performance-based tender.

Procurement decisions shape maintenance and environmental exposure for years. Learn how municipal tenders can consider water hydraulics through measurable service requirements, lifecycle cost and clear evidence from suppliers.

Article 096Economics & Funding10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Use renewal to improve the specification

Replacing an ageing gate actuator or HPU is a chance to solve familiar problems rather than carry them into the next asset. Describe the required service, oil exposure and maintenance constraints, then invite a water hydraulic option where it fits. This gives procurement a clear basis for comparing the benefit, equipment and long-term support.

A responsible review starts with the public function and the asset's condition. Water hydraulics may be one option where hydraulic force remains appropriate and removing oil has a meaningful benefit. Electric actuation or an improved conventional arrangement may also deserve consideration. The procurement process should make those alternatives comparable through outcomes and evidence rather than through an unexamined preference for either continuity or novelty.

Define the service before naming the equipment

State what the mechanism must achieve: load, travel, movement time, operating frequency, holding and recovery. Describe the process conditions that influence those requirements. A water-control gate may face changing differential levels or debris, while a modulating valve has a different pattern of movement. The specification should connect the actuator to the mechanism and structure it serves.

Engineering references on water-control structures can help frame the relationship between mechanisms and civil works. [3] The project's responsible designer still needs the actual site criteria. Procurement should avoid using the old actuator's nameplate as the only duty description, because it may reflect a historical selection rather than the current requirement. A clear functional brief allows suppliers to explain how their proposed arrangement meets the same public-service need.

Record why oil is being reconsidered

Identify the affected oil inventory and potential routes of leakage. Include normal servicing as well as a damaged hose or seal. A mechanism above an open channel may justify a different exposure assessment from equipment in a contained room. This evidence gives the project a concrete reason to assess water hydraulics rather than presenting oil removal as a universal objective detached from location.

Canadian pollution-prevention guidance provides context for releases that could affect receiving water. [2] The procurement brief should not imply that a water circuit creates unrestricted discharge permission. It should describe the particular oil pathway being removed and retain appropriate controls for remaining fluids and mechanical hazards. That precision helps evaluators distinguish a genuine application benefit from a broad environmental claim that cannot be checked.

Use performance requirements to support fair comparison

A performance-oriented brief identifies the required result and the evidence needed to demonstrate it. This can allow different technical approaches to be assessed where the municipality's procurement rules and project needs permit. The responsible procurement team should determine the appropriate process and wording; this article does not prescribe legal tender requirements or override local policy.

The engineering contribution is to make necessary constraints explicit and avoid unnecessary ones. If a particular medium is required because of a defined exposure, explain the boundary and rationale. If alternatives are allowed, state how they will be compared. Ambiguity can produce bids based on different assumptions, leaving evaluators to reconstruct the intended scope after closing. Clear requirements make competition more useful by directing it toward the service the municipality actually needs.

Price the complete installed arrangement

Bid comparisons should include supporting utilities, controls, pipework, access, commissioning and training. A package price is not a completed-project price if essential site work is excluded. Water supply, freeze protection or fluid management may be relevant to one offer; containment or cooling may be relevant to another. The actual proposed design determines the scope.

A schedule of inclusions and exclusions helps evaluators reconcile these differences. Identify who supplies each interface, who installs it and who confirms its operation, especially when civil, electrical and mechanical work are delivered under separate project contracts. It should identify reused equipment and the evidence supporting reuse. If an existing assembly cannot be confirmed compatible with a proposed water circuit, leave that uncertainty visible rather than assigning a nominal allowance that disappears inside the total. A complete scope reduces the risk that the preferred bid becomes materially more expensive during detailed integration.

Make lifecycle criteria transparent before evaluation

Where lifecycle cost forms part of the procurement assessment, define the period, operating schedule and cost categories consistently. Include energy, routine service, consumables and relevant planned replacements. Shared facility costs should only be counted as avoided where the project genuinely removes them. A conversion of one small circuit may not eliminate an entire waste-handling service or oil-storage area.

The evaluation should distinguish evidence from assumptions. Measured consumption and current service quotations have a different basis from preliminary estimates. System-level pumping guidance supports assessing the full operating arrangement rather than comparing isolated pump efficiency values. [4] A transparent method allows bidders to provide useful information and gives the municipality a defensible explanation of how long-term ownership influenced the decision.

Include operators in the design review

Operators understand the practical conditions under which equipment is exercised, inspected and recovered. Their input can identify access problems, awkward isolation points and service tasks that are difficult during adverse conditions. Involving them before the scope is fixed can prevent the municipality from buying a technically capable package that is unnecessarily burdensome to maintain.

The review should examine the work, not only personal preference for familiar equipment. A new technology may require training but offer a better arrangement; an existing approach may have support advantages worth retaining. Record the operational reasons behind comments so the design team can respond concretely. This turns consultation into useful engineering evidence rather than a general vote for or against change.

Standby and emergency duties need explicit evidence

Municipal mechanisms may operate infrequently yet be important during an abnormal event. Their procurement requirements should address readiness after standby and the consequences of unavailable power or supply. A successful movement immediately after installation does not demonstrate every condition relevant to a seldom-used asset. The acceptance and maintenance basis should reflect the intended service.

Define recovery arrangements and the responsibilities for confirming them. A manual backup may be useful only if staff can access and operate it under the conditions in which it is needed. Likewise, redundancy should be assessed against actual common dependencies. These details can distinguish a resilient proposal from one that merely includes a spare component without establishing how the public function is maintained when the main system is unavailable.

Ask for configuration-specific documentation

The delivered package should have clear equipment identification, drawings, supported operating limits and service instructions. Approval documents should apply to the actual configuration and intended use. A supplier quality certificate or a related model's document is not automatically evidence for the complete installation. The public scope of ISO 4413 provides hydraulic-system context but does not establish project compliance. [5]

Municipal assets can outlast the people who purchase them, so documentation is part of the long-term service capability. It should support replacement-part identification and review of future substitutions. If a water-compatible seal or valve is essential to the design, that requirement should remain visible to a contractor working years later. Otherwise, an apparently minor replacement can undermine the original engineering basis.

Define acceptance before the purchase is complete

Acceptance criteria should connect to the functional brief and identify how performance will be demonstrated. Some checks belong at the factory, while others require the installed mechanism and process interfaces. Record the conditions represented by each test and the method for handling design conditions unavailable during commissioning, such as a particular water level.

The handover should also verify deliverables that affect operation: alarms, position feedback, access, labels, training and records. A package can move correctly while still leaving important responsibilities incomplete. Assign an owner and completion requirement to deferred items. This makes acceptance a controlled transfer into service rather than a ceremony that closes the purchase while operators inherit unresolved integration work.

Make innovation proportionate to uncertainty

An unfamiliar technology does not automatically require a large research programme. If suitable equipment has evidence for the proposed duty, ordinary engineering verification may be sufficient. Where an uncertainty could materially affect service, a limited study or pilot may be worthwhile. The purpose should be to answer a defined question, not to delay every new option indefinitely or to demonstrate novelty for its own sake.

A staged approach can protect public funds by matching commitment to evidence. Establish the uncertainty, the test or analysis needed and the decision that follows the result. This gives procurement a clear route for progressing a promising option while retaining accountability. It also helps suppliers understand what information would make their offer acceptable, rather than leaving them to respond to a vague concern that the technology is not yet familiar locally.

Explain the decision in public-service terms

A strong decision record states the required service, the alternatives considered, the evidence and the reasons for selection. Environmental benefits, ownership cost and operating capability should be visible without being exaggerated. Professional sustainability guidance supports considering broader consequences within engineering practice, while leaving the technology choice to the project's actual circumstances. [1]

WaterQuest can contribute a supported water-hydraulic option and a clear explanation of where oil removal matters. The municipality's task is to choose the arrangement that best serves the asset and community over its life. Rethinking oil is valuable when it prompts that disciplined review. The outcome should be dependable infrastructure, fewer avoidable exposures where practicable and a purchase decision that engineers, operators, finance staff and elected officials can all understand.

After the first operating period, review whether the promised service and ownership assumptions were realised. Retain the findings for the next procurement rather than allowing each renewal to restart from anecdote. This creates a municipal evidence base that can improve both conventional and alternative technology decisions over time.
