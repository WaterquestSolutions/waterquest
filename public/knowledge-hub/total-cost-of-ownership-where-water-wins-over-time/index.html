Total Cost of Ownership: Testing the Case for Water

Find where oil removal changes the lifetime operating cost.

A water hydraulic option earns its place when it delivers the required service at a worthwhile total cost. Compare installation, energy, maintenance, downtime and oil exposure using a common operating baseline.

Article 097Economics & Funding10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Build the ownership case around the work you avoid

Removing hydraulic oil can remove oil changes, top-ups and waste handling from a selected circuit. A total-cost comparison puts those changes beside installation, electricity, water management, maintenance and retirement costs. Use the same required service and study period for each option so the proposed water system’s value can be assessed clearly.

The useful question is where the water option could win and which assumptions control that conclusion. A project with difficult oil handling may have a different cost structure from one dominated by electrical consumption. Another may be driven by access and interruption rather than fluid expense. Identifying the dominant costs allows the team to test the case efficiently instead of building a large model whose most influential assumptions remain unexamined.

Keep the useful service constant

Before comparing costs, establish the load, movement, schedule and availability each alternative must provide. A less expensive system that operates more slowly or requires longer interruptions may not deliver the same service. Those differences can be acceptable, but they should be valued explicitly rather than hidden in the comparison. The owner should know what changes along with the price.

This is particularly important when replacing ageing equipment. The realistic alternative might be a new like-for-like package, continued operation with repairs or another actuation technology. Each has a different starting condition. Do not compare a new water package with a conventional option assumed to operate forever without renewal, or with one assigned every historical defect of the old installation regardless of what a replacement would correct.

Find the cost drivers before refining small inputs

A first-pass ledger should include installed cost, energy, routine service, planned replacements and relevant interruption. Then identify which categories dominate or differ materially between alternatives. A small uncertainty in a large cost can matter more than a large percentage error in a minor consumable. This ranking guides the next engineering or commercial enquiry.

For example, a remote installation may spend more on access and specialist attendance than on working fluid. In that case, the service model deserves close attention. A continuously operating package may be more sensitive to electrical input at its normal duty. System-level pumping guidance supports examining the full operating arrangement when assessing energy. [1] The objective is to understand the asset's economics rather than assume every application shares the same savings mechanism.

Separate avoidable costs from allocated costs

An accounting report may allocate a share of facility overhead to a machine, but converting that machine does not necessarily remove the expenditure. Waste collection, storage space, staff travel or central maintenance resources can remain because other equipment still needs them. Count only the change the project can reasonably produce, while explaining any broader strategic benefit separately.

This distinction can materially affect an oil-to-water comparison. Removing one circuit's oil consumption is a direct change. Eliminating the entire site's oil-handling programme is a different claim that requires a much wider conversion boundary. A credible ownership model identifies costs as eliminated, reduced, unchanged or introduced. That classification helps finance understand cash effects and helps operations identify whether the proposed savings correspond to tasks that will actually disappear.

Test the break-even operating saving

A simple threshold calculation can reveal how demanding the cost case is. Suppose the water option has an assumed installed premium of $12,000 and the owner examines eight years without discounting or residual value. The option needs an average net operating advantage of $1,500 per year to offset that premium under this simplified method. These are invented teaching values, not a quotation or financial recommendation.

The team can then ask whether documented energy, labour and consumable differences plausibly reach that threshold. If the case depends on $4,000 of annual savings while the whole relevant operating budget is only $2,000, the estimate needs correction. A threshold does not prove the preferred option, but it converts a broad claim of lower ownership cost into a concrete question that available records and supplier information can help answer.

Examine how utilisation changes the result

Some costs increase with operating hours or cycles, while others follow calendar time or remain fixed. Energy commonly depends on time in particular operating states. Travel for an annual inspection may not change when production hours rise modestly. Component replacement may depend on several factors rather than one linear usage rule. The model should preserve those distinctions.

Create a small number of coherent utilisation scenarios. In a high-duty case, update energy and any usage-sensitive service assumptions together where justified. In a low-duty case, retain calendar-based obligations and standby consumption. This can show why water wins in one application but not another with similar equipment. The conclusion becomes more transferable when the owner understands the operating conditions that make it true.

Access and repair time can outweigh part price

The cost of replacing a component includes finding the fault, reaching the equipment, controlling energy, performing the work and restoring service. A lower-priced part may create a higher total repair cost if it requires difficult disassembly or specialist attendance. Conversely, a more expensive replaceable assembly may reduce interruption if it can be exchanged safely and efficiently.

Ask suppliers to describe the service procedure and the support model for the actual installation. Clarify which work the owner's team can perform and what tools or training are needed. Spare-part lead time also matters where the asset is critical. These practical details make maintainability a financial input grounded in work, rather than an adjective added to a proposal without evidence of how the equipment will be serviced.

Evaluate replacement assumptions without inventing life guarantees

Expected service life depends on configuration, duty, fluid condition and maintenance. A material's laboratory properties do not establish the replacement interval of a complete component. Dedicated water-equipment documentation helps define supported operating conditions, but a project-specific life estimate requires an appropriate basis. [2] Where that basis is limited, uncertainty should remain visible.

Use replacement scenarios to assess whether the recommendation is robust. A model might compare an earlier and later refurbishment event rather than claim an exact future date. If the preferred option changes under a modest shift, obtaining better evidence may be worthwhile. If the conclusion remains similar across a reasonable range, the decision may be less sensitive to that uncertainty than reviewers initially assume.

Avoid double counting interruption and risk

Downtime can create lost production, temporary service costs and repair expenditure. Some categories overlap depending on the owner's accounting method. Agree the treatment before adding them. For instance, a production-loss estimate may already include certain idle resources; charging those again can exaggerate the cost. Planned work during an existing outage also differs from a new unplanned interruption.

Major release events should usually remain explicit scenarios unless reliable data support an expected-value calculation. Removing oil from a defined pathway can have substantial value, but inventing a precise annual incident cost weakens the financial case. The water option retains other possible consequences, including mechanical failure and working-water damage. A fair analysis describes what changes and what remains, rather than using asymmetric assumptions to force a preferred answer.

Include the cost of preserving the operating condition

The water option may need particular supply quality, filtration, temperature protection or other supporting arrangements. These are part of ownership where required by the selected design. The oil option has its own fluid and environmental requirements. Neither should be presented as a bare component package while the other's supporting tasks are fully costed.

Changes to those conditions can affect both performance and cost. A later water-source change or an altered duty may require review. The owner should understand the commitment needed to preserve the supported envelope. The public scope of ISO 4413 provides hydraulic-system context, but it does not establish a universal maintenance budget. [3] The cost model should follow the real equipment and operating plan rather than a generic claim about the medium.

Use sensitivity results to choose the next action

A sensitivity review should lead to a decision, not merely another chart. If energy uncertainty dominates, measure the baseline and obtain duty-matched data for the proposed package. If installation scope dominates, resolve interfaces and exclusions. If service logistics dominate, obtain a clearer support quotation or maintenance demonstration. The next action should target the assumption with the greatest decision value.

Sometimes the result supports a limited pilot before full deployment. The pilot should have a defined question and a way to interpret the result. It may test service access or operating performance rather than repeat a basic demonstration that water can transmit force. The aim is to spend a proportionate amount resolving uncertainty before committing to the larger ownership obligation.

State where the water case is strong

A defensible conclusion identifies the conditions under which the water option provides value. It may combine a manageable installed premium, lower avoidable service costs and removal of a consequential oil exposure. Another project may favour a different arrangement. The lesson is not that water must win every comparison, but that the owner should understand the mechanism behind the result rather than accept a universal cost-saving claim.

WaterQuest can support that understanding through a complete scope and transparent performance information. Engineers can assess the operating assumptions, procurement can compare responsibilities and finance can test the thresholds. After installation, actual costs should be compared with the model under the actual observed operating duty. This turns total cost of ownership from a sales promise into a practical learning tool that improves the next equipment decision.

The final record should retain the assumptions that would trigger reconsideration, such as a major change in operating hours or the loss of a local service arrangement. That makes the analysis useful beyond the purchase meeting and helps future managers recognise when an originally sound conclusion needs updating.
