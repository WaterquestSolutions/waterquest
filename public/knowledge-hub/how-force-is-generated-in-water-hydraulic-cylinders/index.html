How Force Is Generated in Water Hydraulic Cylinders

Calculate the cylinder force available to do useful work.

Cylinder force comes from pressure acting over an area. Learn why push and pull forces differ, how pressure on the return side changes the result and how to use the calculation when choosing a water hydraulic cylinder.

Article 006Start Here10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Use piston area to turn water pressure into force

A water hydraulic cylinder converts pressure acting over its effective piston area into a push or pull. Increasing that area provides more ideal force at the same pressure difference. Check both cylinder chambers, return pressure and friction to understand the force available at the rod, then connect it to the load through the mounting and mechanism.

The pump supplies the circuit with flow and the ability to operate within a pressure range; it does not assign an arbitrary force to the rod. The load, valve arrangement and pressures on both sides of the piston determine the hydraulic force available. Mechanical friction and other effects then influence the force transmitted to the driven mechanism. OpenStax's discussion of hydraulic pressure provides a useful introduction to the underlying relationship. [1]

For a buyer, this means that the cylinder bore, rod diameter and operating pressures belong together in the selection. A catalogue's maximum pressure alone cannot tell you whether a gate will open, a clamp will hold or a mechanism will move through its complete travel. Begin with the required function and use the calculation to connect it to a supported assembly.

Identify the two effective piston areas

On the cap end of a typical single-rod cylinder, pressure acts over the full piston area. On the rod end, the rod occupies part of that area, leaving an annular working area. If the bore diameter is D and the rod diameter is d, the full area is πD²/4 and the annular area is π(D² − d²)/4. Use consistent units before multiplying either area by pressure.

The difference is not a minor detail. At the same chamber pressure, the smaller annular area produces a smaller force contribution. It also requires less fluid volume for the same length of movement. A double-acting cylinder can therefore have different extension and retraction behaviour even when the pump and command remain unchanged.

Draw the piston with the two chambers labelled before calculating. Show the desired direction and the pressures acting on the corresponding areas. This simple sketch makes the signs clear and reduces the chance of calculating a full-area force while overlooking pressure on the opposite side. For an unfamiliar actuator design, confirm its actual internal arrangement rather than assuming that every product follows the same single-rod geometry.

Calculate a net force with pressure on both sides

Take an illustrative cylinder with a 50 millimetre bore and a 30 millimetre rod. Its full piston area is approximately 0.0019635 square metres; its annular area is approximately 0.0012566 square metres. Suppose the cap-end pressure is 80 bar (1,160 psi) and the rod-end pressure is 10 bar (145 psi) during extension. The ideal cap-end contribution is about 15.71 kilonewtons, while the opposing contribution is about 1.26 kilonewtons.

Subtracting those contributions gives an ideal net extension force of approximately 14.45 kilonewtons. This is the hydraulic result before friction and design allowances. If the calculation had ignored the rod-end pressure, it would have overstated the available force. A return connection is not necessarily at zero pressure, especially when the circuit includes line losses or a load-control function.

Now reverse the pressure assignment for retraction: 80 bar (1,160 psi) on the annular side and 10 bar (145 psi) on the full-area side. The corresponding ideal net retracting force is approximately 8.09 kilonewtons. The same physical cylinder and high-side pressure give a different result because the active areas have changed. These numbers are teaching values, not a recommended component or a certified working load.

Translate cylinder force into the force at the machine

The actuator may push a lever, rotate a gate or move a linkage rather than act directly along the final load direction. In those arrangements, geometry changes the relationship between cylinder force and useful output. The perpendicular distance from a pivot to the cylinder's line of action determines the turning moment contributed by the actuator. That distance can change during travel.

For example, an ideal turning moment of 4,000 newton metres with a perpendicular arm of 0.16 metres (0.525 ft) requires 25,000 newtons of cylinder force. If the mechanism reduces that arm near an endpoint, the required force increases for the same resisting moment. A selection based only on the most favourable position can therefore fail to complete the movement despite appearing adequate at mid-stroke.

Include the process load as well as geometry. A gate may see a changing pressure difference across its face, seal contact or accumulated deposits. A lifting linkage may change its moment arm as it moves. Provide a load-versus-position description where possible and identify uncertain regions. The hydraulic calculation becomes useful when it is connected to the actual mechanism, not when it is applied to an isolated nominal load.

Account for the movement conditions that change demand

Starting, accelerating, running and stopping are distinct parts of a motion. Breakaway resistance can differ from resistance after movement has begun. Acceleration requires a force contribution associated with the moving mass, while a load that drives the actuator can require controlled restraint rather than additional pushing force. A single static number cannot describe all of these conditions.

The specification should explain which parts of the sequence matter most. A process valve may need a reliable first movement after a long dwell. A production mechanism may require repeatable acceleration and settling. A descending load may need controlled speed and stopping under changing resistance. These requirements influence the circuit and control design as well as the nominal cylinder size.

Avoid hiding every uncertainty inside one unexplained multiplier. State what the allowance is intended to cover and which requirements need measurement or a representative test. This allows the mechanical designer, hydraulic supplier and owner to review the same assumptions. It also helps prevent excessive sizing that creates a new problem with speed, installation space or energy use.

Understand what a larger bore changes

At a given pressure, increasing the bore increases the full piston area and therefore the ideal force contribution. Because area depends on diameter squared, doubling a bore diameter gives four times the full area. The change also increases the volume needed for a given stroke. More force at the same pressure does not arrive without consequences for flow and component size.

With a fixed supply flow, the larger area moves more slowly. If the process requires the original movement time, the circuit needs correspondingly more flow, subject to the complete design. That can affect pump selection, valves, lines and input power. Comparing cylinders only by force can therefore conceal the system changes required to preserve the intended production sequence.

The same reasoning helps procurement assess alternatives. A lower-pressure proposal with a larger actuator may be technically sensible, but its dimensions, flow requirements and mounting loads need review. A smaller high-pressure proposal has different constraints. Neither arrangement can be selected from a force number alone; compare complete configurations against the same motion and installation requirements.

Connect force with stroke volume and travel time

Using the illustrative 50 millimetre bore, a 400 millimetre extension displaces approximately 0.785 litres (0.207 US gal) into the cap-end chamber. An ideal supply flow of six litres per minute would fill that volume in about 7.85 seconds. On the annular side, the same stroke corresponds to approximately 0.503 litres (0.133 US gal), giving an ideal retraction time of about 5.03 seconds at the same chamber supply flow.

These volume calculations are separate from the net-force calculation, but the two must be satisfied at the same operating point. A pump that delivers the required flow only at a lower pressure may not achieve the calculated movement under load. Conversely, sufficient pressure with inadequate useful flow can move the load too slowly. Ask for the supported simultaneous pressure and delivery conditions.

Return flow also follows the chamber geometry. During retraction, the full-area chamber can discharge more volume per second than enters the smaller annular chamber. The return path must be assessed for its actual flow rather than assumed equal to pump delivery in every direction. That is one reason a complete circuit review is necessary even when the cylinder calculations themselves are straightforward.

Check the mechanical structure and failure behaviour

Hydraulic force capability is only one limit on the assembly. The rod, piston connection, mountings, pins and supporting structure must withstand the relevant loads. A rod under compression may require a buckling assessment based on its unsupported length and end conditions. Side loading and alignment can affect guidance and sealing behaviour. These checks belong to the mechanical design, not to the pump's pressure rating.

The circuit must also deliver the required response after an interruption or failure. Removing power does not necessarily secure a load or remove stored energy. Holding, lowering, isolating and recovering the mechanism require an architecture appropriate to the process. ISO 4413 addresses hydraulic systems as a safety subject; the public scope is a starting reference, while an actual design needs the applicable requirements and equipment-specific information. [2]

Acceptance should therefore include the complete operating sequence and the relevant controlled failure-state demonstrations. A successful extension against a test load is useful, but it does not establish safe holding, stopping or maintenance access. Make each required function explicit so that the supplier and owner agree what evidence will demonstrate it.

Use the calculation to make a better enquiry

Provide the required travel, load behaviour, movement time, mounting arrangement and operating schedule. Include the water source and environmental conditions so that the supplier can connect mechanical requirements to an appropriate water-service configuration. Product documentation such as the Water Hydraulics Company's pump information demonstrates why operating limits and fluid conditions remain part of selection even after the ideal cylinder arithmetic is complete. [3]

Water hydraulics become a compelling option where the supported assembly can meet these requirements while removing hydraulic oil from a sensitive part of the process. The force calculation is valuable because it makes that conversation testable: the owner can see what must be delivered, which assumptions remain open and how the installed equipment will be accepted.

From understanding to equipment selection

Calculate net force at the chambers

For a double-acting cylinder, account for supply pressure acting on one effective area and return pressure acting on the opposing area. Check extension and retraction independently before applying the project's load and mechanical allowances.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the cylinder force calculator to compare extension and retraction with opposing pressure and the required load. Use the system pressure-drop calculator to combine component losses, elevation and a design allowance.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Review bore, rod, stroke and mounting options together with the valve and return path. Request a configuration that covers the loaded motion in both directions.
