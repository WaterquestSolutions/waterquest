Performance Comparisons

Efficiency of Water Hydraulic Pumps Compared to Oil Pumps

Find the efficiency that matters at your operating point.

Pump efficiency affects the power bill, but the most useful comparison is at the flow and pressure your machine actually uses. Learn how to read efficiency data and account for part-load and standby operation.

Article 015Performance Comparisons10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Compare efficiency where your pump will run

Water’s low viscosity creates opportunities to reduce flow resistance, while purpose-built clearances and surfaces manage leakage and friction. Assess those effects at the flow, pressure and speed your machine needs. Pump efficiency compares shaft input with hydraulic output; a power-unit comparison also includes the motor, drive and time spent waiting between movements.

The operating condition matters just as much. Pressure, speed, temperature and the selected fluid can influence performance. A best-point result may be useful for describing a pump, yet say little about a machine that spends most of its time elsewhere on the operating map. Comparing an ideal value from one product with an installed electrical measurement from another is not a fair test.

For water versus oil, begin with the useful duty that must be delivered. Then compare supported components at that duty using consistent definitions. Water's properties create design opportunities and challenges, but they do not provide a universal efficiency ranking. The evidence should show what the selected equipment does under stated conditions and what that result means for the owner's actual schedule.

Establish theoretical and delivered flow

For a positive-displacement pump, theoretical flow follows displacement per revolution multiplied by shaft speed. An illustrative pump displacing five cubic centimetres per revolution at 1,500 revolutions per minute has a theoretical flow of 7.5 litres per minute (1.98 US gpm). If its measured outlet delivery is 6.9 litres per minute (1.82 US gpm) at a stated condition, the corresponding volumetric efficiency is 92 percent.

This example does not describe a particular WaterQuest product. It demonstrates that the delivered result can differ from the geometry-based estimate. Internal leakage and the actual operating condition affect useful delivery. The theoretical number is a reference for the calculation, not a promise that every revolution transfers that full volume to the outlet under every pressure and temperature.

Record where flow is measured. A measurement downstream of a bypass or another branch may not represent pump outlet delivery. It may instead be the useful flow available to an actuator. Both can be valuable measurements, but they answer different questions. A diagram of the measurement boundary prevents circuit behaviour from being incorrectly attributed to the pump's internal volumetric performance.

Calculate hydraulic output from the pressure difference

Hydraulic output power at a defined pump boundary is pressure difference multiplied by delivered volume flow. With bar and litres per minute, dividing their product by 600 gives kilowatts. A delivered flow of 6.9 litres per minute (1.82 US gpm) across a 120 bar (1,740 psi) pressure difference represents 1.38 kilowatts (1.85 hp) of hydraulic output. The inlet pressure belongs in the pressure-difference definition where it is significant to the calculation.

Suppose the measured shaft input for that illustrative condition is 1.8 kilowatts (2.41 hp). Overall pump efficiency would be about 76.7 percent. That result is different from the 92 percent volumetric efficiency in the preceding example because mechanical and other losses also contribute to shaft power. High volumetric delivery alone does not establish high overall pump efficiency.

A simple arithmetic check is to preserve the boundary in every term. Do not use pressure measured at a distant actuator with flow measured before a bypass and call the product pump output without accounting for the intervening circuit. The calculation may still produce a plausible percentage, but its physical meaning would be unclear. Consistent locations and units make the result auditable.

Add the electrical boundary when comparing power units

The motor and drive require electrical input to produce shaft power. In the illustrative example, if measured electrical input is two kilowatts while hydraulic output is 1.38 kilowatts (1.85 hp), the electrical-to-hydraulic ratio is 69 percent. The pump-only figure remains about 76.7 percent because it uses the smaller shaft-input boundary. Neither result contradicts the other; they describe different parts of the energy path.

A purchasing comparison should state which boundary matters to the owner. Utility consumption is an electrical measure, while pump development may reasonably focus on shaft performance. If a package contains cooling, circulation or control equipment, identify whether those loads are included. An omitted auxiliary load can make one offer appear better even when the required installed service is equivalent.

Use suitable instruments and a measurement method that accounts for the actual electrical supply and drive arrangement. A motor nameplate value is not automatically its measured input during the test. Similarly, a catalogue efficiency value at one condition should not be silently substituted for measured operation at another. Separate measured, calculated and assumed quantities in the test record.

Compare a useful operating map, not one attractive point

A pump may operate across several pressure and flow demands within a single machine cycle. Its performance at the dominant duty is important, but starts, low-load operation and other required conditions may also influence the installed result. Request information that covers the proposed operating range and identifies which parts are supported by measurements for the selected configuration.

Plotting or tabulating several conditions can reveal a trade-off that a single maximum number hides. One design may perform well near a particular duty but less favourably elsewhere. Another may offer a broader useful range. The appropriate choice depends on how the machine will operate, not on which product has the highest isolated catalogue value.

The Water Hydraulics Company's P3 page provides performance information for an identified water-service product alongside its operating context. Use current supplier curves for the selected configuration and confirm their conditions. Do not extend one model's information across an entire product range or treat a graph as a guarantee for an installation with different fluid, inlet or control conditions. [2]

Include the energy consumed while the actuator is waiting

Peak efficiency can be a poor predictor of annual energy where the machine operates intermittently. A unit that runs briefly and then waits may consume a significant share of its energy outside the loaded movement. The standby architecture, auxiliary loads and control sequence therefore belong in the assessment. A pump comparison becomes a system comparison as soon as those functions are included.

Consider a hypothetical daily schedule with 20 minutes of operation at two kilowatts and 460 minutes at 0.2 kilowatts (0.268 hp). The first state consumes about 0.667 kilowatt-hours; the second consumes about 1.533 kilowatt-hours. Total daily consumption is approximately 2.2 kilowatt-hours for the stated eight-hour period. Despite its lower power, the waiting state accounts for more energy because it lasts much longer.

These are teaching assumptions, not typical values for water or oil equipment. Their purpose is to show why measured duty states matter. Reducing movement losses and changing standby behaviour are different improvements. Attribute the benefit to the change actually made rather than describing every reduction as an inherent consequence of the working fluid. DOE's system-level pumping guidance supports this broader operating assessment. [1]

Understand the role of viscosity without oversimplifying it

Viscosity influences resistance to flow and leakage through internal paths. Water's lower viscosity relative to many hydraulic oils changes the balance that the pump designer must manage. It can reduce some losses while increasing the importance of clearance and contact design. The resulting efficiency belongs to the engineered component and operating condition, not to the liquid considered in isolation.

Use consistent temperature and units when discussing the fluids. Dynamic and kinematic viscosity are different quantities linked through density. A comparison that mixes them or compares different reference temperatures can exaggerate the apparent difference. Water-property formulations such as the IAPWS reference provide a scientific basis within a defined range, while the chosen oil needs its own applicable data. [3]

Temperature can also change the operating result during a test. A short measurement taken before conditions stabilise may not represent sustained duty. Record the relevant fluid condition and explain whether the test covers cold starts, steady operation or both. If the application spans a significant range, the proposal should identify how the supported pump performance changes across it.

Design the test to separate improvements from uncertainty

A useful comparison holds the required output and test conditions consistent while measuring the inputs and delivered function. Identify the instruments, their relevant accuracy and the expected magnitude of the difference. If the apparent efficiency improvement is similar in size to the measurement uncertainty, the result should not be presented as a precise established advantage without further work.

Repeat observations where needed to distinguish a consistent effect from variation in load, temperature or control sequence. Keep the raw conditions connected to the reported totals. A single headline percentage can conceal a changed movement time, an unloaded test or a different fluid temperature. The record should allow a reviewer to understand why the two measurements are comparable.

The acceptance plan should also cover functionality. A more favourable energy result is not sufficient if the machine fails to meet movement, holding or availability requirements. Define those requirements first, then assess energy within the set of solutions that satisfy them. This keeps the comparison focused on useful service rather than rewarding a low input obtained by doing less work.

Put efficiency beside the other reasons for selecting water

Energy is one part of the decision. The owner may also value removal of hydraulic oil from a location where a release affects a process or receiving environment. Installation, maintenance, water management and spare-parts support influence the total result. Keep these benefits and costs explicit so that an energy estimate is not asked to justify the entire project by itself.

A water-hydraulic proposal can be attractive even where the measured energy advantage is modest or not yet established, provided its other application-specific benefits justify the complete investment. Equally, an unsupported claim of major efficiency improvement can weaken an otherwise credible case. Present the measured result, explain its boundary and identify what remains to be demonstrated at site.

WaterQuest can help translate the operating schedule into a comparison that engineers, procurement and management can all inspect. The strongest efficiency argument shows the required output, the selected equipment, the measurement conditions and the resulting energy use. It gives the owner a basis for acceptance and later review, rather than a percentage that cannot be connected to the machine's actual work.

From understanding to equipment selection

Compare efficiency at matched duty points

Compare pumps at the same delivered flow, pressure difference, speed and water condition. Separate volumetric loss from torque-related loss; a single peak-efficiency figure does not describe operation over an intermittent duty cycle.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the pump sizing calculator to estimate displacement, drive torque and shaft power from your flow, pressure and efficiency assumptions. Use the hydraulic efficiency calculator to separate volumetric, mechanical and overall efficiency using measured operating data.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Compare the pump family and the P6 model page as starting points, then check the relevant model's operating data against the actual duty. The P6 link is an example, not an automatic recommendation.

Which measurements help separate pump deterioration from circuit problems?
