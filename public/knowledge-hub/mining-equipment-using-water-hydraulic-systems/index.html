Mining Equipment Using Water Hydraulic Systems

Match water hydraulic power to the mine’s operating reality.

Mining equipment must keep moving through demanding production schedules. Explore where water hydraulics can serve a defined machine function while removing hydraulic oil, with water quality, abrasive exposure and maintenance access addressed from the start.

Article 022Industry Applications10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Build the mining package around the production duty

A processing gate, handling fixture and underground mechanism face different loads and environments. Choose a defined task where water hydraulics can deliver the required force and remove an oil burden near the process. Then bring supply conditions, debris protection, access and support into the selection so the equipment fits the way the mine operates.

An opportunity may exist where hydraulic force is valuable and oil exposure creates a particular concern near process water, material streams or sensitive locations. Removing oil from that working circuit changes one source of contamination or combustible inventory. It does not establish that the entire machine becomes environmentally harmless, fire-safe or suitable for a hazardous area. Those broader questions require the complete equipment and installation to be assessed.

A site-specific screening exercise can identify suitable candidates without committing the mine to a fleet-wide change. Begin with an asset whose load, operating sequence and maintenance history can be understood. Evidence from a bounded application is more valuable than a broad forecast based only on the fact that mining already uses substantial quantities of water.

Separate process water, spray water and hydraulic working water

A mine may have several water networks with different purposes and quality. Process water can carry dissolved constituents or suspended material. Dust-control water is selected and delivered for spray performance and exposure control. Hydraulic working water must satisfy the requirements of the pump, valves, seals and actuators through which it circulates. One network's usefulness does not prove its suitability for the others.

NIOSH's engineering-control material describes water sprays as one method used in dust-control arrangements. That is relevant process context, not evidence that the same water should supply precision hydraulic machinery. [2] A spray system discharging water into the environment and a recirculating actuator system also have different flow balances. Their shared use of pressurised water should not obscure those differences.

Obtain a representative analysis of the proposed hydraulic supply and its variation. Identify what happens when the source changes, a treatment stage is unavailable or a connection is made during field service. If existing mine water cannot satisfy the equipment requirements economically, a controlled separate supply may be necessary. The cost and operating responsibility of that arrangement belong in the feasibility comparison.

Convert rough service into defined load cases

Descriptions such as heavy duty or severe service are useful signals, but they are not sufficient selection data. Identify forces, travel, speed, reversals, holding periods and the frequency of demanding events. Distinguish planned mechanical work from shock loads or obstruction conditions. A cylinder should not be selected by its static force rating alone when its supports, rod stability or end-of-stroke behaviour may govern serviceability.

A process mechanism can experience changing resistance as solids accumulate or operating conditions vary. Agree which resistance the actuator must overcome and which condition should trigger a stop or intervention. Increasing available force can damage another part of the machine if the load path was not designed for it. Hydraulic protection and mechanical limits should be coordinated, with responsibility for the complete mechanism clearly assigned.

Record the basis of each input. Measured production data, supplier calculations and operator observations provide different types of evidence. All can be useful when labelled honestly. A short measurement campaign may resolve a critical uncertainty about cycle frequency or pressure demand before the owner spends money on a replacement sized around an unrepresentative estimate.

Protect internal machinery from the external environment

A water hydraulic circuit can be controlled internally while its components remain exposed to dust, grit, washdown and impact. Rod surfaces, seals, connectors and service openings need attention to that external environment. Dirt introduced during a repair can defeat careful fluid preparation upstream. Contamination control therefore includes maintenance practice and physical protection, not only a filter specification.

Consider the route by which abrasive material could enter or damage each assembly. Exposed rod travel near a transfer point differs from a valve manifold inside a protected enclosure. The design may need guards, suitable positioning, accessible cleaning or an alternative mounting arrangement. These decisions should preserve service access rather than enclosing equipment so tightly that inspection becomes impractical.

Purpose-designed water pumps such as the manufacturer's Janus P3 demonstrate that water service involves deliberate component engineering. [1] Their existence does not make ordinary oil components interchangeable. Ask for the exact permitted water, filtration, inlet and duty conditions, then verify that the installed arrangement can maintain those conditions in the mine's real environment.

Evaluate the supply where the machine operates

A fixed processing installation may have stable electrical power and a maintained water supply. A mobile or remote machine may need to carry fluid, tolerate changing orientation and operate far from workshop support. The difference affects tank arrangements, make-up logistics, protection against freezing and the practical cost of service. Avoid transferring conclusions from a stationary package directly to mobile equipment.

At the pump inlet, examine the complete path under the lowest relevant supply condition. Long hoses, restrictive components, temperature changes and source fluctuations can alter what the pump actually receives. A satisfactory reading at an upstream header may not describe the inlet during peak demand. Instrumentation and protective functions should address the condition at the equipment boundary that matters.

Shared water services introduce dependencies. Another user may reduce available pressure or change supply quality. The project should establish whether the hydraulic duty requires an independent supply or a defined priority. An apparently inexpensive connection becomes a poor bargain if ordinary plant operations repeatedly place the actuator outside its supported operating envelope.

Match control strategy to production and standby

The supply arrangement should reflect the time spent moving, holding and waiting. A continuously running pump may waste energy during long idle periods, while an aggressively stopped supply may fail to provide the required response. Determine the allowed delay, starting frequency and holding behaviour before choosing a control strategy. The process schedule is an engineering input, not merely an assumption added to a cost spreadsheet.

Measure energy at a consistent boundary. Include necessary boosters, drives, cooling and water-management equipment when comparing complete alternatives. The DOE pumping sourcebook supports this system perspective, but it does not establish a universal efficiency advantage for water hydraulics. [4] Report the useful output and operating conditions beside any measured consumption figure.

Thermal behaviour also changes with the schedule. Frequent cycling can accumulate heat, while prolonged shutdown can expose remote branches to cold conditions. A design acceptable during a short factory run may need additional verification for a long production shift or seasonal restart. Specify the relevant states and the evidence needed for each rather than relying on one favourable demonstration.

Keep pressure safety and hazardous-area suitability explicit

High-pressure water can injure people, and machinery can retain energy after its drive stops. Gravity, springs and external loads may continue to act on a hydraulic mechanism. Oil removal does not resolve those hazards. CCOHS hazardous-energy guidance is useful for identifying the range of energies that must be controlled; the approved site procedures determine how work is actually performed. [3]

Some mining locations also have requirements related to ignition sources, electrical equipment or other hazardous conditions. The working fluid is only part of that assessment. A water hydraulic pump connected to an unsuitable motor, sensor or control enclosure cannot be described as approved for a hazardous area merely because the fluid is not mineral oil. Evidence must match the complete relevant configuration and location.

Use the applicable machinery and site requirements to define normal protection, abnormal response and maintenance boundaries. ISO 4413 provides a broad hydraulic-system framework, but its public abstract is not a detailed design review or a mining approval. [5] Assign responsibility for integration so that essential safety functions do not fall between the equipment supplier and the site team.

Plan service around the realities of the mine

Availability depends on more than component durability. Access, isolation, diagnosis, spare parts and restoration all affect downtime. A machine with replaceable modules and clear fault indications may return to service faster than one whose nominal parts life is attractive but difficult to support. Discuss the actual repair sequence with maintenance staff while layout decisions can still be changed.

Identify which spares are configuration-specific and what preparation they need before installation. Record acceptable replacement parts, water-management consumables and relevant settings. A familiar-looking valve or seal may differ in material, leakage or operating requirements. Keeping an accurate equipment schedule reduces the chance that an improvised substitution undermines a successful installation over time.

A remote site also needs a practical escalation route. Define what operators can observe, what information should be recorded and when specialist support is required. This is particularly useful for a new technology: the objective is not to turn every fault into a factory visit, but to make diagnosis disciplined enough that staff do not change pressure or fluid conditions in an attempt to solve an unrelated mechanical problem.

Use a bounded pilot to test the adoption case

Choose acceptance criteria that reflect the candidate duty. These may include completed cycles, movement time, temperature, leakage observations, intervention frequency and water condition. Record operating conditions and distinguish equipment faults from process interruptions. A pilot that reports only hours of operation cannot explain whether the machine performed useful work or spent most of its time waiting.

Retain a credible means of preserving production during the evaluation. The appropriate arrangement depends on the site and could involve an alternative asset, an agreed outage window or another approved operational provision. Set review points and criteria for resolving deviations. These decisions make a trial manageable without pretending that every uncertainty must be eliminated before any field evidence can be collected.

Water hydraulics can be a worthwhile mining option where the selected duty benefits from oil removal and the complete system fits the site's water, load and service conditions. The business case should combine those benefits with measured performance and a realistic support plan. Engineers gain a defensible selection, procurement gains a comparable scope, and operations gains equipment whose requirements are understood before it becomes essential to production.

From understanding to equipment selection

Select for the mine environment and the movement

Define the specific gate, clamp or auxiliary drive duty, including dust, washdown, water quality and installation access. Separate the hydraulic power demand from any process-water service sharing the same site.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the cylinder force calculator to compare extension and retraction with opposing pressure and the required load. Use the motor speed and torque calculator to relate water-motor displacement, flow, pressure and gearing to shaft speed and torque.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Choose cylinder information for linear movement and motor information for rotary duty. Give the supplier the load sequence and environmental conditions before selecting a model.
