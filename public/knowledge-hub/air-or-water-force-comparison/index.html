Air or Water? Comparing Force at 6 and 80 Bar

A 60 mm cylinder gives 1.7 kN at 6 bar and 22.6 kN at 80 bar before losses. Compare the actual pressure, stiffness, supply energy and complete duty.

Choose the supply that serves the movement

Existing utilities are a legitimate factor, but they should not hide the engineering requirements. A nearby air line may lack the flow or stable pressure needed during simultaneous demand.

Pressure and area explain the force comparison

The ideal force on a piston follows pressure difference and effective area. The same basic relationship applies to pneumatic and hydraulic cylinders, although the actual supported pressures and dynamic behaviour differ. A higher operating pressure can provide a required force with a smaller effective area, but it also imposes different component and protective requirements. Compare the available pressure at the actuator during the duty rather than the maximum rating of a remote compressor or pump.

For a teaching example, a 60 millimetre bore has an area of about 0.002827 square metres. With negligible opposing pressure and friction, 6 bar (87 psi) produces approximately 1.70 kilonewtons, while 80 bar (1,160 psi) produces approximately 22.6 kilonewtons. The complete selection must include the rod side, losses, mounting and load.

Compressibility changes the response to a disturbance

Air stores substantial energy through compression and can act like a spring as load and pressure change. A liquid circuit is generally stiffer, although liquid compressibility, hoses, trapped gas and structures still contribute compliance. They do not mean every pneumatic system is inaccurate or every water system is precise.

A simple end-to-end pneumatic duty may not need intermediate positioning at all. The mechanism can travel to a defined stop and provide the required process function.

Trace supply and exhaust or return paths

A pneumatic actuator receives compressed air and usually exhausts it after use. The exhaust path can affect speed, noise and local conditions.

Cylinder geometry also matters. A single-rod actuator has different effective areas in its two directions, changing ideal speed and volume requirements. The relevant gas or liquid conditions must be used consistently when comparing consumption.

Include the compressor behind the air connection

Compressed air requires generation and distribution. The DOE and NREL compressed-air evaluation guidance treats the supply, distribution and demand system together. A local actuator's air requirement is only one input.

The marginal energy effect of a new demand can depend on how the compressor system responds. A brief small load and a continuous load may influence operation differently. Use appropriate measurements or a documented assessment rather than applying an unsupported universal efficiency to every plant.

Include the complete water supply arrangement

A water-hydraulic option needs a compatible pump, controls, actuator and fluid-management arrangement. Depending on the duty, it may also need treatment, boosting, cooling or winter protection. The chosen architecture must be explicit before resource use can be compared meaningfully.

The DOE's pumping guidance supports assessing the whole system and operating schedule rather than one component efficiency. Identify active, holding, idle and shutdown states.

Compare holding and failure response explicitly

A mechanism may need to hold position, move to a defined state or permit supervised recovery when normal supply is lost. Neither pneumatic nor hydraulic actuation provides every such function automatically. The safe state is application-specific.

Stored energy can remain after the primary supply is isolated in either system. A pressure indication at one point does not necessarily describe every trapped region. ISO 4413 provides context for hydraulic systems, while the project must also establish the applicable requirements for the pneumatic alternative. The comparison should include verification of the required response under an approved plan rather than treating a normal powered stroke as proof of behaviour during an interruption.

Evaluate the local environment and maintenance work

Pneumatic equipment may involve air preparation, exhaust noise and moisture management. Water equipment introduces water-quality, compatibility and freeze-protection questions where relevant. The maintenance tasks should be based on the selected configuration and operating conditions, not a general reputation for simplicity.

Consider the skills, tools and spare parts already available at the site. Familiarity can reduce the effort of adopting one option, while standardizing a new application family can improve support over time. Ownership value depends on the installed arrangement that technicians must maintain, not only the apparent simplicity of the component on a bench.

Test motion and energy at the same useful duty

A comparative trial should use the same load, travel, movement time and relevant environmental conditions. Measure the required result directly. Pressure alone does not establish successful movement, and a controller command does not establish position.

Energy measurements need a stated boundary. A pneumatic point-of-use measurement and a water package electrical measurement are not automatically equivalent.

Choose the fit that the owner can support

A plant with an effective air network and modest intermittent force requirements may reasonably choose pneumatic actuation. A duty needing compact high force or a particular liquid-power response may justify a water-hydraulic arrangement.
