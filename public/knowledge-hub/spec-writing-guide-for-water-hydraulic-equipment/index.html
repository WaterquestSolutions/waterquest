Specification & Design

Spec Writing Guide for Water Hydraulic Equipment

Turn the operating need into a clear, testable specification.

A clear specification helps you receive comparable quotations and suitable equipment. Learn how to state force, motion, water quality, controls, documentation and acceptance requirements in terms suppliers can answer.

Article 047Specification & Design9 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Write a brief suppliers can build and you can verify

Describe what moves, its load and travel, how quickly it must operate and how often the sequence repeats. Add holding, stopping and recovery requirements, then specify the water and the oil-removal objective. This gives a supplier the information needed to propose a coordinated package and gives your team a clear basis for acceptance.

Distinguish required performance from preferred implementation. An owner may need a valve to close within an agreed interval, but the best pump size depends on actuator volume and the permitted control arrangement. Conversely, an existing mounting envelope may be a firm constraint. Label both clearly. This gives the supplier room to propose an appropriate supported circuit while preventing a technically attractive offer from overlooking an interface the project cannot change. The result is a specification that encourages useful engineering rather than guesswork.

Describe a duty envelope rather than one operating point

A single force and speed seldom describe the complete duty. Include the highest opposing load, the lowest controlled speed, the frequency of reversals and the longest expected holding period. State whether loads are known measurements, design calculations or estimates awaiting confirmation. The equipment may behave differently at cold start, after prolonged running or after months of inactivity. Describe these states in ordinary operating terms so that they can be translated into selection and testing requirements.

A duty schedule can be simple. It might identify normal travel, seating, standby, maintenance and emergency operation, with the conditions relevant to each. Avoid combining independently occurring extremes into an impossible continuous duty unless that combination is actually required. Equally, do not omit a credible demanding combination merely because it is infrequent. An exceptional operation can still govern motor power, inlet supply, actuator strength or energy storage. The specification should show which conditions must be met continuously and which require a separately defined capability.

Define the working fluid and its supply

Water compatibility is configuration-specific. State the intended source, available analysis and expected variation. Temperature, particles, dissolved constituents and treatment chemicals can affect equipment selection in different ways. Identify whether the circuit recirculates its fluid or uses a once-through supply. Describe make-up, flushing and preservation arrangements where known. If information is missing, make it a required confirmation before manufacture rather than silently assuming ideal water. A supplier cannot responsibly select materials and clearances from the word water alone.

The Water Hydraulics Company publishes purpose-designed water equipment and configuration guidance, illustrating why the fluid and inlet arrangement belong in an enquiry. [2] The project specification should request the limits for the exact offered configuration, including permitted inlet conditions and return or case-drain constraints. Do not copy a limit from one catalogue model into every component schedule. Provide the site conditions and ask the supplier to demonstrate that the selected assembly supports them. This keeps the design boundary traceable when options change during procurement.

State the oil-free boundary precisely

The phrase oil-free can describe different things. It may mean that the working hydraulic circuit contains no mineral oil, that a pump has no oil-lubricated internal stage, or that a particular process area must not be exposed to lubricant release. These are not interchangeable requirements. Identify the fluid path and the contamination route that matter to the project. The specification can then require the supplier to list other lubricated elements, such as drive bearings or mechanical joints, where they affect the objective.

This precision also improves evaluation of alternatives. A water actuator supplied by an assembly with a separately lubricated drive may meet a circuit-level objective while failing a broader process constraint. The issue should be resolved before award, not discovered through disagreement over a marketing term. Ask for a clear description of the offered boundary and any exceptions. A defined requirement is easier to price, easier to verify and more persuasive than a broad promise that different readers interpret in different ways.

Give bidders the interfaces they need to price

Hydraulic equipment connects to the rest of the installation through more than pressure ports. Include electrical supply, control signals, communications, mounting, access, drainage and environmental exposure. Identify who supplies cables, pipework, supports, isolation devices and field instruments. Show the physical limits of the package and the responsibilities outside it. If the project requires an enclosure, heating, cooling or a local operating station, make that visible in the tender scope rather than treating it as an assumed accessory.

Control interfaces deserve particular attention. Define the commands available, the feedback required and the expected response to a missing signal. Separate a request to move from permission to move. State which system owns the operating sequence and which provides protective functions. A pump supplier, controls integrator and owner may each have a role. Requiring an interface schedule helps expose gaps before they become commissioning problems. It also makes bids comparable because each supplier is responding to the same division of work.

Request evidence that matches each important claim

A technical submission should connect performance statements to conditions. For force, identify pressure at the actuator and the effect of opposing pressure. For movement time, identify load, temperature and available flow. For energy, identify the measurement boundary and operating schedule. Manufacturer curves can support selection, but they should not be presented as guaranteed installed performance without the necessary agreement. Ask bidders to identify typical data, calculated predictions and contractual commitments as separate categories.

The same discipline applies to service life and maintenance. A statement that a seal lasts a certain number of hours is not useful without its duty, water condition, loading and failure criterion. Where evidence is limited, specify a verification activity rather than invent a universal requirement that no bidder can substantiate. The DOE pumping guidance provides a useful system-level approach to assessing energy performance, while project-specific measurements establish the actual result. [3] Procurement is stronger when the evidence requested can genuinely answer the decision being made.

Make deviations easy to see

Require a consolidated deviations schedule. Each exception should identify the specification item, the offered alternative and its consequence for performance, cost or ownership. A statement buried in a catalogue attachment should not be the only indication that an important requirement is unmet. This is especially valuable where a supplier proposes a different control arrangement or fluid-management method that could be acceptable if the owner understands it. Transparent departures encourage better choices rather than automatically rewarding the least informative offer.

Define the order of precedence among the specification, drawings, supplier proposal and final agreed documents. Otherwise, contradictory statements can survive into manufacture. Record accepted changes in a controlled design basis and circulate them to the people responsible for affected interfaces. A changed cylinder bore, for example, may alter both mounting loads and required flow. Treating the amendment only as a component substitution can leave the electrical, controls or structural scope based on the original assumption. Good document control preserves the integrity of the whole system.

Write acceptance criteria before testing begins

Factory testing should have a defined purpose, method and record. It may verify assembly integrity, control sequences and selected performance using a representative load arrangement. Site testing then addresses actual piping, mechanism loads, operating controls and environmental conditions. Specify what each stage establishes so that one is not mistaken for the other. Include measurement accuracy and acceptable variation where they influence the result. A vague requirement to demonstrate satisfactory operation leaves too much to negotiation at the least convenient time.

Test abnormal states in accordance with an approved safe plan. Low supply, interrupted power or a missing position signal may require a particular response. The test should verify that response without creating uncontrolled movement or exposure. ISO 4413's public scope reinforces the system-wide nature of hydraulic safety, but a public abstract is not a complete test specification. [1] The responsible project team must define the applicable requirements and methods. Record unresolved deviations with an owner, due date and agreed disposition before final acceptance.

Purchase the information needed to own the equipment

Handover should include installed schematics, component identification, settings, permitted fluid conditions and maintenance instructions. Require a spare-parts list tied to the actual configuration. Generic model-family literature can be useful background, but it does not tell a technician which seal option or control card was installed. Ask for clear instructions covering preservation, return to service and fault diagnosis. These documents should be available in a usable form before training so that operators can learn from the information they will later rely on.

Training needs practical scope as well as attendance. Identify who must understand normal operation, who may adjust settings and who is authorised to perform maintenance. Include access to service support and the information required when reporting a fault. A buyer who purchases only hardware may discover that the missing knowledge is expensive to reconstruct. Water hydraulics are easier to adopt when the procurement package treats documentation, commissioning and support as part of the asset rather than optional paperwork delivered after the equipment invoice.

End with a specification that can survive a change of team

Review the completed document from the perspective of someone who did not attend the project meetings. Can that person identify the required duty, the fluid, the interfaces and the evidence needed for acceptance? Can they distinguish firm requirements from unresolved assumptions? If not, the specification still depends too heavily on informal knowledge. Resolve ambiguous terms and remove requirements that conflict with one another. A shorter clear document is often more useful than a large collection of uncoordinated clauses.

WaterQuest can help translate an operating problem into a supported water-hydraulic scope, but the purchase decision should remain understandable independently of a sales conversation. A well-written specification creates that clarity. It gives engineers a design basis, procurement teams a comparison method and operators a defined asset to maintain. Most importantly, it makes the benefit of removing oil from the selected circuit something the owner can verify alongside the mechanical service the equipment was bought to perform.
