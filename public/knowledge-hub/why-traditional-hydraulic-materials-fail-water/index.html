Why Traditional Hydraulic Materials Fail in Water Systems

Why carbon steel, cast iron, aluminium, NBR seals, polyurethane seals, and standard hydraulic hose all fail in water service , and why a clean-sheet material specification is the only correct response.

These materials work well in oil systems , precisely because oil is what they were designed for. This is not a cautionary tale told to sell new components. It is a materials science explanation that should inform every specification decision made by engineers who design or convert hydraulic systems. The failures are not edge cases. They are predictable, well-documented, and unavoidable.

Carbon Steel: Fast, Aggressive Corrosion

Carbon steel is the dominant structural material in oil hydraulic systems. Hydraulic cylinders, valve bodies, reservoirs, pipe, manifolds, and pump housings are routinely manufactured from carbon steel grades (SAE 1020, 1045, ST52) in oil hydraulic service. In water, it corrodes rapidly.

The corrosion mechanism is straightforward electrochemistry. The corrosion front continues to advance into the steel at rates typically in the range of 0.1 to 0.5 mm per year in aerated, neutral-pH water.

Critical Failure Timeline

A carbon steel hydraulic cylinder barrel with 3mm wall thickness in direct water contact will lose structural integrity within 6 to 30 years from corrosion alone. But long before structural failure, the roughened bore surface produced by early-stage corrosion will destroy seals, contaminate the fluid, and cause system failure within months of initial water contact.

The corrosion products , iron oxide particles released into the water , are themselves destructive. They are hard (Mohs hardness approximately 5.5), abrasive, and rapidly contaminate the hydraulic fluid to the point where filters are overwhelmed. A carbon steel component in a water hydraulic system does not merely corrode in isolation. It contaminates the entire system and accelerates wear of every precision component within it.

Chrome Plating on Carbon Steel

Standard oil hydraulic piston rods are manufactured from carbon steel with hard chrome plating applied to the sealing and bearing surfaces. Does chrome plating protect a carbon steel rod in water service? Only briefly, and incompletely.

Chrome plating is a hard, corrosion-resistant coating , but not a sealed barrier. Micro-cracks and pinholes in the chrome plating allow water to reach the carbon steel substrate. Once water contacts the steel at a pinhole, galvanic corrosion accelerates , the large area of noble chrome acts as a cathode, the small exposed steel area acts as a small anode, and the steel corrodes rapidly at the defect location. This undercutting corrosion expands under the chrome and eventually blisters and spalls the plating off in sheets. The only correct solution for piston rods in water service is solid 316 stainless steel or a solid stainless substrate with HVOF ceramic coating.

Cast Iron: Even More Vulnerable

Cast iron is widely used in oil hydraulic pump housings, valve bodies, and cylinder end caps. In water, cast iron corrodes even more aggressively than carbon steel through a phenomenon called graphitic corrosion. The graphite flake structure of grey cast iron creates internal galvanic cells , graphite is cathodic relative to the iron matrix. The iron matrix corrodes selectively while the graphite flakes remain, leaving a weak, porous graphite residue with no structural integrity.

Catastrophic Failure Mode

Graphitic corrosion of cast iron in water systems can reduce tensile strength by 80% or more while the component retains its outward appearance. Pressure-containing cast iron components in water service can fail catastrophically without visible prior deterioration. Never use cast iron in water hydraulic systems.

Aluminium: Galvanic and Chemical Attack

Aluminium forms its own passive oxide layer in neutral water , similar in concept to stainless steel's passive layer. However, this layer breaks down in hydraulic systems under conditions that are common: pH below 5 or above 8, chloride ion concentrations above a relatively low threshold, and galvanic coupling to more noble metals.

In a water hydraulic system where aluminium components are used alongside 316 stainless steel , exactly the situation that arises when an engineer attempts to save money on some components , the galvanic couple is severe. The potential difference between aluminium and passive 316 stainless (approximately 0.85V) drives rapid galvanic corrosion of the aluminium at any wetted interface.

Additionally, aluminium dissolves chemically in the slightly alkaline water (pH 7.5 to 8.5) that is recommended for water hydraulic systems. The same water chemistry that protects stainless steel slowly dissolves aluminium. There is no correct pH target that protects both materials simultaneously.

Nitrile Rubber (NBR) Seals

NBR is the most common seal material in oil hydraulic systems. In water, NBR absorbs moisture and swells. At operating temperatures of 40 to 60°C, linear dimensional swelling of 3 to 8% is typical for standard NBR grades. This swelling increases interference fits, generates excessive contact pressure, elevates friction to the point of stick-slip behaviour or seal stall, and accelerates mechanical wear.

In extended water service, NBR seals also hydrolyze , the polymer chains are broken by water molecules, reducing tensile strength and elongation and causing the seal to crack and fail. Both mechanisms operate simultaneously in warm water service.

Polyurethane (PU) Seals

Polyurethane seals are among the worst performers in water service. PU is susceptible to hydrolytic degradation: the urethane linkages in the polymer chain are attacked by water, particularly at elevated temperatures and low pH. The degradation is not visible in early stages , the seal maintains its shape while its molecular weight decreases and mechanical properties decline. Then, under pressure, it extrudes catastrophically into clearances and the cylinder fails. Polyurethane seals in water service at 50°C can lose 50% of their tensile strength within 2,000 hours.

Standard Hydraulic Hose: Internal Degradation

Conventional hydraulic hose has a synthetic rubber inner tube , typically NBR or EPDM , surrounded by wire braid reinforcement. The inner tube is not visible in service and its condition cannot be directly inspected without disassembly. In water service, the NBR inner tube undergoes swelling and hydrolytic degradation with two consequences: reduced bore diameter (increasing flow resistance) and generation of rubber particles that contaminate the hydraulic fluid and damage downstream components. The hose appears intact externally while generating internal contamination that destroys other system components.

The correct specification for flexible connections in water hydraulic systems is PTFE-lined hose with stainless steel braid and stainless steel end fittings. PTFE is fully inert to water at all hydraulic operating temperatures and generates no contamination particles.

The Correct Response: A Clean-Sheet Material Specification

The consistent lesson across all these material failure modes is the same: you cannot adapt an oil hydraulic system to water service by changing the fluid and keeping the components. The traditional hydraulic material palette fails in water service through mechanisms that are fundamental to the materials' chemistry and physics.

The correct approach to water hydraulic system design is a clean-sheet material specification: 316L stainless steel for all pressure-containing metal components, PEEK and PTFE composites for all sealing and bearing surfaces, PTFE-lined hose for all flexible connections, and ceramic or hardened stainless for all wear-critical sliding surfaces.

Key Takeaways

  • Traditional hydraulic materials fail in water service through specific, predictable mechanisms: carbon steel corrodes to red oxide within months; cast iron undergoes graphitic corrosion that destroys strength while maintaining appearance
  • Aluminium suffers galvanic attack in contact with stainless steel and dissolves chemically in the alkaline pH required to protect stainless steel , the same water chemistry protects one and attacks the other
  • NBR seals swell and hydrolyze; PU seals degrade hydrolytically and extrude catastrophically; standard hydraulic hose generates contamination particles as the NBR inner tube degrades
  • There is no partial solution , a single non-compatible component contaminates the entire system and accelerates failure of every precision component within it
  • A correctly engineered water hydraulic system requires a clean-sheet material specification throughout: 316L stainless, CF30-PEEK seals, PTFE-lined hose, and hardened or ceramic-coated sliding surfaces
