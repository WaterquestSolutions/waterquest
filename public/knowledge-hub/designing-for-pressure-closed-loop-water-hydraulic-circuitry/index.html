Designing for Pressure: Closed-Loop Water Hydraulic Circuitry

Follow the water through supply, load and return.

A recirculating water hydraulic circuit can deliver controlled power while managing its working water. Learn how pressure supply, return flow, filtration and protection fit together, and how to describe the circuit clearly.

Article 107Specification & Design10 min readUpdated 19 October 2026

Follow the return water as carefully as the supply

Collecting and recirculating working water can support a practical hydraulic installation. Start by drawing where the water goes after each movement. A reservoir-based recirculating circuit differs from a closed hydraulic loop that returns actuator flow directly towards the pump. Clear terminology helps you specify the right make-up supply, filtration and volume management for the chosen arrangement.

The distinction affects the questions an engineer must answer. Flow balance, replenishment, cooling and pressure protection depend on the actual paths. A label cannot establish whether the proposed pump arrangement is suitable or whether the actuator will behave as intended. Begin with a functional schematic showing where water goes in each operating state.

This article explains design questions rather than providing a construction circuit. ISO 4413 supplies a general hydraulic-system safety framework, while the selected equipment documents and application assessment establish the details. [1] Water hydraulics can use different architectures, but each must account for the fluid volumes, energy and component conditions that its operation creates.

Follow the water through each state

A circuit should be understood as a sequence of states rather than a static picture. During extension, retraction, holding and shutdown, different paths may be available or blocked. A line that serves as a supply in one state may carry return flow in another. Identify those changes explicitly.

For each state, ask where the pump receives water, where displaced actuator water goes and which components control the load. Include any replenishment, drain or auxiliary paths required by the selected equipment. Leaving a small supporting connection off the conceptual description can conceal an important operating requirement.

A state table is often a useful companion to the schematic. It can identify the commanded function, expected flow direction, relevant pressure regions and required indication. This helps mechanical, electrical and controls participants discuss the same behaviour. It also provides a practical basis for later commissioning checks without pretending that a diagram alone proves the installed sequence.

Account for unequal cylinder volumes

A single-rod cylinder has different effective areas on its two sides. Consequently, a given piston movement displaces different volumes at the cap and rod ends. A circuit that simply assumes equal outgoing and returning flow can therefore be incomplete, even if its drawing looks symmetrical.

Consider a hypothetical cylinder with a cap-end area of 20 square centimetres and an annular rod-side area of 12 square centimetres. Over a 100 millimetre stroke, the associated volumes are 0.20 litre (0.0528 US gal) and 0.12 litre (0.0317 US gal). The difference is 0.08 litre (0.0211 US gal). These teaching dimensions illustrate a geometric requirement, not a product specification.

The architecture must accommodate that difference through an appropriate supported arrangement. It must also address leakage and any other flows that cross the main loop boundary. The solution depends on the chosen pump and actuator system; it should not be improvised by assuming that a sealed volume can always supply the missing water or accept the excess without consequence.

Use pressure difference to understand actuator force

Actuator force depends on pressures acting over the relevant effective areas. For a single-rod cylinder, the opposing pressures act on unequal areas, so both sides must be included. A pump-outlet gauge alone does not establish the force available at the load. Line losses and the pressure on the opposite side matter.

The underlying hydraulic principle is pressure acting over area. [2] The real mechanism also includes friction, inertia and linkage effects. Keep those contributions separate in the calculation so that the designer can see which assumptions govern the result. A high indicated supply pressure does not automatically establish adequate useful force at every mechanism position.

This becomes especially important where flow paths change direction or the load assists motion. The pressure pattern can differ from the most familiar pump-driving-load case. The responsible designer should evaluate the relevant operating and abnormal conditions rather than extending one steady-state force calculation across every state of the machine.

Distinguish pressure generation from pressure limitation

A positive-displacement supply creates flow into a resisting system, and pressure responds to the load and circuit conditions. Protection functions limit pressures within their intended scope. Control functions regulate motion or process behaviour. Although one component may contribute to more than one purpose, those purposes should remain explicit in the design assessment.

The main pressure-limiting device may not protect every isolated section. Closed valves can separate part of the circuit from the normal supply protection path. External loading or temperature changes may then alter pressure in that trapped region. The review should identify such regions and determine the appropriate protection for the actual configuration.

Avoid using a nominal component pressure rating as the complete protection strategy. The assembly must remain within the relevant limits during the conditions it can encounter. That requires understanding connections and operating sequences as well as selecting individually rated components. A list of suitable parts is the beginning of circuit engineering, not its conclusion.

Preserve the pump's required inlet and drain conditions

The pump depends on the conditions at its actual connections. An architecture that appears attractive for recirculation may be unsuitable if it does not maintain the required inlet, casing or drain conditions for the selected model. These are equipment-specific requirements and should be confirmed from current documentation.

The Water Hydraulics Company's pump information distinguishes configurations and supporting connection requirements. [3] That is a reminder to match the circuit to the ordered build rather than treating every pump in a product family as interchangeable. A different port arrangement can represent a meaningful operating difference.

Review the least favourable conditions, including changing water level, filter loading and the expected temperature range where relevant. A favourable static supply reading does not necessarily describe the condition during movement. Instrument locations and acceptance checks should therefore relate to the actual requirement being verified, allowing the team to distinguish adequate supply from an assumption based on a remote measurement.

Treat leakage and heat as flows to be managed

Even a recirculating arrangement is not an ideal sealed energy system. Internal leakage, external losses and intentional auxiliary paths can affect the water balance. Mechanical and hydraulic losses also produce heat. The architecture must manage those effects within the equipment's supported operating range.

Describe where replenishment comes from and how an abnormal increase would be recognized. Make-up demand can provide useful operating information, but it must be interpreted in context. Planned discharge, service work or a changed operating schedule may affect it. A raw total without an operating explanation cannot identify the cause.

Likewise, cooling should be based on the expected loss and heat-rejection path, not on water's reputation as a good coolant. A fluid can transport heat while the overall installation still has no adequate place to reject it. The design should show how the proposed duty reaches a stable, acceptable thermal condition and how that condition will be observed in service.

Examine transient behaviour and compliance

Steady calculations are necessary but do not describe every pressure event. Rapid changes in flow, valve operation or load movement can produce transient behaviour. Entrained gas and the flexibility of hoses and structures can also affect the response. The relevant time scale depends on the machine and its control requirements.

A slowly responding gauge may not capture a short local event. Where transients could govern component selection or machine behaviour, the responsible engineer should determine an appropriate analysis and measurement approach. Do not assume that a comfortable average pressure proves the absence of unacceptable peaks.

The design question is practical: how does the complete assembly respond when a command changes or the load changes? The answer may influence control sequencing, component selection and instrumentation. It should be established for the actual architecture rather than inferred from the working fluid alone. Water properties matter, but they interact with the physical installation.

Make service isolation a circuit function

Maintenance requires a known and controlled equipment state. The design should allow authorised personnel to understand which regions can retain pressure or be influenced by an external load. Removing electrical power from the pump does not, by itself, describe the condition of every hydraulic volume or attached mechanism.

Provide the information and provisions required by the site's approved service procedures and applicable equipment guidance. Labels, drawings and accessible identification points help prevent confusion between similar lines or valves. The specific isolation and verification method must be developed for the installation rather than taken from a generic educational article.

Consider service access while arranging the equipment. A necessary inspection point that cannot be reached without disturbing another pressure boundary creates avoidable complexity. The owner benefits when maintainability is designed into the circuit and layout together, allowing the intended configuration to be restored and checked after intervention.

Validate the architecture through observable functions

An acceptance plan should connect each required function to an observation. Movement can be checked against travel and timing requirements; holding can be assessed under defined conditions; supply and thermal behaviour can be measured at relevant locations. Control indications should confirm the state the operator needs to know, not merely that an electrical command was issued.

Retain the configuration used for those checks. Changes to valves, hoses, settings or software can alter behaviour even if the principal pump remains the same. An accurate record helps the owner interpret later performance and gives a future integrator a dependable starting point for modifications.

WaterQuest can help develop a supported water-compatible scope within this engineering process. The benefit of recirculation is meaningful only when the proposed circuit also manages its volume balance, pressure regions and operating conditions. The persuasive argument is a design whose behaviour can be explained and verified, rather than an efficient-sounding label applied to a drawing.

For procurement, ask the supplier to identify the interfaces that belong to others. A complete responsibility schedule can distinguish pump supply, actuator selection, protective functions and controls integration. This avoids a gap in which each participant assumes another has resolved a critical circuit question, and makes the final acceptance evidence easier for the owner to review.

From understanding to equipment selection

Define what closed loop means in the schematic

Identify whether the term describes a recirculating fluid path, feedback control or both. Draw replenishment, cooling, pressure protection and return routes explicitly so each operating state has an understood pressure balance.

Calculate the relevant duty

Use the hydraulic power calculator to translate pressure and flow into hydraulic output and estimated electrical input. Use the system pressure-drop calculator to combine component losses, elevation and a design allowance.

Connect the calculation to equipment

Compare the power-unit arrangement with the actuator duty and control sequence. Send the load, motion and water specification together so the components can be assessed as one circuit.

Explore water hydraulic power units and water hydraulic cylinders.

Which operating measurements make future troubleshooting easier?

Monitor water condition and temperature at consistent locations. Include losses during holding and recirculation in the annual energy estimate, not only the useful actuator stroke.

Use the calculated duty as an enquiry input; efficiency assumptions are not verified equipment ratings.
