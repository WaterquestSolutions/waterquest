The customer benefit

Build the water supply and the actuation around the plant’s needs.

A changing production rate puts demands on purified-water delivery, while utility valves need reliable movement throughout startup, operation and shutdown. Water can serve as both a process feed and a hydraulic working medium, with each circuit designed for its own duty. Purpose-designed pumping protects the specified fluid path from pump-lubricating oil; water hydraulic actuators remove oil from the selected actuation loop. Keep those benefits connected to clearly defined equipment interfaces.

How water does the work

See how water does the work.

In electrolysis, electricity splits water into hydrogen and oxygen. The feed pump delivers water at the quality, pressure and flow required by the electrolyser. In a separate hydraulic duty, pressurised water acts on an actuator piston to move a valve or mechanism. The hydraulic loop need not share the electrolyser’s purified-water supply; its materials and water quality are specified independently.

01

Reaction water is only one part of site demand.

The chemical reaction requires approximately 9 kg (19.8 lb) of water per kilogram of hydrogen on a stoichiometric basis. Actual raw-water demand includes purification losses and any cooling, flushing or other utility needs. Keep those streams separate in the water balance and check which can realistically be recovered.

02

Purity must be maintained after treatment.

Water can pick up contaminants from tanks, pipework, seals or stagnant branches after leaving the purification plant. Select wetted materials, instrumentation and circulation arrangements for the electrolyser’s water specification. Conductivity can be useful, but it does not measure every possible contaminant.

An illustrative calculation

Separate theoretical demand from plant demand

Producing an illustrative 100 kg (220 lb) of hydrogen per day consumes about 900 kg (1,984 lb) of reaction water, approximately 0.9 m³ (31.8 ft³). At an assumed 75% purification recovery, that alone requires about 1.2 m³/day (317 US gal/day) of raw water.

Raw water for reaction = reaction-water demand ÷ purification recovery

Take the calculation into equipment selection.

Cooling, flushing and other uses are additional. The assumed recovery is illustrative; use the actual treatment and electrolyser specifications.

Build the commercial case

Connect the equipment choice to the operating result.

Separate the value of dependable process-water delivery from the value of removing oil from an actuator circuit. Compare each package at its own duty, including quality control, availability, maintenance and the work needed to integrate it into the plant.

  1. Define the improvement. Record the present duty, service effort and operating problem. Agree the movement, output or availability the new equipment must deliver.
  2. Specify and demonstrate the package. Match components to the actual water, load and operating range. Use agreed commissioning checks or a focused trial to confirm the result.
  3. Compare the complete ownership cost. Include equipment, installation, utilities, fluid management, servicing and downtime. Use measured results where available and identify estimates clearly.

Questions customers ask

Make an informed equipment choice.

What is the benefit of water-lubricated pumping?

A purpose-designed water-lubricated pump uses the pumped water to lubricate its internal working surfaces, keeping oil lubrication out of that pumping circuit. The right model depends on water chemistry, flow, pressure and inlet conditions. WaterQuest helps match those requirements to the equipment.

Can ordinary tap water feed the stack?

The electrolyser manufacturer defines acceptable water quality. Many installations need purification and controlled handling; do not assume the incoming supply is suitable.

Does removing hydraulic oil remove ignition risk?

No. Electrical, mechanical and process ignition sources remain. The hydrogen-system hazard assessment and area classification still govern the equipment.
