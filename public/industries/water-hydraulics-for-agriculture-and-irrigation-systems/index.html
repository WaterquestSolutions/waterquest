Agriculture And Irrigation

The water that grows your crops shouldn't have to share its environment with leaking hydraulic oil.

Irrigation gates, agricultural machinery and remote water-control equipment need dependable movement, often with limited maintenance access. Explore where water hydraulic actuation can remove petroleum-based fluid from the working circuit while delivering the force needed to manage equipment and water flows.

The customer benefit

Control the flow without placing hydraulic oil beside it.

A gate that sticks or a valve that closes poorly makes water distribution harder to manage. Water hydraulic actuation gives the mechanism controlled force and repeatable movement while removing oil from that circuit. Design the actuator supply around the available pressure, seasonal water quality and operating schedule. A separate conditioned loop is useful where irrigation water does not meet the hydraulic components’ requirements.

How water does the work

See how water does the work.

Water pressure acting on a piston creates the force to move a gate or valve; controlled flow sets its speed. Sensors and controls can connect that movement to level or irrigation schedules. Where supply pressure is insufficient or water quality varies, a dedicated water hydraulic power unit provides the actuator with its own controlled operating conditions.

01

The crop and the components see different water requirements.

Salinity and water chemistry affect irrigation suitability, while particles and deposits can affect emitters and hydraulic components. An acceptable crop-water source is not automatically suitable for a precision actuator circuit. Specify filtration, flushing and any segregation around the actual equipment.

02

Zoning controls simultaneous demand.

Opening more zones increases flow demand and can reduce available pressure at distant outlets. Valve sequencing should respect pump capacity, pipe losses and crop requirements. Confirm the minimum pressure at the end of the distribution run, rather than judging performance from the pump gauge.

Practical applications

Water-powered control from the canal to the greenhouse.

Use these examples to connect an operating problem with a possible water-based arrangement. Bring the closest match to your enquiry, along with the site conditions that will shape the design.

01

Greenhouse zone manifold

The customer’s needSeveral crop areas have different watering schedules and flow requirements.

The water-based approachUse suitably controlled valves and verify total simultaneous demand. Where water hydraulic actuation is appropriate, condition its supply and retain local service isolation without interrupting the whole greenhouse.

Confirm the resultMeasure flow and far-end pressure for every permitted zone combination. Check closure leakage and alarm response to an unexpected flow.

02

Irrigation canal gate

The customer’s needA canal operator needs repeatable level control despite seasonal debris and variable head.

The water-based approachUse an actuator selected for loaded breakaway and an accessible control arrangement. Define the response to lost telemetry, freezing and a blocked gate with the water manager.

Confirm the resultTest motion at the agreed head and trend level stability. Prove local recovery and inspect the gate after the first operating season.

03

Protected-crop vent mechanism

The customer’s needA greenhouse vent or shade mechanism operates near crops and washdown areas.

The water-based approachDesign a dedicated water hydraulic loop against the mechanism’s force, wind loading and hold requirements. Provide a safe manual recovery method and protect equipment from freezing.

Confirm the resultDemonstrate positioning, drift and fault response across expected loads. Confirm that leaks cannot contaminate the crop or create slippery access routes.

An illustrative calculation

Check the whole zone schedule

Three illustrative irrigation zones each requiring 2 m³/h (8.81 US gpm) demand 6 m³/h (26.4 US gpm) if operated together. Sequencing one at a time reduces instantaneous demand to 2 m³/h (8.81 US gpm), but triples the time needed to deliver the same volume.

Delivered volume = zone flow × run time

Take the calculation into equipment selection.

This is a scheduling trade-off, not an automatic water saving. Confirm that the longer schedule suits the crop and that pressure remains adequate.

Build the commercial case

Make the oil-free option a practical investment.

Start with a gate or valve whose reliability affects water distribution or operator time. Compare positioning, leakage at closure, inspection, seasonal servicing and the cost of managing hydraulic oil near crops or channels.

  1. Define the improvement. Record the present duty, service effort and operating problem. Agree the movement, output or availability the new equipment must deliver.
  2. Specify and demonstrate the package. Match components to the actual water, load and operating range. Use agreed commissioning checks or a focused trial to confirm the result.
  3. Compare the complete ownership cost. Include equipment, installation, utilities, fluid management, servicing and downtime. Use measured results where available and identify estimates clearly.

Questions customers ask

Make an informed equipment choice.

Can water hydraulics provide the force this equipment needs?

Yes, when the equipment is sized for the duty. Cylinder force comes from pressure acting over the effective piston area; controlled flow sets movement speed. WaterQuest helps match the cylinder, pump and valves to the load, stroke and operating sequence, using components designed for water.

Can irrigation pressure operate the valve actuator?

Possibly, if the actuator is designed for the available pressure and water quality at the worst condition. A separate power supply may be needed.

Can the system be left full over winter?

Only if the design provides a validated freeze-protection strategy. Draining must include low points and trapped cavities, with a documented restart procedure.
