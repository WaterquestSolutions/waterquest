Wastewater Treatment

Your treatment plant works to remove contaminants. Why introduce another one?

From channel gates to valve actuation and pumping equipment, treatment plants depend on mechanical systems operating in wet, corrosive and difficult-to-access locations. Hydraulic oil adds another contamination and maintenance concern. Explore where water-powered equipment can remove that dependency while meeting the required operating duty.

The customer benefit

Remove an oil source while improving access and control.

A slow gate, a difficult valve or an inaccessible actuator can delay isolation and disrupt treatment. A water hydraulic package addresses the movement as a complete system while removing hydraulic oil from the selected circuit. Plan position feedback, service isolation and accessible components around the way operators run the plant. Keep the clean working-water loop separate from wastewater so process contamination does not enter the hydraulic equipment.

How water does the work

See how water does the work.

A pump supplies pressurised water to an actuator through control valves. Pressure creates the force or torque to move the gate or valve, while regulated flow sets the operating time. Position signals confirm progress to the plant controls. The operating sequence also accounts for upstream levels, parallel treatment capacity and the conditions required before maintenance.

01

Breakaway is often harder than normal movement.

A gate left in one position can accumulate deposits or experience increased seal friction. Differential head, debris, guide alignment and stem condition affect the actual force. Establish loaded breakaway and seating requirements with the gate supplier and avoid using excessive actuator force to overcome a mechanical fault.

02

Isolation must be proven, not inferred from a command.

A closed command does not prove a gate is seated or a channel is safe to enter. Position sensing, leakage acceptance, upstream isolation and hazardous-energy procedures are distinct parts of the operating plan. Keep maintenance restraint and verification accessible to the people doing the work.

Practical applications

Water hydraulic actuation through the treatment process.

Use these examples to connect an operating problem with a possible water-based arrangement. Bring the closest match to your enquiry, along with the site conditions that will shape the design.

01

Inlet-channel gate

The customer’s needOne screening channel needs isolation while a parallel channel remains in service.

The water-based approachUse a cylinder selected for the loaded gate duty, with position indication and a plant-approved closure sequence. Include the effect on upstream level and available bypass capacity.

Confirm the resultProve the sequence at representative flow and head, measure stroke time and check the agreed isolation leakage criterion.

02

Sludge-service valve

The customer’s needA difficult valve needs reliable movement in a corrosive, dirty service area.

The water-based approachObtain the valve maker’s torque envelope for the actual process. Protect the actuator’s clean circuit, provide safe access and define a response to rising actuation pressure or incomplete travel.

Confirm the resultTest representative operating conditions and alarm handling. Confirm that the valve can be isolated and serviced without exposing staff to an energised mechanism.

03

Filter backwash mechanism

The customer’s needA treatment train depends on several valves reaching the correct state in sequence.

The water-based approachUse confirmed-position permissives and monitor pressure or travel time so a failed movement does not silently advance the backwash cycle. Assess whether a shared supply is an unacceptable common failure point.

Confirm the resultRun complete normal and interrupted sequences, including a simulated valve fault. Verify the treatment train returns to an agreed recoverable state.

An illustrative calculation

Volume determines the stroke demand

An illustrative 80 mm (3.15 in) bore cylinder extending 600 mm (23.6 in) displaces about 3.02 L (0.798 US gal). At 9 L/min (2.38 US gpm), the ideal extension takes about 20 seconds.

Stroke volume = piston area × stroke · Time = volume ÷ flow

Take the calculation into equipment selection.

Allow for leakage, flow sharing and the required motion profile. Retraction uses the annular area and must be checked separately.

Build the commercial case

Make the oil-free option a practical investment.

Start with a gate or valve whose reliability or access affects plant operation. Compare stroke performance, inspection, service isolation, fluid handling and planned downtime across the same operating sequence.

  1. Define the improvement. Record the present duty, service effort and operating problem. Agree the movement, output or availability the new equipment must deliver.
  2. Specify and demonstrate the package. Match components to the actual water, load and operating range. Use agreed commissioning checks or a focused trial to confirm the result.
  3. Compare the complete ownership cost. Include equipment, installation, utilities, fluid management, servicing and downtime. Use measured results where available and identify estimates clearly.

Questions customers ask

Make an informed equipment choice.

Can water hydraulics provide the force this equipment needs?

Yes, when the equipment is sized for the duty. Cylinder force comes from pressure acting over the effective piston area; controlled flow sets movement speed. WaterQuest helps match the cylinder, pump and valves to the load, stroke and operating sequence, using components designed for water.

Can wastewater be the hydraulic fluid?

Do not assume so. Solids, chemistry and biological activity can be incompatible with pumps, valves and seals. Use an agreed fluid specification.

Does a closed gate make the channel safe for entry?

No. The operator must prove isolation and manage upstream flow, stored energy and confined-space hazards through the approved work procedure.
