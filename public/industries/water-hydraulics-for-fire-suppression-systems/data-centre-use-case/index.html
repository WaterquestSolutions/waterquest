A local incident. A consequential response.

Use water’s cooling capacity where it can make the difference.

Fine droplets provide a large surface area for heat transfer. As water absorbs heat and evaporates near the fire, it cools the combustion region; the mist can also reduce radiant heat transfer to nearby equipment. The purpose is to limit fire growth and collateral damage using the discharge required by the tested design. See the manufacturer’s explanation of water-mist cooling.

Direct the response to the protected area

One supply. Defined zones. A controlled release sequence.

A zone is a defined protected area served through a section-valve arrangement. Its boundary follows the approved fire-protection design: compartment layout, hazard, airflow, obstructions and nozzle coverage. It is not simply an arbitrary number of racks.

Concept only. The design must allow the required simultaneous demand; more than one zone can need protection.

  1. Detect and identify. Fire detection identifies the affected area and signals the control system.
  2. Apply the interlocks. In a double-interlock pre-action arrangement, confirmed detection and a nozzle-operation condition are both required before water enters the protected piping.
  3. Discharge through activated nozzles. In a closed-nozzle system, heat opens individual nozzles. Opening a section valve does not mean every nozzle in the zone sprays. Open-nozzle deluge systems behave differently.
  4. Monitor and manage recovery. The approved sequence governs alarms, supply operation and shutdown or reset by authorised personnel. A cleared detector signal alone is not a reason to stop cooling.

Release logic varies by system and approval. Marioff’s documented data-centre example explains double-interlock operation. Conventional sprinklers can also activate locally; zoning alone does not establish a water saving.

Make the project comparison explicit.

Compare total discharge for the same protected hazard, required coverage and discharge duration, using each approved design’s operating conditions. Include every zone required to operate together.

Water saved = baseline volume − proposed volume
Saving (%) = water saved ÷ baseline volume × 100

Illustrative arithmetic: a 10,000 L (2,642 US gal) baseline and a 1,000 L (264 US gal) proposed discharge would save 9,000 L (2,378 US gal), or 90%. These are example volumes, not design demands. Actual quantities must come from the project’s hydraulic calculations and approved operating duration.

Protect assets. Reduce the recovery burden.

Protect the equipment around the fire—and the recovery plan.

Limit thermal exposure

Cooling can reduce the heat reaching neighbouring equipment and help limit fire growth. Nozzle placement and obstructions must match the tested arrangement.

Limit unnecessary wetting

Local nozzle activation and the selected pre-action controls can narrow the discharge footprint and reduce accidental release risk. The objective is effective protection with controlled water exposure.

Reduce water-handling demands

A lower verified discharge volume can reduce the water that must be stored, collected, extracted and dried after an event. Drainage and containment still need to suit the facility.

Plan for a faster recovery

A smaller affected area can make inspection, cleaning and restoration more manageable. The business case should quantify the recovery process and dependencies that actually govern lost service.

Minimum damage is the design objective, not a promise of zero damage or uninterrupted service. Smoke, corrosive residues, water and heat can still affect electronics. Emergency procedures, electrical isolation decisions, post-event inspection and authorised re-energisation remain essential. NFPA 75 addresses these fire-related exposures in information-technology areas.

The water-hydraulic connection

Water-lubricated pumping connects the supply to the suppression design.

A purpose-designed water-lubricated pump uses water as both the pumped medium and the lubricant for its internal working surfaces. That keeps pump-lubricating oil out of the suppression-water circuit. Separate water hydraulic actuators can serve suitable valve or mechanism duties. WaterQuest helps specify these components for integration into the fire-protection specialist’s approved package.

WaterQuest’s stated hydraulic design ceiling remains 160 bar (2,321 psi). The selected nozzle’s approved operating range and the calculated pressure losses determine the required duty. Use the lower applicable component limit, and obtain evidence for the complete pump, controls, section valves, piping, nozzles and water supply.

Specify performance, approval and service access together.

  • Approval or listing covering the actual hazard and installation conditions.
  • Nozzle coverage matched to rack layout, cable trays, airflow and obstructions.
  • Zone plan, release logic, alarm interfaces and required simultaneous operation.
  • Calculated discharge volume, duration, supply capacity and drainage provision.
  • Emergency power, commissioning tests, inspection and maintenance arrangements.
  • Separate assessment of battery rooms, UPS equipment and other distinct hazards.

Have the responsible fire-protection designer and authority having jurisdiction assess the complete application. A data-hall arrangement should not be assumed to cover a lithium-ion battery hazard or every other room in the facility.
