The customer benefit

Dependable movement without an oil-filled circuit above the water.

An oil leak at an outfall, canal or treatment intake can turn a routine equipment fault into a clean-up and service interruption. A purpose-designed water hydraulic system removes that hydraulic-oil inventory while retaining controlled force, speed and positioning. The opportunity is especially clear during a gate refurbishment or actuator replacement: improve the mechanism, make servicing easier and change the working fluid as part of one planned project.

How water does the work

See how water does the work.

A pump supplies water to a control valve, which directs it to the gate cylinder. Pressure acting on the piston creates lifting force; the flow rate sets the travel speed. Position feedback tells the controls where the gate is. A separate, conditioned working-water circuit allows the actuator to operate beside a waterway without drawing untreated channel water through precision components.

01

Start with the water load and the gate mechanism.

Deeper water and a larger gate create more load. The actuator must also overcome seal friction, gate weight, guide resistance and any debris. Linkages change the force needed through the movement. Calculate opening and closing separately, including the extra force needed to start a gate after a long idle period.

02

Plan how quickly the gate must move.

The amount of water flowing into the cylinder helps determine how quickly the gate moves. Pipe losses, return pressure and linkage geometry affect the actual travel time. The drainage or operating study sets the required speed: rapid closure can create a pressure surge, while slow closure may leave an unwanted flow path open.

Practical applications

Put water hydraulic power to work across your network.

Use these examples to connect an operating problem with a possible water-based arrangement. Bring the closest match to your enquiry, along with the site conditions that will shape the design.

01

Stormwater outfall isolation

The customer’s needKeep reverse flow out while preserving the drainage capacity upstream.

The water-based approachCombine water hydraulic gate actuation with water-level sensors and position feedback. Include a local way to recover control. Use the drainage study to decide what happens during a power failure, allowing for upstream storage and bypass capacity.

Confirm the resultTest the loaded gate at the design water-level difference. Record travel time and demonstrate the required response to lost power or a failed sensor, including how operators restore drainage.

02

Treatment-plant valve gallery

The customer’s needPosition a valve above a clean-water channel with reliable movement and walkway access for service.

The water-based approachUse a separate clean-water hydraulic supply and size the actuator from the valve manufacturer’s torque requirements. Provide local isolation and a position indicator that operators can read safely.

Confirm the resultMeasure the load across the stroke, confirm correct seating and demonstrate safe isolation for maintenance.

03

Canal level regulation

The customer’s needMaintain canal level through changing inflows with small, controlled gate movements.

The water-based approachAdjust gate position using water hydraulic flow control, position feedback and a level controller. Check for mechanical play, jerky starting movement and communication failures before enabling automatic operation.

Confirm the resultTrend canal level, gate position and movement frequency through representative demand changes. Confirm stable control without excessive cycling.

An illustrative calculation

From force to flow

For an illustrative 40 kN (8,992 lbf) extension force at 100 bar (1,450 psi), the ideal piston area is 0.004 m² (0.0431 ft²): about a 71 mm (2.8 in) bore. A 0.5 m (1.64 ft) stroke displaces 2 L (0.528 US gal); completing it in 20 seconds needs 6 L/min (1.59 US gpm) before leakage allowance.

F = Δp × A · Q = A × stroke ÷ time

Take the calculation into equipment selection.

Use this example to understand the relationship between force and flow. Final cylinder selection also includes rod-side pressure, friction, resistance to buckling, mountings and the required design margins.

Questions customers ask

Make an informed equipment choice.

Can water hydraulics provide the force this equipment needs?

Yes, when the equipment is sized for the duty. Cylinder force comes from pressure acting over the effective piston area; controlled flow sets movement speed. WaterQuest helps match the cylinder, pump and valves to the load, stroke and operating sequence, using components designed for water.

Can an existing oil cylinder simply use water?

Only with explicit manufacturer acceptance for the complete cylinder, valves, seals and operating conditions. A purpose-designed replacement may be required.

Should every gate close on a fault?

No. The safe position depends on flood routing, upstream storage, downstream exposure and process isolation. Record the decision for each gate.
