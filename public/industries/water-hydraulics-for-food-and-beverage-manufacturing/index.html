Food And Beverage Manufacturing

One hydraulic oil leak can put far more than a machine out of service.

When machinery operates near food or beverage production, contamination can mean interrupted processing, rejected product, cleanup and quality investigations. Water hydraulics offers a different approach to transmitting force, helping eliminate hydraulic oil from selected equipment circuits while leaving hygienic design and applicable compliance requirements firmly in focus.

The customer benefit

Address an avoidable oil source beside the production line.

When a machine must position, clamp or open a valve near product, the working fluid matters as well as the movement. A dedicated water hydraulic circuit removes the hydraulic-oil reservoir and oil-filled lines from that duty. Combine that benefit with cleanable geometry, suitable materials and accessible service points so the equipment supports your sanitation routine. The complete installation still follows the plant’s hygiene and food-safety requirements.

How water does the work

See how water does the work.

A water hydraulic pump supplies pressure to purpose-designed valves and actuators. Pressure establishes the available force; metered flow sets the movement speed. The working-water loop is kept separate from food and cleaning chemicals. Water-compatible seals, wear surfaces and corrosion-resistant components make the difference between a designed water system and an oil machine filled with water.

01

Working fluid and food-contact water are different specifications.

A recirculating hydraulic circuit can collect particles and support microbial growth if it is poorly managed. Do not assume its contents are suitable for direct food contact. Define segregation, backflow prevention, drainage and the response to a leak with the plant’s food-safety team.

02

Repeatable movement depends on mechanical control.

For a positioning cylinder, available pressure determines force and controlled flow determines speed. Conveyor timing, cushioning and sensor feedback govern whether the package is handled consistently. Avoid excessive clamping force that can damage packaging even when the actuator itself is operating correctly.

Practical applications

Controlled movement around your production process.

Use these examples to connect an operating problem with a possible water-based arrangement. Bring the closest match to your enquiry, along with the site conditions that will shape the design.

01

Packaged-product positioning

The customer’s needA bottling line needs a repeatable stop or guide movement beside closed product.

The water-based approachUse a cleanable water hydraulic actuator with protected sensing and hoses routed outside the product path. Set the force to the packaging duty and make wear points visible for inspection.

Confirm the resultMeasure position repeatability and cycle time; assess package damage, leak response and access during a sanitation walk-through.

02

Process-valve actuation

The customer’s needA process valve is exposed to frequent washdown and changing temperatures.

The water-based approachSelect the actuator for the valve’s torque and seating requirements. Check external seal and fitting compatibility with cleaning chemicals and physically separate the actuation water from the product circuit.

Confirm the resultVerify the full torque envelope, position feedback, safe loss-of-power state and the plant’s cleaning procedure.

03

Wash-area handling fixture

The customer’s needA wet handling station needs controlled movement with minimal exposed lubrication near product.

The water-based approachDesign the fixture with drainable surfaces, accessible guards and a serviceable cylinder. A mechanical restraint supports maintenance after hydraulic pressure is isolated.

Confirm the resultReview cleanability with operations and quality staff. Test motion after sanitation and verify that retained water cannot drip into the product zone.

An illustrative calculation

Force must match the package

An illustrative 25 mm (0.984 in) bore has a piston area of 491 mm² (0.761 in²). At a 20 bar (290 psi) pressure difference its ideal extension force is about 982 N (221 lbf). That may be excessive for a light package, even though it is a modest hydraulic duty.

Ideal force = pressure difference × piston area

Take the calculation into equipment selection.

Account for return pressure and friction. Set force limits from the handling task and validate them with representative packaging.

Build the commercial case

Make the oil-free option a practical investment.

Start with a station where oil exposure adds clean-up work or complicates the production environment. Compare the complete installation, including sanitation access, cycle performance, servicing and the response to a fluid leak.

  1. Define the improvement. Record the present duty, service effort and operating problem. Agree the movement, output or availability the new equipment must deliver.
  2. Specify and demonstrate the package. Match components to the actual water, load and operating range. Use agreed commissioning checks or a focused trial to confirm the result.
  3. Compare the complete ownership cost. Include equipment, installation, utilities, fluid management, servicing and downtime. Use measured results where available and identify estimates clearly.

Questions customers ask

Make an informed equipment choice.

Can water hydraulics provide the force this equipment needs?

Yes, when the equipment is sized for the duty. Cylinder force comes from pressure acting over the effective piston area; controlled flow sets movement speed. WaterQuest helps match the cylinder, pump and valves to the load, stroke and operating sequence, using components designed for water.

Does using water make a machine food-safe?

No. Food safety depends on materials, contamination prevention, water management, sanitation and operation of the complete equipment.

Can the plant wash-water supply feed the circuit?

Possibly, but only after confirming chemistry, temperature, inlet conditions and backflow protection. Cleaning chemicals must not enter components that are not rated for them.
