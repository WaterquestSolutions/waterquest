Dust Control Systems

You can see the dust. The real cost is often what you can't see.

Reduced visibility, cleaning, equipment contamination and exposure concerns can affect an entire operation. Effective dust control starts with delivering water at the right pressure and flow, through a system matched to the area and material being managed. The customer benefit

Turn water delivery into a controlled part of the process.

A pump and a cloud of mist are only useful when water reaches the dust source. Start with the material transfer, airflow and acceptable added moisture. Then connect the chosen spray pattern to a pump, filtration and zone controls that maintain the required nozzle conditions. This makes water delivery repeatable, gives operators useful pressure and flow information, and avoids spraying when the process does not need it.

How water does the work

See how water does the work.

The pump turns shaft power into water flow at pressure. A nozzle converts that pressure into a spray; its geometry and inlet pressure shape the pattern and droplet distribution. In a purpose-designed water-lubricated pump, water is both the pumped medium and the lubricant for its internal moving surfaces. Here the main duty is spray supply, rather than cylinder actuation.

01

Droplets must reach the target.

Nozzle design and pressure influence droplet size, velocity and pattern. Air movement can carry fine droplets away; very large droplets may pass through airborne dust without effective contact. Match the spray to the material, drop height and ventilation, and avoid adding enough moisture to create buildup or slippage.

02

Pressure at the nozzle is the useful pressure.

The pump outlet must cover nozzle demand, elevation and losses in pipework, filters and valves. Multiple zones can change the total required flow. Monitor flow as well as pressure: a blocked nozzle and an open broken line can each make a pressure-only picture misleading.

Practical applications

Put controlled spray where material releases dust.

Use these examples to connect an operating problem with a possible water-based arrangement. Bring the closest match to your enquiry, along with the site conditions that will shape the design.

01

Enclosed transfer point

The customer’s needA conveyor transfer releases dust into a predictable moving air stream.

The water-based approachCombine enclosure improvements with spray placement close to the source. Put serviceable nozzles outside the impact path and interlock the zone with material flow.

Confirm the resultCompare exposure and visible escape under matched throughput. Record added moisture, housekeeping effort and water use.

02

Truck-tipping hopper

The customer’s needDust escapes when a load falls into a receiving hopper.

The water-based approachUse the hopper geometry and prevailing airflow to place an event-controlled spray zone. Protect nozzles from vehicle impact and provide a suitable collection route for excess water.

Confirm the resultTest across representative load types and weather. Check driver visibility, slip conditions and dust at occupied locations.

03

Outdoor aggregate handling

The customer’s needVariable wind makes a stockpile transfer difficult to cover consistently.

The water-based approachPrioritise source wetting and controlled transfer geometry; select outdoor spray placement after reviewing wind and evaporation. Avoid relying on a drifting mist curtain as the only control.

Confirm the resultTrack operating conditions alongside dust measurements. Confirm that runoff and product moisture remain within the site’s requirements.

Questions customers ask

Make an informed equipment choice.

What is the benefit of water-lubricated pumping?

A purpose-designed water-lubricated pump uses the pumped water to lubricate its internal working surfaces, keeping oil lubrication out of that pumping circuit. The right model depends on water chemistry, flow, pressure and inlet conditions. WaterQuest helps match those requirements to the equipment.

Does finer mist always capture finer dust?

No. Contact depends on droplet momentum, relative velocity, airflow, residence time and particle properties. A nozzle trial needs measurements at the real duty.

Why measure both pressure and flow?

Together they make fouling, leakage and unexpected demand easier to diagnose. They also establish whether the operating point matches the nozzle selection.
