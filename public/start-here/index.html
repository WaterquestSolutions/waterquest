WaterQuest · Water hydraulics explained

What is water hydraulics?

Hydraulic systems use a liquid under pressure to move heavy things: lifting gates, pressing parts, powering tools. Most use oil. Water hydraulics does the same job with clean water.

A pump pressurizes the water, a valve directs it, and a cylinder turns that pressure into powerful, controlled motion. It works on the same principle as a car jack: a small push becomes a big force.

Why choose water?

  • Remove hydraulic oil from this leak path. A water circuit removes its hydraulic-oil inventory. A water leak still needs attention: pressure, flooding, water chemistry and the receiving environment matter.
  • A non-flammable working fluid. Water does not add a hydraulic-oil fuel load. Electrical equipment, surrounding materials and the application still need their own fire assessment.
  • Reduce one contamination source. Removing hydraulic oil can help sensitive processes. Water quality, hygienic design and slip or leakage hazards still need control.
  • Less oil handling. There is no hydraulic oil to buy, test, store or dispose of in the converted circuit. Compare energy, water treatment, service and installed cost for the complete duty.
  • Precise and powerful. Water’s stiffness can support responsive movement. Precision and temperature depend on the complete controls, components, duty and cooling arrangement.
  • Remove a defined oil-release source. Water hydraulics can remove hydraulic oil from equipment near waterways. Assess energy, water use and discharge conditions alongside that benefit.

Materials Engineering

Oil and water play by different rules. Good engineering respects the difference.

Oil and water can both transmit hydraulic power, but a system designed for oil cannot simply be expected to run reliably on water. Water demands a different approach to lubrication, wear and, especially, material compatibility.

The concern goes beyond ordinary rust. When dissimilar metals—such as carbon steel and stainless steel—are electrically connected and exposed to water containing dissolved salts or minerals, they can behave like a small battery. The water carries ions between them, allowing an electrochemical reaction that accelerates corrosion of the more active metal. This is galvanic corrosion. A small carbon steel fitting connected to a much larger stainless steel assembly can be particularly vulnerable, concentrating the damage in that single component.

That is why material selection must extend through the entire water circuit. A carefully engineered pump is only part of the solution: the valves, fittings and connections must also be compatible with the water and with one another. Our approach centres on 316 stainless steel and carbon-reinforced PEEK, with materials selected for their role and operating conditions.
